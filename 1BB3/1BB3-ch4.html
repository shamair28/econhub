Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Chapters 1–3 gave you tools that apply to any single market. Starting here, the course shifts to measuring and explaining the economy as a whole — the actual subject of macroeconomics. GDP is the single most-cited number in economic journalism: when the news says "the economy grew 1.7% last year" or "Canada is in a recession," they are talking about real GDP. Before you can analyze unemployment, inflation, growth, or any policy question in the rest of this course, you need to know precisely what GDP measures, what it doesn't, and how to calculate it.

This chapter also introduces a skill you'll use constantly for the rest of the term: converting between nominal figures (measured in current dollars) and real figures (adjusted for inflation). Every later chapter — unemployment and inflation (Chapter 5), growth (Chapter 7), the short-run models (Chapters 8–9), and every policy chapter after that — depends on the real/nominal distinction and the expenditure identity you'll learn here.

Chapter Roadmap This chapter covers: what GDP measures (and what it excludes), the expenditure approach (C+I+G+NX), why GDP equals total income, nominal vs. real GDP and the GDP deflator, GDP per capita, the limitations of GDP as a well-being measure, and how Statistics Canada actually compiles it. Term Test #2 tests Chapters 4–8 (30% of your grade, 40 multiple-choice questions, around week 9).
Section 2

What Is GDP? Defining the Measure

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders during a specific period of time (usually reported per year, though Statistics Canada releases quarterly figures). Every word in that definition is doing work:

  • Market value: GDP uses actual prices to add up unlike things — you can't literally add cars and haircuts, but you can add their dollar values.
  • Final goods and services: GDP counts a good only once, at its final sale. It excludes intermediate goods (inputs used up in producing something else) to avoid double-counting.
  • Produced: GDP counts new production, not the resale of existing goods. A used car sale doesn't add to this year's GDP (it was already counted when new) — though a dealer's service fee for arranging the sale does count, since that's a newly produced service.
  • Within a country's borders: GDP is a domestic concept. A Toyota plant in Cambridge, Ontario counts fully in Canadian GDP, even though Toyota is a foreign-owned company — production happened here. Conversely, profit a Canadian company earns from a factory in another country does NOT count in Canadian GDP (it would count in Gross National Product, GNP, a related but distinct measure).
  • During a specific time period: GDP is a flow (production per year), not a stock (a cumulative total, like national wealth).

Why final goods only? Because production happens in stages, and adding up every transaction along a supply chain would count the same value repeatedly. The alternative, equivalent way to compute this is the value-added method: sum only the value each firm adds at its own stage of production. The sum of value-added across every stage always equals the final good's market price.

Worked Example — Value-Added Through a Production Chain: A Wooden Table
A logging company sells logs to a sawmill for $50. The sawmill turns the logs into lumber and sells it to a furniture maker for $120. The furniture maker builds a table and sells it to a retailer for $300. The retailer sells the finished table to a consumer for $450. How much does this table contribute to GDP?
Value added at each stage:
  Logging company:    $50  − $0   (no purchased inputs) = $50
  Sawmill:             $120 − $50  (bought logs)          = $70
  Furniture maker:     $300 − $120 (bought lumber)        = $180
  Retailer:            $450 − $300 (bought table)         = $150
                                                    ─────────────
  Sum of value added:                                       $450

Compare to naively summing every transaction:
  $50 + $120 + $300 + $450 = $920 — more than DOUBLE the true
  contribution to GDP, because the log's value gets counted
  again inside the lumber's price, and the lumber's value gets
  counted again inside the table's price, and so on.

Correct answer: this table contributes $450 to GDP — exactly
its final sale price — NOT $920.
Common Mistake: Summing Every Transaction Students sometimes try to add up every dollar that changes hands in a supply chain. Don't. Either (a) count only the FINAL sale price to the end user, or (b) sum the value-added at each stage. Both give the identical, correct answer — summing raw transaction values does not.
Section 3

The Expenditure Approach: GDP = C + I + G + NX

The most common way to calculate GDP is to add up spending on final goods and services by each type of buyer in the economy:

GDP (Y) = C + I + G + NX

  • C — Consumption: household spending on goods and services (durables like cars, nondurables like groceries, and services like haircuts or streaming subscriptions). This is consistently the largest component of Canadian GDP.
  • I — Investment: business spending on capital — new machinery, equipment, and factories — plus new residential construction and the change in business inventories. This is not the everyday "investment" of buying stocks or bonds — buying a share of Shopify doesn't directly create new production, so it isn't counted here (that's financial saving, not the GDP-sense of investment).
  • G — Government purchases: government spending on goods and services (salaries of public employees, military equipment, infrastructure). This excludes transfer payments like Employment Insurance or Old Age Security, because the government receives no good or service in return for those — they just redistribute income that was already counted when originally earned.
  • NX — Net exports: exports minus imports. Exports are foreign spending on Canadian-made goods (add to GDP); imports are Canadian spending on foreign-made goods (subtracted, since they're already included in C, I, or G but weren't actually produced domestically).
Worked Example — Canada's Actual GDP Composition, 2025
Statistics Canada's 2025 expenditure-based GDP accounts show approximately: household consumption 58.5%, government consumption 22.1%, non-residential business investment 8.5%, residential investment 6.3%, intellectual property and inventory investment 2.5%, government investment 4.1%, and net exports −2.3% of GDP.1 Group these into the C+I+G+NX framework.
C (Consumption)            = 58.5%
I (Investment)             = 8.5% + 6.3% + 2.5%  = 17.3%
  (non-residential business + residential + IP/inventory)
G (Government)              = 22.1% + 4.1%        = 26.2%
  (government consumption + government investment)
NX (Net Exports)            = −2.3%

Check: 58.5 + 17.3 + 26.2 − 2.3 ≈ 100% (rounding) ✓

Interpretation: Consumption alone is nearly 60% of the entire
Canadian economy — this is why economists watch household
spending data so closely as a leading indicator, and why a
consumer pullback (as in a recession) hits GDP hard.
Net exports are negative, meaning Canada imports more than it
exports in dollar terms, even though it exports enormously in
absolute terms (oil, lumber, potash — recall Chapter 2).
Classifying Transactions: A Quick Test Ask three questions: (1) Who bought it — household, business, government, or a foreigner? (2) Was it a NEW good/service, or a transfer/resale? (3) Was it a final good, or an input into something else? A new laptop bought by a student is C. A delivery van bought by a courier company is I. A new fighter jet bought by the Department of National Defence is G. A shipment of Canadian wheat bought by a buyer in Japan is +NX (export).
Section 4

GDP Is Also Total Income

Recall the three-sector circular flow diagram from Chapter 2: every dollar of spending in the product market becomes someone's income in the factor market. This means GDP can be measured two equivalent ways: by adding up expenditure (Section 3) or by adding up income — wages, rent, interest, and profit earned by households and firms. In principle, these two totals must be identical, because one person's spending is always another person's income.

In practice, Statistics Canada calculates both the expenditure-based and income-based measures independently from different data sources, and reconciles small discrepancies between them (see Section 8). The income-based approach adds up compensation of employees (wages and benefits), corporate profits, net interest, rental income, and a few smaller adjustments including depreciation of capital.

Worked Example — One Transaction, Two Sides
A homeowner pays a contractor $30,000 to build a backyard deck, using entirely new lumber and labour. Show how this single transaction is captured on BOTH the expenditure side and the income side of GDP.
EXPENDITURE side:
  The $30,000 is household spending on a newly produced service
  (construction) → counted as C (or I, if classified as a home
  improvement/residential investment — construction spending on
  structures typically falls under residential investment, I).

INCOME side:
  The same $30,000 doesn't vanish — it becomes income for
  everyone involved in producing the deck:
    → Wages paid to the contractor's workers
    → Profit earned by the contractor's business
    → Payments to the lumber yard, which itself becomes wages
      and profit for sawmill and logging workers upstream

Key insight: The $30,000 appears ONCE in GDP (as expenditure),
but it simultaneously flows through the economy as income to
several different people. Expenditure-based and income-based
GDP are two different ways of measuring the SAME underlying
economic activity — not two separate quantities to be added
together.
Section 5

Nominal GDP, Real GDP, and the GDP Deflator

Nominal GDP values current production at current prices — it rises when either output rises OR prices rise, so it can overstate how much the economy is actually producing more of. Real GDP values current production at the prices of a fixed base year, isolating the pure change in output from the effect of price changes. Real GDP equals nominal GDP exactly in the base year.

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

The GDP deflator equals 100 in the base year by construction, and rises above 100 as the overall price level rises relative to the base year. The percentage change in the deflator from one year to the next is one measure of the economy-wide inflation rate.

Worked Example — Calculating Real GDP and the GDP Deflator
A simple economy produces only two goods. Base year = 2020.
  Product   2020 Qty   2020 Price   2024 Qty   2024 Price
  Apples      300        $1.00        350        $1.50
  Bagels      100        $0.50         90        $0.80

Step 1 — Nominal GDP 2020:
  (300 × $1.00) + (100 × $0.50) = $300 + $50 = $350

Step 2 — Nominal GDP 2024:
  (350 × $1.50) + (90 × $0.80) = $525 + $72 = $597

Step 3 — Real GDP 2024 (valued at 2020 base-year prices):
  (350 × $1.00) + (90 × $0.50) = $350 + $45 = $395

Step 4 — GDP Deflator for 2024:
  (597 / 395) × 100 = 151.1
  (Deflator for the base year 2020 = 100, by definition.)

Step 5 — Inflation rate, 2020→2024:
  (151.1 − 100) / 100 = 51.1% cumulative price growth
Common Mistake: Percentage Change vs. Point Difference When calculating an inflation rate from two deflator (or CPI) values, always divide by the OLD value. If the deflator rises from 126.8 to 128.5, the inflation rate is (128.5−126.8)/126.8 = 1.3% — NOT "1.7," which is just the raw point gap between the two numbers, not a percentage change.
Section 6

GDP Per Capita and Comparing Economies

Total GDP alone can be misleading when comparing countries of very different sizes, or the same country as its population changes. GDP per capita divides GDP by population, giving a rough (imperfect) measure of average output — and, by extension, average income — per person.

GDP per Capita = GDP / Population

GDP per capita is one of the standard proxies economists use for average standard of living, and it's central to the growth story you'll study in Chapters 6–7: a country can have a large total GDP simply by having a huge population, while still having low output — and low living standards — per person.

Worked Example — Total GDP vs. GDP Per Capita: Canada, the US, and China
As of 2025, China's total GDP is the second-largest in the world (behind only the United States), while Canada's total GDP ranks far lower. Yet GDP per capita tells a very different story. Approximate 2025 figures (current US$, nominal exchange rates): Canada ≈ $45,400 per person; United States ≈ $86,000–90,000 per person; China ≈ $13,000–13,500 per person.2 Explain the apparent contradiction.
Resolution: Total GDP and GDP per capita answer different
questions.

Total GDP answers: "How large is this economy overall?"
  → China: ~1.4 billion people producing a huge combined total
  → Canada: ~41 million people producing a much smaller combined total

GDP per capita answers: "How much does the average person
produce/earn?"
  → China's enormous population means its huge total GDP is
    divided among far more people, so the AVERAGE is much lower
  → Canada's much smaller population means its more modest
    total GDP is divided among far fewer people, so the AVERAGE
    is much higher

Lesson: A country can be an economic superpower by total GDP
while still having a much lower average standard of living than
a smaller, richer-per-person country. Never assume "bigger GDP"
means "richer population" without checking per-capita figures.
A Deeper Limitation: Exchange Rates vs. Purchasing Power The figures above convert every country's GDP into US dollars at current market exchange rates — but a dollar doesn't buy the same amount of goods in every country (a haircut costs far less in many countries than in Canada or the US). Economists often adjust for this using purchasing power parity (PPP), which can meaningfully change how countries rank. Comparisons using simple exchange-rate conversion, as used here, are a common first approximation but not the full picture.
Section 7

What GDP Leaves Out

GDP is an extremely useful summary statistic, but it was never designed to measure overall well-being — only market production. Several important things are systematically excluded:

  • Non-market production: unpaid household work (childcare, cooking, home repairs) and volunteer work are real, valuable production, but since no market transaction occurs, none of it counts in GDP.
  • The underground economy: cash-only work, unreported income, and illegal activity are real production too, but by definition go unmeasured.
  • Leisure time: an economy where everyone works 70-hour weeks could show higher GDP than one where people work 35-hour weeks and enjoy more leisure — even if the second society is arguably better off.
  • Environmental and social costs: GDP counts the market value of production but doesn't subtract the cost of pollution, resource depletion, or other externalities generated in the process.
  • Income distribution: GDP per capita is an average. An economy where income is extremely unequally distributed can have the same GDP per capita as one where income is spread evenly — the number alone can't tell you which.
Alternative Measures Because of these gaps, some researchers and even a few governments track alternative indices alongside GDP — for example, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness index, or various "Genuine Progress Indicators" that attempt to subtract environmental and social costs from GDP-like production measures. None has replaced GDP as the standard headline statistic, largely because GDP is comparatively easy to measure consistently and objectively, while well-being is much harder to quantify the same way across countries and time.
Section 8

How Statistics Canada Actually Measures It

Statistics Canada compiles GDP as part of the Canadian System of Macroeconomic Accounts, combining a wide range of data sources — business and household surveys, tax records, and trade data — from two independent angles: the expenditure-based approach (Section 3) and the income-based approach (Section 4). In theory these two approaches must produce identical totals; in practice they rely on different underlying data sources with different coverage and timing, so Statistics Canada carries out a detailed reconciliation process to align them.3

GDP is released quarterly, with each release reporting on the previous quarter and typically including revisions to prior quarters as more complete data becomes available — so the "first estimate" you see in the news is not necessarily the final word. Since 2001, Canada's official quarterly measure of real GDP growth has used a method called the Fisher chain index, chosen because it produces a more accurate measure of quarter-to-quarter growth than simpler fixed-base-year methods (like the apples-and-bagels example in Section 5, which uses a single fixed base year for simplicity).3

Why This Matters for the Exam You won't be tested on the Fisher index formula itself — but you should know that (a) GDP is released quarterly and gets revised, (b) it's measured two independent ways (expenditure and income) that are reconciled rather than simply matching by definition, and (c) "real GDP growth" reported in the news is the inflation-adjusted, chain-weighted measure — not simple nominal growth.

Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Value-Added

A wheat farmer sells wheat to a miller for $2,000. The miller grinds it into flour and sells it to a bakery for $3,500. The bakery bakes bread and sells it to consumers for $6,000. Calculate the value added at each stage, and confirm it equals the final sale price.

Show answer
Farmer: $2,000 − $0 = $2,000
Miller: $3,500 − $2,000 = $1,500
Bakery: $6,000 − $3,500 = $2,500
Sum of value added: $2,000 + $1,500 + $2,500 = $6,000 ✓ — matches the final sale price of bread. (Naively summing every transaction would give $2,000+$3,500+$6,000 = $11,500, nearly double the correct GDP contribution.)
Problem 2 — Classifying GDP Components

Classify each transaction into C, I, G, NX, or "not counted in GDP":
(a) A family buys a newly built condo.
(b) The federal government pays a border officer's salary.
(c) You buy a 2018 used Honda from a private seller.
(d) Canada exports canola oil to India.
(e) A retiree receives an Old Age Security payment.

Show answer
(a) I (residential investment — new construction)
(b) G (government purchase of a service)
(c) Not counted — already counted when the car was new; no new production occurred
(d) +NX (export)
(e) Not counted — a transfer payment, not a purchase of a good or service
Problem 3 — Nominal GDP, Real GDP, and the Deflator

Nominal GDP is $1,720.75 billion in Year 1 and $1,777.25 billion in Year 2. Real GDP is $1,356.87 billion in Year 1 and $1,383.14 billion in Year 2. Find the GDP deflator for each year and the inflation rate for Year 2.

Show answer
Deflator Year 1 = (1720.75/1356.87) × 100 = 126.8
Deflator Year 2 = (1777.25/1383.14) × 100 = 128.5
Inflation rate = (128.5 − 126.8) / 126.8 = 1.3%
(Common mistake to avoid: do NOT report "1.7" — that's the raw point gap between the deflator values, not a percentage change.)
Problem 4 — GDP Per Capita

Country X has a total GDP of $2.1 trillion and a population of 38 million. Country Y has a total GDP of $18 trillion and a population of 1.4 billion. (a) Calculate GDP per capita for each country. (b) Which country has the larger total economy? (c) Which country has the higher average standard of living, by this measure?

Show answer
(a) Country X: $2,100,000,000,000 / 38,000,000 = ~$55,300 per person
Country Y: $18,000,000,000,000 / 1,400,000,000 = ~$12,900 per person
(b) Country Y has the larger total GDP ($18T vs. $2.1T).
(c) Country X has the higher GDP per capita, and by this proxy, the higher average standard of living — despite having a much smaller total economy.
Problem 5 — Economics Application: What a Single GDP Report Hides

Suppose Statistics Canada reports that real GDP grew 2.0% last year, with consumption up 3%, government spending up 1%, business investment down 2%, and net exports roughly flat. Meanwhile, a labour economist notes that unpaid caregiving (mostly for aging parents) rose sharply over the same period as home-care wait lists grew, and a separate study finds that greenhouse gas emissions from the energy sector also rose. (a) Using the expenditure approach, explain what a "2.0% real GDP growth, driven mainly by consumption" report tells you about which C+I+G+NX components moved and by how much, relative to each other. (b) Using Section 7, explain why this same GDP report is silent on the rise in unpaid caregiving and the rise in emissions — and why that silence does not mean the report is "wrong." (c) Propose one alternative or supplementary statistic policymakers could look at alongside GDP to capture what this report leaves out.

Show answer
(a) Real GDP growth is a weighted sum of the growth in its components. Consumption growing fastest (3%) while being the largest share of GDP (recall Section 3: ~58.5%) explains why it's described as the main driver of the 2.0% overall figure — it contributes the most to the total even before accounting for government spending (+1%, a smaller ~26% share) and investment (−2%, a drag, on a smaller ~17% share), with net exports contributing roughly nothing. The overall 2.0% is a blend of these, weighted by each component's share of GDP.

(b) GDP measures only market production — goods and services bought and sold at a price. Unpaid caregiving has no market transaction, so by definition it falls outside GDP's scope (Section 7's "non-market production" gap), regardless of how much real economic value and effort it represents. Similarly, GDP counts the market value of energy production but does not subtract the cost of the emissions it generates (Section 7's "environmental costs" gap). The GDP report isn't "wrong" — it is accurately measuring what it was designed to measure (market production); the omission simply reflects the boundary of that definition, not a calculation error.

(c) Any reasonable answer that names a genuine supplementary measure and explains what it adds: e.g., time-use surveys that estimate the value of unpaid household/caregiving work, an environmentally adjusted ("green") GDP measure that nets out estimated pollution/resource-depletion costs, or a broader well-being index (life satisfaction surveys, health and education indicators) reported alongside GDP rather than replacing it.
