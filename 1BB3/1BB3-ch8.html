Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Chapter 7 was about the long run: what makes real GDP grow over decades. This chapter is about the short run: why real GDP jumps around its trend from year to year. The big change is one assumption. The price level is held constant. That lets us see how total spending, by itself, decides how much the economy produces.

Two real stories from the slides show why this matters. In slide 28, firms get optimistic about future AI profits and raise planned investment by $250 billion. In slide 27, US trade policy cuts Canadian exports. Each shock changes spending, and spending changes production, jobs and income.

Big Picture: The Spending Loop

Spending → production → income → spending.

  • Firms produce only what they expect to sell.
  • Producing pays out wages and profits. That is income.
  • Households spend part of that income. That is more spending.

A change anywhere in the loop gets passed around it. The multiplier (Section 8) measures how much.

Section 2

The Aggregate Expenditure Model

The aggregate expenditure (AE) model is a short-run model that explains how total spending determines real GDP, assuming the price level is constant. Because P is fixed, every change in spending is a change in real output, not in prices. (Chapter 9 relaxes this.)

Planned aggregate expenditure is total planned spending in the economy. It has four components, the same four as in Chapter 4:

AE = C + Ip + G + NX

  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Planned investment (Ip): firms’ spending on capital goods, plus household spending on new homes.
  • Government purchases (G): spending by governments on goods and services.
  • Net exports (NX): exports minus imports.

Here are the real numbers for Canada in 2024 (Table 8.2, real dollars, 2017 prices, Statistics Canada Table 36-10-0104-01):

Planned vs Actual Investment

Inventories are goods that firms have produced but not yet sold. A change in inventories counts as investment spending in GDP. But a firm only plans some of that change. The rest is a surprise.

Actual investment = Planned investment + Unplanned change in inventories

Example: a bookstore wants 5 copies of a book on the shelf. It expects to sell 95 and orders 95. If it sells only 90, it now holds 10 copies. The extra 5 are an unplanned rise in inventory. Actual investment is 5 higher than planned.

Vocabulary Trap — Planned vs Actual

Statistics Canada measures actual investment. GDP therefore includes any inventory build-up. The AE model uses planned investment, so AE leaves the unplanned part out. That is why AE and GDP are not the same thing. The slides assume the StatCan data are close enough to planned investment for our estimates.

Vocabulary Trap — Transfers Are Not in G

Government purchases cover goods and services the government actually receives: roads, RCMP salaries, hospitals. Transfer payments (pensions, welfare, EI) are excluded, because no good or service is received in return (slide 18). A bigger pension cheque raises household income, so it shows up in C, not in G.

Memory Aid — AE Has Four Parts: “C-I-G-N”

Say it as “Can I Get Net exports?” Consumption, Investment (planned), Government, Net exports.

Self-test: list the four parts, then name the one that is not counted when it is a transfer, and the one that GDP counts but AE does not.

Section 3

Macroeconomic Equilibrium and the Inventory Signal

Macroeconomic equilibrium happens when planned aggregate expenditure equals GDP:

C + Ip + G + NX = GDP

In a normal market, a price change clears a shortage or a surplus. Here the price level is fixed, so prices cannot do that job. Instead, unplanned changes in inventories act as the signal. They tell firms to change how much they produce.

If…then…and…
Planned AE = GDPInventories do not changeThe economy is in macroeconomic equilibrium
Planned AE < GDPInventories riseGDP and employment decrease
Planned AE > GDPInventories fallGDP and employment increase

Table 8.1. This is the firms’ “reaction function.” Slide 6 calls it the one to remember.

Think of a store manager. If shelves fill up with unsold goods, she orders less and may lay off workers. If shelves run empty, she orders more and hires. Add up every firm and you get the whole economy moving.

Memory Aid — Shelves Full → Slow Down. Shelves Empty → Speed Up.

Unplanned inventory change = GDP − AE.

  • Positive (shelves full): AE < GDP, so output falls.
  • Negative (shelves empty): AE > GDP, so output rises.
  • Zero: equilibrium.

Direction shortcut: output always moves toward AE. If AE is the bigger number, GDP heads up. If AE is the smaller number, GDP heads down.

Self-test: if Y = 900 and AE = 880, what is the unplanned inventory change, and what do firms do? (+20; they cut output.)

Common MC Trap — The Sign

“Inventories fall” sounds bad, but it means strong spending, and GDP goes up. “Inventories rise” sounds like growth, but it means weak spending, and GDP goes down. Read the sign as a report on sales, not on production.

Section 4

Consumption, the MPC and the MPS

Consumption is the largest part of AE. In 2024 it was 55.4% of GDP (slide 7). It follows a smooth upward trend, and it only stalls or dips in recessions. Investment is much jumpier (Section 5).

Five Determinants of Consumption

  • Current disposable income (income − taxes + transfers). Most important. Higher income, more spending.
  • Household wealth (assets − liabilities). More wealth means more spending at the same income. A stock-market boom works this way.
  • Expected future income. People smooth consumption over time. A student may spend freely, and even borrow, because higher pay is coming.
  • Price level. Higher prices cut the real value of wealth (your $2,000 chequing balance buys less), so spending falls.
  • Interest rate. A higher real rate rewards saving and makes loans dearer. Spending falls, most of all on durable goods like cars.
Memory Aid — “I WIPE”

Consumption is swept up and down by: Income (current disposable), Wealth, Interest rate, Price level, Expected future income.

Direction shortcut: Income, Wealth and Expected income push C up when they rise. Interest rate and Price level push C down when they rise. (Up, up, up; down, down.)

Self-test: cover this box and spell out I-W-I-P-E, then give the sign for each.

Apply the Concept — Do Changes in Consumer Confidence Affect Consumption Spending?

The Conference Board of Canada’s Consumer Confidence Index comes from four survey questions: how the family’s finances compare with six months ago, the outlook for the next six months, the job outlook, and whether it is a good time for big purchases. Research by Kwan and Cotsomitis finds the index helps predict Canadian household spending. That fits the “expected future income” determinant: if people expect less income, they spend less now.

The Consumption Function and the MPC

The consumption function is the relationship between consumption and disposable income. Plot Canadian data from 1981 to 2023 and the points sit close to a straight line (slide 9). Two things describe that line:

  • Autonomous consumption: consumption when disposable income is zero. This is the intercept. It is paid for out of past saving.
  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the amount consumption rises when disposable income rises by $1. It is the slope of the consumption function.

MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYD   so   ΔC = MPC × ΔYD

Worked Example A — Estimating the Canadian MPC (slide 10)
From 2016 to 2017, consumption rose $39.7 billion while disposable income rose $51.9 billion. Estimate the MPC. If disposable income rises by $10,000, how much does consumption rise?
MPC = ΔC / ΔYD = 39.7 / 51.9 = about 0.76   (slide: 0.764)

ΔC = MPC × ΔYD = 0.764 × 10,000 = $7,640

So of every extra dollar, about 76 cents is spent.

Consumption as a Function of Real GDP

The AE diagram puts real GDP (Y) on the horizontal axis, so we need C as a function of Y. Slides 11–13 make two simple assumptions:

  1. National income equals GDP. This is not exactly true. The gap between GNI and GDP in Canada is about 1% to 2% of GDP.
  2. Net taxes (taxes − transfers) are constant. Then disposable income = Y − net taxes, and a change in Y is a change in YD.

So the slope of C against Y is also the MPC. Using the slide’s numbers, with net taxes of 1,000:

Saving and the MPS

National income is used three ways: consumed, saved or paid in taxes. Start from Y = C + S + T. Look at changes:

ΔY = ΔC + ΔS + ΔT
Net taxes are constant, so ΔT = 0:   ΔY = ΔC + ΔS
Divide through by ΔY:                1 = ΔC/ΔY + ΔS/ΔY
                                     1 = MPC + MPS

The marginal propensity to save (MPS) is ΔS ÷ ΔY: the part of each extra dollar of income that is saved. Every extra dollar is either spent or saved, so MPC + MPS = 1.

    Of every extra dollar, about 24 cents is saved.

Memory Aid — Spend It or Save It

MPC is the slope of the C line (and, soon, of the AE line). MPC is always between 0 and 1. MPC + MPS = 1, so if you know one, you know the other.

Self-test: MPC = 0.8. What is the MPS? (0.2.) If income rises $500 and taxes are fixed, how much is saved? (100.)

Section 5

Investment, Government Purchases and Net Exports

Planned Investment

Real investment has grown over time, but it is much more volatile than consumption and falls sharply in recessions (slide 16). Three groups of determinants (slide 17):

  • Expected future profits. The most important. Optimistic firms invest more. Pessimistic firms cut back, as in recessions.
  • The real interest rate. It sets the cost of borrowing. A higher real rate lowers investment.
  • Taxes and cash flow. Higher corporate taxes reduce funds and incentives to invest. Tax incentives and strong cash flow (profits) raise investment.
Memory Aid — Investment Runs on “P-R-T”

Profit expectations, Real interest rate, Taxes and cash flow.

Direction shortcut: Profit expectations ↑ → I ↑. Real rate ↑ → I ↓. Taxes ↑ → I ↓.

Apply the Concept — Housing Prices Are Just Too High!

New homes are part of planned investment, and in 2024 new housing was roughly 40% of all investment. Home prices jumped about 60% for the typical single-family home between February 2020 and February 2022. Demand rose (more people, very low mortgage rates, more space wanted) while supply grew slowly. Housing swings matter for the whole economy. The decline in housing in 2008 helped cause that recession.

Government Purchases

G includes spending by federal, provincial and local governments on goods and services. It also includes government investment such as roads and buildings. Transfers are excluded. In real terms G mostly rose from 1981 to 2023, with a dip in the mid-1990s when governments cut spending to cut deficits (slide 18). Unlike C or I, G is set by policy decisions, not by the current level of GDP.

Net Exports

NX = exports − imports. Three determinants (slide 20):

  • Relative price levels. If Canadian prices rise faster than foreign prices, exports fall, imports rise, and NX falls.
  • The exchange rate. A stronger Canadian dollar makes imports cheaper and our exports dearer. NX falls. A weaker dollar does the opposite.
  • Relative growth rates. Faster Canadian GDP growth raises imports more than exports. NX falls.

The US takes about 70–80% of Canadian exports, so a US recession or a US tariff hits Canada’s NX directly.

Memory Aid — NX Runs on “P-E-G”

Prices (relative), Exchange rate, Growth (relative). Direction shortcut: if the thing is “higher at home,” NX goes down. Home prices higher, C$ stronger, home growth faster: all push NX down.

Common MC Trap — The Pension Question

“The government raises pension payments. What happens to G?” Nothing. Transfers are not government purchases. The effect arrives through disposable income and consumption, and it is smaller than a matching rise in G, because households save part of the cheque (MPS).

Section 6

The 45°-Line Diagram

The 45°-line diagram (also called the Keynesian cross) puts real GDP (Y) on the horizontal axis and planned AE on the vertical axis. The 45° line shows every point where AE = GDP. So every point on it is a possible equilibrium. Our job is to find the one the economy actually lands on.

To do that we draw the AE line: planned spending at each level of GDP. We assume Ip, G and NX are constant (autonomous). Only C changes with GDP. So:

Memory Aid — Slope of the AE Line = MPC

Only C moves with Y, and C moves by the MPC per dollar. So the AE line has slope = MPC. Because MPC < 1, the AE line is flatter than the 45° line. That is why the two lines cross exactly once.

Above the 45° line: AE > GDP. Below it: AE < GDP.

Building the AE Line (Figure 8.9)

Start with the consumption function. Stack Ip on top, then G, then NX. The result is the AE line. Where it crosses the 45° line, planned AE equals GDP. That is macroeconomic equilibrium.

Vocabulary Trap — A Negative NX Layer

In the slides’ numbers, NX is −$0.05 trillion. That layer pulls the AE line down, not up. Net exports are only positive when exports exceed imports. In Canada in 2024 they did not.

How the Economy Gets to Equilibrium (Figure 8.10)

  • Left of equilibrium (say Y = 2.0): AE is above Y. Spending beats production, so inventories run down. Firms raise output and hire. Income and consumption rise. The economy slides up the AE line toward 3.0.
  • Right of equilibrium (say Y = 4.0): AE is below Y. Inventories pile up. Firms cut output and lay off workers. GDP falls toward 3.0.

Section 7

Potential GDP and Recessions on the 45° Diagram

Potential GDP is the level of real GDP when firms operate at normal capacity with a normal workforce (slide 26). At potential GDP:

  • The economy is at full employment. Cyclical unemployment is zero. (Frictional and structural unemployment remain, so unemployment sits at the natural rate.)
  • Production is sustainable, not the maximum possible.
  • Potential GDP grows with the labour force, the capital stock and technology.

Equilibrium can be anywhere on the 45° line. It does not have to be at potential GDP.

Slide 27: A Trade Shock

In Figure 8.11, potential GDP is $3.2 trillion. Suppose the economy starts in equilibrium at 3.2. Then US policy cuts Canadian exports. NX falls. Planned AE is now too low for 3.2 to remain the equilibrium. The AE line shifts down. The new equilibrium is below potential GDP. Firms run below normal capacity, and unemployment is above the natural rate.

Notice the size of the shock. NX fell by only 0.1, yet equilibrium GDP fell by 0.2. That is the multiplier at work (Section 8). The multiplier here is 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2.

The gap is also easy to see on the graph. At Y = 3.2, AE2 is 1.5 + 0.5 × 3.2 = 3.1. That is 0.1 below the 45° line. It is the unplanned inventory build that would occur at potential GDP. If some part of AE rose by 0.1, the economy would return to potential.

The reverse case also exists. If AE is high enough that equilibrium is above potential GDP, firms are stretched past normal capacity and inflation pressure builds. Textbook terminology calls these an expansionary (inflationary) gap and a recessionary gap.

Memory Aid — “Below the Line Means Below Potential”

Equilibrium below potential GDP → unemployment above its natural rate (recession). Equilibrium above potential → unemployment below its natural rate (overheating). The unemployment rate and GDP move in opposite directions.

Self-test: in Figure 4, which curve is the economy on after the trade shock, and is it above or below potential?

Vocabulary Trap — Potential Is Not Maximum

The economy can temporarily produce more than potential (overtime, running machines hard). It cannot keep that up. “Full employment” does not mean zero unemployment. It means zero cyclical unemployment.

Section 8

The Multiplier Effect

Autonomous expenditure is spending that does not depend on the current level of GDP. In this model Ip, G and NX are autonomous, and so is the intercept of the consumption function. Consumption also has an induced part, the part that changes because income changed (a movement along the AE line).

The multiplier is the ratio of the change in equilibrium GDP to the change in autonomous expenditure. A $1 change in autonomous spending changes equilibrium GDP by more than $1. Why? The first round of spending becomes income, and some of that income is spent again.

The AI Investment Example

Firms are optimistic about future AI profits. Planned investment rises by $250 billion (slide 28). The AE line shifts up by $250 billion. The new equilibrium is $500 billion higher when MPC = 0.5.

Total ΔGDP = 250 + MPC × 250 + MPC² × 250 + MPC³ × 250 + …

Memory Aid — Multiplier = “1 Over the Leak”

Each round, the MPS share leaks out into saving and is not re-spent. The multiplier is 1 ÷ MPS: one over the leak. Smaller leak, bigger multiplier.

Direction shortcut: MPC up → multiplier up. MPC = 0.5 gives 2, 0.75 gives 4, 0.8 gives 5.

Self-test: MPC = 0.9. Multiplier? (10.) An autonomous cut of $40B? (GDP falls $400B.)

Common MC Trap — 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), Not 1 ÷ MPC

If MPC = 0.8, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. It is not 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25. Always subtract the MPC from 1 first (or just use 1 ÷ MPS).

Four Key Points About the Multiplier (Slide 32)

  1. It works both ways. With MPC = 0.5, a $250B drop in autonomous spending cuts GDP by $500B.
  2. It makes the economy more sensitive to shocks. A trade-war fall in net exports hits exporters first. Then it spreads, through lost income and spending, to firms that never sell abroad.
  3. A higher MPC gives a larger multiplier. When people spend more of each extra dollar, the ripples are stronger.
  4. The simple formula overstates reality. It ignores imports (leaks), inflation, interest rates and income taxes, which all dampen the effect.
Don’t Let This Happen to You — Protectionism Is Not a Free Multiplier

A tariff cuts imports, so NX rises and the multiplier lifts GDP. Tempting in a recession. But it only works if nobody retaliates. In a trade war every country’s exports fall too. After the Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930, world trade fell by about two-thirds and economies shrank further.

The Paradox of Thrift

In the long run, more saving funds investment and supports growth (Chapters 6 and 7). In the short run, if households all save more and spend less, AE falls. Firms sell less, cut output and lay off workers. Incomes fall. Total saving may not rise at all, and households end up no better off. Many households saving at once can start or deepen a recession.

Memory Aid — Saving Is a Virtue in the Long Run, a Drag in the Short Run

Long run: S → I → growth (Chapters 6 and 7). Short run: S ↑ means C ↓, so AE ↓, so GDP ↓. The word paradox comes from this flip: what is good for one household is bad when everyone does it.

Section 9

From Aggregate Expenditure to Aggregate Demand

So far the price level was fixed. In real life, when AE rises, both real GDP and P tend to rise. To handle that, we need the aggregate demand (AD) curve: the relationship between the price level (P) and the quantity of real GDP demanded by households, firms and the government.

The key idea (slides 34–37, Week 8 slides 10–13): each point on the AD curve is an equilibrium from the 45° diagram. So we can derive AD by asking: how does a change in P move the AE line?

Why a Higher P Shifts the AE Line Down

Wealth Effect (C)

A higher price level lowers the real value of household wealth. Households spend less, so C falls.

International-Trade Effect (NX)

If domestic prices rise faster than foreign prices, exports fall and imports rise. NX falls.

Interest-Rate Effect (Ip)

Higher prices raise the demand for money. With a fixed money supply, interest rates rise. Planned investment falls.

Bottom line (slide 35): a higher P lowers planned AE, so the AE line shifts down. A lower P raises planned AE, so the AE line shifts up.

Memory Aid — “W-T-I”: Wealth, Trade, Interest

Three channels, each hitting one component: Wealth → C, Trade → NX, Interest rate → Ip. G is not on the list. Government purchases do not respond to the price level in this model.

Self-test: name the component that each channel changes.

Deriving the AD Curve (Figure 8.13)

Start at price level P1 with equilibrium at 3.0. If P falls to P2, the AE line shifts up and equilibrium GDP rises to 3.5. Slide 37 leaves the case P1 to P3 (a higher price level) for you to finish. The AE line shifts down, so equilibrium GDP falls. In the figure, we use 2.5 as an illustration (the exact value depends on how much AE moves).

Plot each pair (Y, P) on a new graph with the price level on the vertical axis. Join the points. The line slopes downward. That is the AD curve.

THE BIG TRAP — A Change in P SHIFTS the AE Line but Is a MOVEMENT ALONG the AD Curve

This is the most tested idea in this section. Read it twice.

  • A change in the price level (P): shifts the AE line. But on the AD graph it is a movement along the AD curve. The AD curve does not shift.
  • A change in autonomous spending (Ip, G, NX, or autonomous C) at a given P: shifts the AE line and shifts the AD curve.

Why? The AD curve already contains the effect of P. P is on its vertical axis. Anything else that changes spending at every price level is what moves the whole curve.

Shortcut: P is on the axis, so P moves you along. Everything else moves the curve.

Vocabulary Trap — “AE Shifts” Does Not Mean “AD Shifts”

Both a price-level change and an autonomous-spending change shift the AE line. Only the second one shifts AD. When a question says “the AE line shifts down,” ask first: why? If the reason is a higher P, AD does not move.

The Algebra of Equilibrium (Appendix)

Graphs and tables show the direction of change. Equations give numbers. Governments, firms and universities use econometric models, equations whose parameters are estimated from real data, to forecast and to test policies (slide 38).

The Master Equation

Equilibrium GDP = Autonomous expenditure × Multiplier.

The AE function as a line: AE = (autonomous expenditure) + MPC × Y. The intercept is autonomous expenditure. The slope is the MPC.

Exam Method — 3 Steps
  1. Add the autonomous terms: the constant in C, plus Ip, G and NX (keep the signs).
  2. Find the multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  3. Multiply: Y = autonomous × multiplier.

For a change: ΔY = Δautonomous × multiplier.

Worked Example F — A Rise in G in the Algebra Model
In Worked Example E, the government raises G by 20. What is the new equilibrium GDP?
Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 5
ΔY = 20 × 5 = 100,  so new Y = 1,600 + 100 = 1,700

Check from scratch: autonomous = 100 + 125 + 145 − 30 = 340
  Y = 340 × 5 = 1,700 ✓   (or: 0.2Y = 340, Y = 1,700)
Common Mistake — Keep the Sign of NX

NX = −30 is part of autonomous spending, so it subtracts. Autonomous expenditure here is 100 + 125 + 125 − 30 = 320, not 380.

Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — The Inventory Signal

An economy has Y = 900, C = 650, Ip = 120, G = 150 and NX = −40 (billions). (a) Find planned AE. (b) Find the unplanned change in inventories. (c) Find actual investment. (d) What will firms do?

Show answer
(a) AE = 650 + 120 + 150 − 40 = 880.
(b) Unplanned inventory change = Y − AE = 900 − 880 = +20 (inventories rise).
(c) Actual investment = planned + unplanned = 120 + 20 = 140.
(d) AE < GDP and shelves are full, so firms cut output. GDP and employment fall until AE = GDP.
Problem 2 — MPC, MPS and the Multiplier

Disposable income rises by 2,000 and consumption rises by 1,500. (a) Find the MPC and MPS. (b) Find the multiplier. (c) Exports fall by $30B. What happens to equilibrium GDP?

Show answer
(a) MPC = 1,500 / 2,000 = 0.75. MPS = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25.
(b) Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 1 / 0.25 = 4.
(c) NX falls by 30, so ΔY = −30 × 4 = −$120B. GDP falls by $120 billion.
Problem 3 — Solve the Model and Close a Gap

C = 200 + 0.75Y, Ip = 300, G = 400, NX = −100 (billions). (a) Find autonomous expenditure and the multiplier. (b) Find equilibrium GDP. (c) Potential GDP is 3,400. How big is the gap, and how much must G rise to close it?

Show answer
(a) Autonomous = 200 + 300 + 400 − 100 = 800. Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 4.
(b) Y = 800 × 4 = 3,200. (Check: C = 200 + 2,400 = 2,600; AE = 2,600 + 300 + 400 − 100 = 3,200 ✓.)
(c) Gap = 3,400 − 3,200 = 200 (recessionary). Needed ΔG = 200 / 4 = 50. (Check: autonomous 850 × 4 = 3,400 ✓.)
Problem 4 — Shift or Move?

For each event, say what happens to the AE line and to the AD curve. Use: shifts up/down (AE), shifts right/left or moves along (AD).
(a) The price level rises.
(b) Firms become optimistic about AI profits.
(c) The Canadian dollar appreciates.
(d) Households save more at every income level (paradox of thrift).
(e) The government raises infrastructure spending.
(f) The price level falls.

Show answer
(a) AE shifts down; AD: movement along the curve (higher P, lower Y).
(b) Ip ↑: AE shifts up; AD shifts right.
(c) NX ↓: AE shifts down; AD shifts left. (The exchange rate is not the price level.)
(d) Autonomous C ↓: AE shifts down; AD shifts left. Short-run GDP falls.
(e) G ↑: AE shifts up; AD shifts right.
(f) AE shifts up; AD: movement along the curve (lower P, higher Y).

Common Exam Traps
  • Planned vs actual investment: GDP counts actual (with unplanned inventories). AE counts planned.
  • Transfers are not in G. A pension increase affects C through disposable income.
  • The AE line’s slope is the MPC, which is less than 1, so it is flatter than the 45° line.
  • A change in P shifts AE but moves along AD. A change in autonomous spending shifts AE and shifts AD.
  • The multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), equivalently 1 ÷ MPS. Not 1 ÷ MPC.
  • The paradox of thrift is a short-run result. In the long run, saving funds investment.
  • Sign of the inventory change: Y − AE > 0 means shelves full and output falls. Y − AE < 0 means output rises.
  • Keep NX negative when it is negative. It subtracts from AE.
