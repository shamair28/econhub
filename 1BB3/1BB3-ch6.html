Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Two facts about Canada’s real GDP shape this whole chapter. Over decades it trends up; that is growth. From one year to the next it bounces around that trend; those are business cycles. This chapter is the bridge between the long run (Chapter 7) and the short run (Chapter 8 onward).

Growth also needs funding. Firms build factories, data centres and machines with money that mostly comes from households’ saving. The financial system is the plumbing that moves saving to investment, and the market for loanable funds is the graph that shows the price of that money: the real interest rate.

Here is a real hook. In 2025–26 tech firms borrowed huge sums to build AI infrastructure. Meta alone sold US$30 billion of bonds on October 30, 2025, the year’s largest corporate bond sale, to fund data centres. What does that do to interest rates for everyone else? Section 9 answers it with one graph.

Heads-Up: What the Prof Moved

Per the slides, the textbook’s material on the determinants of long-run growth (pp. 155–156) is covered in Chapter 7 instead, and potential GDP and the output gap are covered in Chapter 8. Don’t expect them here.

Big Picture

This chapter answers three questions:

  • How fast is the economy growing?
  • Where are we in the cycle?
  • How does saving become investment, and what sets the real interest rate?
Section 2

Long-Run Growth: The Upward Trend

Economic growth is the ability of an economy to produce increasing quantities of goods and services.

The most common, albeit imperfect, measure of living standards is real GDP per capita. (Chapter 4 explained the imperfections: it ignores leisure, non-market work, the environment and the distribution of income.) To raise living standards, real GDP must grow faster than the population.

Shortcut

Growth of real GDP per capita ≈ growth of real GDP − population growth.

Example: real GDP grows 2.5% while population grows 3.0%, so per-capita growth is about −0.5%. The economy grew, but the average person got poorer. This matters whenever population is growing fast.

The overall trend is upward, but growth is not smooth. Look at the figure and you see growth and cycles at the same time.

Calculating Growth Rates

The growth rate is the percentage change from the previous year:

Growth rate = (this year − last year) ÷ last year × 100

Worked Example A — Growth Rates from the Slides
(a) Real GDP was $2,058 billion in 2023 and $2,125 billion in 2024. What was the growth rate? (b) Real GDP growth was 1.7% in 2019, −3.1% in 2020 and 3.3% in 2021. What was the average annual growth rate over the three years?
(a) (2,125 − 2,058) / 2,058 × 100 = 67 / 2,058 × 100 = 3.3%

(b) Average annual growth ≈ simple average of the yearly rates
    (1.7 − 3.1 + 3.3) / 3 = 1.9 / 3 = 0.6%   (0.63 before rounding)

Note: averaging the yearly rates is an approximation that works well
over a few years. Chapter 7 uses compounding for long horizons.
Vocabulary Trap — Real vs Nominal, Level vs Rate

Growth is measured with real GDP. As Chapter 4 showed, nominal GDP can rise from price increases alone.

Also, a falling growth rate is not a falling GDP. If growth slows from 3% to 1%, GDP is still rising, just more slowly. Only a negative growth rate means GDP fell.

What drives long-run growth (capital, technology and human capital) is the subject of Chapter 7.

Section 3

The Business Cycle

The overall trend in real GDP is up, but growth is not smooth or constant. Real GDP fell during the major recessions: the early 1980s, the early 1990s, 2008–2009 and 2020 (the pandemic). The causes of these short-run fluctuations vary. The slides list oil price shocks, monetary policy, financial crises and a global pandemic.

Business cycle theory explains short-run fluctuations around the trend. A cycle has four phases, in this order:

  1. Expansion. Real GDP is rising and employment is rising.
  2. Peak. The turning point where the expansion ends.
  3. Recession. Real GDP is falling: a period of declining output and employment.
  4. Trough. The turning point where the recession ends and the next expansion begins.

Panel (b) shows three things. Recessions are much shorter than expansions. The 2020 drop was the deepest but also the shortest. And real-world expansions have small wiggles that are not recessions.

Memory Aid — E-P-R-T: Every Peak Rolls to a Trough

Expansion → Peak → Recession → Trough → (repeat).

Points vs periods: peaks and troughs are points (turning points, a single month or quarter). Expansions and recessions are periods.

Common MC trap: “the peak is the highest growth rate.” No. The peak is the highest level of real GDP before it starts falling.

Self-test: cover the figure and label the four phases on a blank wave.

Vocabulary Trap — Points vs Periods

An answer saying “the economy is in a trough for two years” is wrong wording, because a trough is a turning point, not a period. The correct picture: the peak marks the end of an expansion, the recession is the period between the peak and the trough, and the trough marks the start of the next expansion.

Section 4

How Do We Know We’re in a Recession?

Statistics Canada produces the data (real GDP, employment) but does not officially declare recessions.

The common rule of thumb is that a recession is two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth, often called a technical recession.

Canada has no official authority that dates recessions. The C.D. Howe Institute’s Business Cycle Council dates Canadian business cycles, but it is not an official body. In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) is the official authority for dating US business cycles.

The better approach, from the lecture, is to look at many indicators and not only real GDP. The C.D. Howe Council defines a recession as a persistent, pronounced and pervasive decline in aggregate economic activity. That gives three dimensions to consider together: duration, amplitude and scope (how widespread the decline is). NBER and the Council look at real GDP, employment and output across many sectors. The Council does not treat the two-quarter rule as the true test.

Memory Aid — The Three P’s

Persistent = Duration (how long). Pronounced = Amplitude (how deep). Pervasive = Scope (how widespread). Think “Long, Deep, Wide.”

Who’s who: Canada has a Council (unofficial); the US has a Bureau (official).

Common MC trap: “Statistics Canada officially declares recessions” is FALSE.

Self-test: cover the page and match each P to its dimension, then say which body is official in each country.

Worked Example B — Did Canada Go Into Recession in Late 2025–Early 2026?
From the Week 6 lecture. Statistics Canada’s initial release (May 2026) showed real GDP growth of −0.25% from 2025Q3 to 2025Q4 and −0.04% from 2025Q4 to 2026Q1. Was Canada in a recession?
Step 1: Apply the rule of thumb to the initial data.
  2025Q3 to 2025Q4:  -0.25%   (negative)
  2025Q4 to 2026Q1:  -0.04%   (negative)
  Two consecutive negative quarters = a technical recession.

Step 2: Why economists did not rush to declare one: REVISIONS.
  Over 2022-24, quarterly GDP growth rates were revised by 0.14
  percentage points on average.
  -0.04% + 0.14 = +0.10%
  A tiny decline can easily turn into growth.

Step 3: That is exactly what happened.
  2026Q1 was revised to about +0.1%.

Final answer: Canada did NOT go into a technical recession.

Lesson: a two-quarter rule applied to first-release data is fragile.
The three-P approach (persistent, pronounced, pervasive) is more
sensible.

Section 5

Inflation and Unemployment Over the Cycle

The inflation rate is the percentage increase in the price level (CPI) from one period to the next, as in Chapters 4 and 5. Here is how it and unemployment behave over the cycle.

  • Expansions: demand for products is high relative to supply, so prices rise faster. Inflation tends to rise, especially toward the end of an expansion.
  • Recessions: demand is low relative to supply, so prices rise more slowly or even fall. Inflation falls (disinflation), sometimes below zero (deflation). From the slides: inflation near 12–13% in 1981 fell to about 4% after the 1981–82 recession, and it dipped below 0% in 2009 and in 2020.
  • Unemployment: it rises sharply during recessions and falls slowly during expansions. From slide Figure 6.9: about 7% to 13% in 1981–82; about 7.5% to 12% around 1990–92; about 6% to 8.7% in 2008–09; and about 5.7% to 14% in spring 2020, followed by a fast recovery.

Vocabulary Trap — Disinflation vs Deflation

Inflation falling from 4% to 1% is disinflation: prices are still rising, just more slowly. Deflation is negative inflation, where the price level actually falls. “Lower inflation” does not mean “lower prices.”

Section 6

The Financial System: Stocks, Bonds and Intermediaries

Firms can finance some expansion through retained earnings (profits that are reinvested), but that is often not enough for rapid expansion.

The financial system is made up of financial markets and financial intermediaries. It supports economic activity by moving funds from households (savers) to firms (borrowers).

Financial Markets: Stocks and Bonds

  • A stock is a financial security representing partial ownership of a firm. Shareholders may receive part of the profits as dividends. The share price depends partly on investors’ expectations of future profitability. Stocks do not mature: they are a permanent ownership claim.
  • A bond is a financial security promising to repay a fixed amount. It is essentially a loan from a household to a firm (or to a government). A coupon bond pays interest every year and repays the principal (face value) at maturity. A discount bond pays no periodic interest; it is sold below face value, and the full face value is repaid at maturity.

The slides’ example: “I pay $1,000 for a 2-year coupon bond with a 6% annual interest rate issued by CT. CT has effectively borrowed $1,000 from me. In 1 year CT pays me $60 interest; in 2 years CT pays another $60 and repays the $1,000 principal.” In total I receive $1,120.

Worked Example C — Bond Cash Flows
Trace the cash flows to the bondholder for each of these bonds. (1) The slides’ CT bond: $1,000, 2 years, 6% coupon. (2) A 3-year, $5,000 coupon bond at 4%. (3) A 1-year discount bond with a $1,000 face value, bought for $950.
(1) CT bond ($1,000, 2 years, 6% coupon)
    Interest each year = 6% x 1,000 = $60
    Year 1: +$60
    Year 2: +$60 interest + $1,000 principal = +$1,060
    Total received = 60 + 1,060 = $1,120

(2) 3-year, $5,000 coupon bond at 4%
    Interest each year = 4% x 5,000 = $200
    End of year 1: +$200
    End of year 2: +$200
    End of year 3: +$200 interest + $5,000 principal = +$5,200
    Total interest = 3 x 200 = $600

(3) 1-year discount bond, face value $1,000, bought for $950
    No payments until maturity; at maturity receive $1,000.
    Return = (1,000 - 950) / 950 = 50 / 950 = about 5.3%

Financial Intermediaries

Financial intermediaries are firms such as banks, mutual funds, pension funds and insurance companies that channel funds from households to firms.

  • Banks use deposits to make loans to firms.
  • Mutual funds and ETFs pool savings and invest them, mostly in stocks and/or bonds, worldwide.
  • Insurance companies collect premiums and invest them in financial assets.
  • Pension funds invest workers’ contributions.
Vocabulary Trap — Two Meanings of “Investment”

In everyday speech, buying shares or bonds is “investing.” In macroeconomics, investment (I) means firms’ spending on new capital goods (machines, buildings, data centres), plus residential construction and inventories (Chapter 4).

Buying a stock is saving that flows through the financial system. It becomes investment only when a firm spends the funds on capital. MC questions love this.

Three Key Services of the Financial System

Risk Sharing

Spreading your money over many assets (diversification) reduces risk while keeping a high expected return.

Example: an ETF that holds 500 companies.

Liquidity

Savers can quickly convert their investments into cash.

Example: selling bank shares online in seconds when you need tuition money.

Information

Security prices reflect investors’ beliefs about future revenue, so the system gathers and communicates information.

Example: a company’s stock price drops after it warns that profits will fall, telling everyone the outlook worsened.

Memory Aid — L-I-R: Lending Is Rewarded

Liquidity, Information, Risk sharing.

Classification trick: spread it = risk sharing; cash it = liquidity; read it in the price = information.

Self-test: name the service for each of the three examples above without looking.

Section 7

Saving Equals Investment

The goal here is to show that in a closed economy (one with no trade with the rest of the world), total saving equals total investment. Follow these steps.

  1. Start from Y = C + I + G + NX (Chapter 4).
  2. In a closed economy, NX = 0, so Y = C + I + G.
  3. Subtract C and G from both sides: Y − C − G = I.
  4. Show that total saving also equals Y − C − G (below).

Total (national) saving is S = Sprivate + Spublic.

  • Private saving, Sprivate = Y + TR − C − T. This is households’ income plus transfers, minus what they consume and pay in taxes.
  • Public saving, Spublic = T − G − TR. This is the government’s revenue minus its purchases and transfers. It can be greater than 0 (a budget surplus), equal to 0 (balanced) or less than 0 (a budget deficit).

Now add them together:

S = Sprivate + Spublic
  = (Y + TR − C − T) + (T − G − TR)
  = Y − C − G

and from the income identity, Y − C − G = I

Therefore  S = I   (closed economy)
Why It Matters

The only way a closed economy can invest more (build more capital and so grow faster, Chapter 7) is to save more. The financial system is how S becomes I.

Vocabulary Trap — Transfers Are Not Government Purchases

TR (EI, CPP and Canada Child Benefit payments) is not part of G and not part of GDP. In the saving formulas TR is added to private saving and subtracted from public saving, so it cancels out of national saving.

Also: budget deficit = −Spublic = G + TR − T.

Worked Example D — Textbook Problem 2.5
From the lecture. A closed economy has Y = $11 trillion, C = $8 trillion, I = $2 trillion, TR = $1 trillion and T = $3 trillion. Find (a) private saving, (b) public saving, (c) government purchases, and (d) whether there is a deficit or a surplus.
(a) S_private = Y + TR − C − T = 11 + 1 − 8 − 3 = $1 trillion

(b) S = I = 2, so
    S_public = S − S_private = 2 − 1 = $1 trillion

(c) Y = C + I + G, so G = 11 − 8 − 2 = $1 trillion
    Check: S_public = T − G − TR = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1 ✓

(d) S_public > 0, so the government has a budget SURPLUS of
    $1 trillion.
Section 8

The Market for Loanable Funds

The financial system has many markets: stocks, bonds, bank deposits, GICs and more. In our macro model we collapse them all into one: the market for loanable funds. It is the (conceptual) interaction of borrowers and lenders that determines the real interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds exchanged. For now we include only domestic agents (households, firms and government), with no foreign lenders or borrowers.

No authority sets this rate. The equilibrium real interest rate is the one where the quantity of loanable funds demanded equals the quantity supplied. (The Bank of Canada sets a short-term policy rate, but that belongs to later chapters. In this model the real interest rate is a market price.)

  • Demand for loanable funds comes from firms borrowing to finance investment projects. A higher real interest rate makes fewer projects profitable, so demand is downward sloping (negatively related to r).
  • Supply of loanable funds is national saving (Sprivate + Spublic). Private saving rises when the real interest rate rises, so supply is upward sloping. Public saving matters too: surpluses add to supply and deficits subtract from it.
  • The relevant interest rate is the real interest rate (nominal minus expected inflation). It is what borrowers really pay and what savers really earn.
Memory Aid — Borrowers Demand, Savers Supply
  • Demand shifts with the expected profitability of investment: new technology, a forecast expansion (shifts it right) or recession (shifts it left), and business taxes (for example, faster write-offs or expensing shift it right).
  • Supply shifts with saving decisions: households saving more (for example, precautionary saving out of job worry) or less, taxes on interest income, and the government budget balance (a deficit shifts supply left; a surplus shifts it right).

Results rule: “Demand moves them together, Supply splits them up.”

  • D↑ → r↑, L↑
  • D↓ → r↓, L↓
  • S↑ → r↓, L↑
  • S↓ → r↑, L↓

Self-test: cover the rule and redraw Figure 10’s four panels.

Vocabulary Trap — Shift vs Movement Along

A change in the real interest rate never shifts either curve here; it causes a movement along them. When demand rises and r goes up, households save more. That is an increase in the quantity of saving supplied (a movement along S), not an increase in supply.

Section 9

Using the Model: Shifts and Crowding Out

For each application below, follow the same chain: event → which curve → which direction → effect on r and L → effect on saving and investment.

An Increase in Demand: New Technology and the AI Build-Out

In the slides’ Figure 6.4, technological change makes investment more profitable. Demand shifts right (D1 → D2). The real interest rate rises from r1 to r2, and the quantity of loanable funds rises from L1 to L2. Saving and investment both rise.

The lecture application is the same shift: tech firms borrowing massive amounts to build generative-AI infrastructure (for example, Meta’s US$30 billion bond sale in October 2025). The result is a higher r for every borrower and more loanable funds exchanged.

An Increase in Supply: Households Save More

In the slides’ Figure 6.5, individuals worried about losing their jobs save more. Supply shifts right (S1 → S2). The real interest rate falls, and the quantity of loanable funds rises. Investment rises too, because more projects are now profitable at the lower rate.

The lecture also asks about Textbook Problem 2.13. Suppose the government taxes only real (not nominal) interest. The after-tax return to saving rises, so households save more at every r. Supply shifts right, the equilibrium r falls and L rises. The quantities of saving and investment both rise (and they are equal, S = I).

A Budget Deficit and Crowding Out

In the slides’ Figure 6.6, the government runs a deficit and borrows from households. Public saving falls, so the supply of loanable funds shifts left (S1 → S2). The real interest rate rises from r1 to r2 and the equilibrium quantity of loanable funds falls from L1 to L2, so private investment falls.

This is crowding out: an increase in government purchases reduces private expenditures, including investment by firms. Households are crowded out too. At the higher r they move along their saving curve and save more privately, which means they consume less.

Why Crowding Out Matters for Growth

Less investment today means a smaller capital stock tomorrow, and so slower long-run growth (see Chapter 7). A budget surplus does the reverse, which is sometimes called “crowding in.”

Lecture Application: The Productivity Mega Deduction

The lecture discusses a proposed federal tax incentive that would let businesses immediately write off (expense) 100% of the cost of eligible capital property in the year it becomes available for use. Federal Budget 2025 (November 4, 2025) packaged these measures as the “Productivity Super-Deduction.”

The analysis is simple. Lower taxes on new capital raise the after-tax profitability of investment, so the demand for loanable funds shifts right: r rises and L rises. It is the same picture as Figure 7.

Worked Example E — The Productivity Mega Deduction
Firms can immediately write off 100% of the cost of eligible capital property. Using the four-step chain, what happens in the market for loanable funds?
Step 1: Who is affected first?
  Firms, which are borrowers. So the DEMAND curve moves.

Step 2: Which direction?
  More investment projects become profitable at every r,
  so demand shifts RIGHT.

Step 3: Result.
  r rises and L rises. Saving and investment both rise.

Step 4: Side effect to mention.
  If the deduction lowers government revenue and creates or
  raises a budget deficit, SUPPLY also shifts left. Then r rises
  even more, and the effect on L becomes ambiguous.
Worked Example F — Textbook Problem 2.10: A Forecast Expansion
The economy is in a recession, but forecasts say an expansion is coming soon. (a) What happens to the expected profitability of new investment in plant and equipment? (b) What happens in the market for loanable funds?
(a) Firms expect more sales, so the expected profitability of new
    investment in plant and equipment RISES.

(b) Demand for loanable funds shifts RIGHT.
    The equilibrium real interest rate r rises and L rises.

(Households may also expect higher incomes, but we focus on the
demand shift, as the textbook does.)
Exam Method — 4 Steps for Any Loanable-Funds Question
  1. Who is directly affected? Borrowers (firms) mean demand. Savers or the government budget mean supply.
  2. Which direction? Is there more or less borrowing or saving at every r?
  3. Shift the curve, find the new equilibrium and read r and L.
  4. Translate. L is the quantity of saving, which equals investment. If r rose, investment that was not directly targeted is crowded out.
Common Mistake — Deficits Shift Supply, Not Demand

In Hubbard’s model the government deficit is negative public saving, so it shifts the supply curve left. Some other textbooks show government borrowing as extra demand. Both give r↑, but for this course draw it as supply shifting left (that is how the slides draw it in Figure 6.6), with L falling.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Growth Rates and a Technical Recession

Real GDP (billions) is Q1 = 600.0, Q2 = 603.0, Q3 = 601.2 and Q4 = 599.4. (a) Find the quarter-to-quarter growth rates. (b) Is this a technical recession? (c) Why might an economist still hesitate to call it a recession?

Show answer
(a) Q1 to Q2: 3 / 600 = +0.50%
Q2 to Q3: −1.8 / 603 = −0.30%
Q3 to Q4: −1.8 / 601.2 = −0.30%
(b) Two consecutive quarters of negative growth, so yes, it is a technical recession on this data.
(c) First-release data get revised (about 0.14 percentage points on average in Canada over 2022–24). A −0.3% decline is more than double that average, so it is less likely to flip to growth than the 2026Q1 figure of −0.04%. But a real recession should also be persistent, pronounced and pervasive, so check employment and sector-wide output, as the C.D. Howe Council does.
Problem 2 — National Saving

A closed economy has (in billions) Y = 2,000, C = 1,300, G = 350, T = 400 and TR = 150. Find private saving, public saving, national saving, investment and the budget balance.

Show answer
Sprivate = 2,000 + 150 − 1,300 − 400 = 450
Spublic = 400 − 350 − 150 = −100, a budget deficit of 100
S = 450 − 100 = 350
I = Y − C − G = 2,000 − 1,300 − 350 = 350 ✓ (S = I)
Problem 3 — Name That Financial Concept

(a) Priya buys a Government of Canada bond that pays $40 a year for 5 years and then $1,000. What kind of bond is it, and is she an owner or a lender?
(b) Marc puts his savings in an ETF holding 300 companies’ shares. Which key service is this?
(c) A firm’s share price drops 20% the day it announces a product recall. Which service is this?
(d) Your bank uses your deposit to lend to a local bakery to buy an oven. Is the bank a financial market or an intermediary? Which macro variable rises when the bakery buys the oven?
(e) True or false: buying shares of an existing company on the TSX counts as investment (I) in GDP.

Show answer
(a) Coupon bond; she is a lender (a bond is a loan).
(b) Risk sharing (diversification).
(c) Information.
(d) A financial intermediary; investment (I) rises when the oven is bought.
(e) False. That is financial investment (saving changing hands). Macro I is spending on new capital goods.
Problem 4 — Shift It

For each event, which curve shifts, in which direction, and what happens to the equilibrium real interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds?
(a) Households fear layoffs and save more.
(b) The government moves from a deficit to a surplus.
(c) Firms expect a recession next year.
(d) A new tax credit for business investment in machinery.
(e) The government taxes only real interest income instead of nominal (Textbook Problem 2.13).

Show answer
(a) Supply right → r↓, L↑.
(b) Public saving rises, so supply right → r↓, L↑ (crowding in).
(c) Demand left → r↓, L↓.
(d) Demand right → r↑, L↑.
(e) Supply right (higher after-tax return to saving) → r↓, L↑; saving and investment both rise.
Problem 5 — Application: Northland’s AI Boom Meets a Deficit

Northland (a closed economy) faces two events at once: tech firms launch a borrowing spree to build AI data centres, and the government runs a large new budget deficit.

(a) Show each shift. (b) What happens to the real interest rate: is the result certain or ambiguous? (c) What happens to the quantity of loanable funds? (d) Which group’s investment gets crowded out, and why does it matter for long-run growth? (e) Before the deficit, GDP growth over the last two quarters was −0.2% and −0.1%, unemployment barely moved and only one sector shrank. Would the C.D. Howe Council likely call a recession?

Show answer
(a) AI borrowing → demand shifts right. The deficit → supply shifts left.

(b) r rises for certain, because both shifts push r up.

(c) Ambiguous. Higher demand raises L and lower supply reduces L, so the result depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts.

(d) Firms outside the AI boom (for example a manufacturer or a small business) face the higher r and cancel projects. Less capital means slower growth of productivity and of real GDP per capita (Chapter 7).

(e) Probably not. It is a technical recession, but the decline is shallow (not pronounced) and narrow (not pervasive). The Council looks at duration, amplitude and scope together.

  • Label the four phases of the business cycle
  • Say who dates recessions in Canada and the US, and explain the 3 P’s

  • Distinguish stocks, coupon bonds and discount bonds, and name four financial intermediaries
  • Name the three services (L-I-R) from an example
  • Derive S = I and compute private saving, public saving and the budget balance
  • Draw the loanable funds market and shift it for technology, saving, taxes and deficits
  • Explain crowding out
