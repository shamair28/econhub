Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every concept you will encounter in ECON 1BB3 — GDP, unemployment, inflation, the business cycle, monetary policy, fiscal policy — traces back to ideas introduced in this chapter. The problem of scarcity, the logic of opportunity cost, and the discipline of marginal thinking are not just introductory vocabulary: they are the lens through which economists examine an entire economy, from why the Bank of Canada raises interest rates to why a federal budget cannot fund every program at once.

Chapter 1 also teaches you how economists think, not just what they have concluded. You will learn to distinguish facts from value judgments, understand how economic models work, and see why the pursuit of efficiency often runs into real trade-offs with equity — a tension that resurfaces later when we discuss fiscal policy and the government's budget. You will also draw the line between microeconomics and macroeconomics, so you know exactly what this course is (and isn't) about.

Chapter Roadmap This chapter covers the three key economic ideas (rationality, incentives, marginal thinking), opportunity cost, the MB vs. MC decision rule, how economists build and test models, the efficiency-equity trade-off, the micro/macro distinction, and positive vs. normative analysis. Section 2

Scarcity and the Economic Problem

Scarcity is the starting point of all economics: human wants are unlimited, but the resources available to fulfill them — time, money, labour, land, capital — are finite. Because we cannot have everything, every choice involves a sacrifice. This is not a pessimistic observation but a fundamental reality that shapes how individuals, firms, and governments behave.

Scarcity forces every society, regardless of its political system or level of development, to answer three fundamental questions:

  • What goods and services will be produced? With limited resources, producing more of one thing means producing less of another.
  • How will they be produced? Which combination of inputs — labour-intensive vs. capital-intensive methods — should be used?
  • Who will receive what is produced? How are goods and services distributed among members of society?

These questions must be answered whether a society uses a market economy (prices and voluntary exchange), a centrally planned economy (government directives), or a mixed economy like Canada's. The difference lies in the mechanism used to answer them — and markets tend to do so more efficiently than central planners.

Worked Example — Applying the Three Questions: Canada's Federal Budget
How does the federal government's annual budget answer the three fundamental economic questions?
WHAT is produced?
  → The budget allocates a limited pool of tax revenue and borrowed
    funds across competing priorities: health transfers, defence,
    infrastructure, Employment Insurance, debt-servicing costs.
    More spent on one (say, defence) leaves less available for
    another (say, infrastructure), given a fixed budget envelope.

HOW is it produced/delivered?
  → Some programs are delivered directly by federal departments;
    health care dollars are transferred to provinces to deliver;
    defence equipment is contracted out to private firms.

WHO receives it?
  → Benefits are distributed by eligibility rule, not equally:
    Employment Insurance goes to eligible job losers, Old Age
    Security by age, health transfers by provincial population.

Key takeaway: Even a government with taxing and borrowing power
faces scarcity. Every dollar allocated to one program is a dollar
unavailable for another — the same trade-off logic you will see
again in Chapter 12 (Fiscal Policy).
Common Misconception A trade-off does not mean money is "wasted" — it means the next best alternative was forgone. If Parliament allocates an extra $2 billion to health transfers, the opportunity cost might be forgone infrastructure spending or a smaller reduction in the deficit — not wasted money. Every allocation of limited resources has an opportunity cost.
Section 3

The Three Key Economic Ideas

Economists distill human behaviour into three foundational assumptions that appear in every model throughout this course — including the aggregate models of GDP, inflation, and unemployment you'll build starting in Chapter 4. Understanding these ideas deeply — not just as bullet points — will let you reason about any macroeconomic scenario from first principles.

Idea 1 — People Are Rational

Rationality in economics does not mean people are always right or never emotional. It means people make decisions they believe will make them better off, weighing costs against benefits. An economist says households are rational when they save more once interest rates rise — that predictable response is what makes economic analysis possible.

Idea 2 — People Respond to Incentives

When the costs or benefits of an action change, behaviour changes in a predictable direction. A higher interest rate discourages borrowing; a more generous income-support program can reduce the urgency of a job search; a tax cut can encourage more spending. Macroeconomic policy analysis is largely about designing or predicting how incentive changes — through interest rates, taxes, or transfers — alter the behaviour of households and firms across the whole economy.

Idea 3 — Optimal Decisions Are Made at the Margin

Economic decisions are rarely all-or-nothing. The relevant question is always: should I do a little more of this activity? The answer depends on the marginal benefit (MB) — extra satisfaction or payoff from one more unit — versus the marginal cost (MC) — extra cost of one more unit. If MB > MC, do more. If MC > MB, do less. Stop when MB = MC.

Worked Example — Incentives in Action: Employment Insurance and Job Search
Employment Insurance (EI) replaces a portion of a laid-off worker's income while they search for a new job. How does the generosity of that benefit affect how quickly people accept a new job offer?
Suppose EI replaces 55% of a worker's previous earnings, up to a
maximum, for as long as 45 weeks depending on the region's
unemployment rate.

A worker weighing a lower-paying job offer against continued
search compares:
  MB of continued search = expected value of finding a
                            better-paying job later
  MC of continued search = income given up now, cushioned
                            by the EI benefit

The more generous or longer-lasting the benefit, the smaller the
MC of continued search — so, predictably, average job search
duration tends to rise. Cut the benefit, and MC rises, so workers
accept available offers faster.

Conclusion: This is Idea 2 in action — but it also previews the
efficiency-equity tension from Section 7. Generous EI cushions
unemployed workers (equity) while dulling the incentive to search
intensively or accept available work quickly (efficiency).
Exam Tip Questions that ask you to explain a household's, firm's, or government's decision using "economic reasoning" almost always want you to invoke one or more of these three ideas: rational cost-benefit weighing, response to changed incentives, or marginal analysis.
Section 4

Opportunity Cost: The True Cost of Every Choice

The opportunity cost of any choice is the value of the next best alternative you give up to make that choice. This is the most important definition in Chapter 1 — and the one students most often get wrong on exams. Two critical details:

  • It is the NEXT best alternative, not any forgone option. If you choose to study economics and your next best option was working ($15/hr), the opportunity cost is $15 — not the sum of everything else you could have done.
  • It includes implicit (non-monetary) costs. If a government spends $10 billion on a stimulus package financed by borrowing, the opportunity cost includes the future debt-servicing costs and whatever else that borrowing capacity could have funded.

Opportunity cost explains why a highly profitable firm won't necessarily expand into every market it could technically enter — the capital and management attention tied up in a new venture is capital and attention forgone somewhere else, even if the new venture alone would be profitable.

Worked Example — Student Time Allocation
You have three hours on Friday evening. You can (A) study for your ECON 1BB3 quiz, (B) work a shift earning $70, or (C) attend a friend's birthday dinner you value at $50. You choose to study. What is your opportunity cost?
Options ranked by value to you:
  A. Study for quiz       ← chosen
  B. Work shift           $70 value
  C. Birthday dinner      $50 value

Opportunity cost = value of the NEXT best forgone alternative
                 = $70 (the shift you didn't work)

Common mistake: Adding $70 + $50 = $120. WRONG.
You can only do one thing with those hours.
The opportunity cost is only the best forgone option.

Section 5

Thinking at the Margin: MB vs. MC

Most economic decisions are not binary — they involve choosing how much of something to do. The marginal framework answers this: compare the extra benefit of doing one more unit to the extra cost. This "one more unit" thinking is why economists say decisions are made at the margin.

The Marginal Decision Rule:

  • If MB > MC: do one more unit — you gain more than you give up.
  • If MB < MC: do one fewer unit — the extra cost exceeds the extra benefit.
  • If MB = MC: you are at the optimum — no improvement possible by changing quantity.

Notice that MB tends to fall as you do more of something (diminishing marginal benefit), while MC tends to rise (each additional unit is harder or more costly). This is why the two curves cross at a finite optimum — the same logic you'll later apply to the marginal benefit and marginal cost of one more dollar of government spending, or one more percentage point of interest rate.

Worked Example — How Many Extra Practice Problems Should You Do?
It's the night before your test. Each extra practice problem gives you some expected grade improvement (MB) but costs you sleep (MC). Should you do one more?
Problem  MB (grade value)   MC (lost sleep value)   Decision
  1          $20                  $4                  Do it (MB > MC)
  2          $16                  $7                  Do it (MB > MC)
  3          $12                 $11                  Do it (MB > MC)
  4           $9                 $15                  Stop (MB < MC)
  5           $6                 $19                  Don't do it

Optimal: Stop after problem 3.
Doing problem 4 would cost more (in tomorrow's alertness)
than the expected grade benefit it provides.
Sunk Cost Warning When applying MB vs. MC, only consider future costs and benefits — never past (sunk) costs. If you already bought a $200 textbook you dislike, that cost is sunk. The relevant question is: is the MB of continuing to use it greater than the MC (your time and frustration)? If not, switch resources — the $200 is gone regardless.
Section 6

Economic Models and the Scientific Method

An economic model is a simplified representation of reality designed to isolate the key forces driving a particular outcome. The map analogy is apt: a subway map omits buildings and topography because those details would obscure the one thing you need to know — which lines connect which stations. Similarly, macroeconomic models omit irrelevant complexity to highlight causal relationships between variables like interest rates, spending, and inflation.

Economists use the scientific method — the same process as physicists and biologists — to develop and test models. The key is that every economic theory must make a testable hypothesis: a prediction that can in principle be disproven by data. Models that consistently fail the data test are revised or abandoned.

The Five-Step Model Development Process

  1. Decide on assumptions — Simplify reality. For example, assume households respond predictably to changes in borrowing costs.
  2. Formulate a testable hypothesis — State a specific, falsifiable prediction: "If the Bank of Canada raises its policy rate, inflation will fall."
  3. Use economic data to test the hypothesis — Collect real-world data and apply statistical methods to see whether the prediction holds.
  4. Revise the model if it fails to explain the data well — Weak explanatory power signals a flaw in assumptions. Adjust and re-test.
  5. Retain the revised model as a useful tool — A model that consistently explains and predicts is retained for policy analysis and forecasting.
Worked Example — Applying the Scientific Method: Does Raising Interest Rates Reduce Inflation?
Use the five-step process to analyze the model "raising the Bank of Canada's policy interest rate reduces inflation."
Step 1 — Assumptions:
  • Households and firms respond to the cost of borrowing (Idea 2)
  • Higher rates raise the cost of financing mortgages, big
    purchases, and business investment

Step 2 — Hypothesis:
  "A 1-percentage-point increase in the policy interest rate
   reduces the inflation rate by roughly 0.3–0.5 percentage
   points within 12–18 months."

Step 3 — Test with data:
  Compare inflation before and after the Bank of Canada's
  2022–23 tightening cycle, when the overnight rate rose from
  0.25% (March 2022) to 5% (July 2023) — the fastest hiking
  cycle in decades.1

Step 4 — Evaluate:
  Inflation did fall from a peak of 8.1% (June 2022) back toward
  the Bank's 2% target, but only gradually — consistent with
  economist Milton Friedman's observation that monetary policy
  works with "long and variable lags," a idea you'll revisit in
  Chapter 11 (Monetary Policy).

Step 5 — Revised model retained:
  "Interest rate increases reduce inflation mainly by cooling
   aggregate demand, with effects appearing gradually over
   multiple quarters rather than immediately" — the relationship
   you will study in depth in Chapters 9 and 11.

Section 7

Efficiency, Equity, and Economic Systems

Economists use two distinct concepts of efficiency. Productive efficiency means a good or service is produced at the lowest possible cost — no waste. Allocative efficiency means resources are used to produce the specific mix of goods and services that society values most — the right things are being made. An economy can be productively efficient (low-cost production) while being allocatively inefficient (producing things nobody wants).

Equity refers to a fair distribution of economic benefits. The tension between efficiency and equity is one of the most debated issues in macroeconomic policy. Policies that redistribute income (progressive taxation, transfer payments) may reduce incentives to work, save, and invest, thereby reducing efficiency — but may be judged desirable on equity grounds. This trade-off has no purely economic answer; it involves normative (value) judgments about fairness, and it will come up again when we cover automatic stabilizers and fiscal policy in Chapter 12.

The three types of economic systems differ fundamentally in how they balance efficiency and equity, and in who answers the three fundamental questions:

Economy Type Who Decides? Productive Efficiency Allocative Efficiency Real-World Example
Market Economy Prices & voluntary exchange High — competition drives costs down High — consumers signal preferences via prices United States (largely)
Centrally Planned Government planners Low — no profit motive to cut costs Low — planners cannot know all preferences USSR (historical), North Korea
Mixed Economy Markets + government regulation, plus an independent central bank Moderate Moderate Canada, most of Europe

Section 8

Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics

Economics is traditionally divided into two broad branches. Microeconomics studies the decisions of individual households, firms, and the specific markets in which they interact — how a single firm sets its price, how a consumer allocates a grocery budget, why one industry consolidates into a few large players. Macroeconomics — the focus of this course — studies the economy as a whole: aggregate measures like total output (GDP), the overall price level (inflation), the national unemployment rate, and economy-wide policy tools like the Bank of Canada's interest rate decisions or the federal government's budget.

Both branches share the same foundational ideas from this chapter — rationality, incentives, marginal thinking, opportunity cost — but apply them at a different level of aggregation. A microeconomist asks, "why did this firm raise its price?" A macroeconomist asks, "why did the average price level across the entire economy rise by 3% this year?" Some questions bridge both fields: understanding how a single labour market clears (a micro question) is part of what explains the national unemployment rate you'll study in Chapter 5 (a macro question).

Branch Typical Question Example Topics
Microeconomics "Why did this firm raise its price?" Supply and demand in a single market, elasticity, firm production costs, market structure
Macroeconomics "Why did prices rise across the whole economy?" GDP, unemployment, inflation, the business cycle, monetary and fiscal policy
This Course's Focus ECON 1BB3 is concerned exclusively with the macro side. Starting in Chapter 4, you'll study how economists measure the whole economy (GDP, unemployment, inflation) and the policy tools — monetary policy via the Bank of Canada, fiscal policy via the federal budget — used to influence it.
Section 9

Positive vs. Normative Analysis

Positive analysis describes the world as it is. Positive statements are factual claims that can — in principle — be tested and disproven with evidence. "Canada's unemployment rate was 6.8% in December 2024" is positive. So is "raising the policy interest rate reduces business investment." You may disagree with that claim, but it can be evaluated empirically.

Normative analysis involves value judgments about what the world ought to be. Normative statements cannot be resolved with data alone — they reflect priorities, values, and political beliefs. "The government should increase infrastructure spending to reduce unemployment" is normative. So is "Canada should run a balanced budget every year." Economists can inform normative debates by providing positive analysis (showing costs and benefits), but the normative choice ultimately belongs to citizens and policymakers.

Worked Example — Positive or Normative? Classify These Statements
Identify each statement as positive (P) or normative (N) and explain your reasoning.
1. "Canada's unemployment rate was 6.8% in December 2024."
   → P. A factual claim; can be verified with government data.

2. "The government should increase infrastructure spending
    to reduce unemployment."
   → N. "Should" signals a value judgment about priorities.

3. "A $10 billion increase in government spending raises GDP
    by more than $10 billion, due to the multiplier effect."
   → P. A testable causal claim (may be right or wrong, but
     it is an empirical question you'll revisit in Chapter 8).

4. "Canada's 2% inflation target is too low."
   → N. "Too low" is a value judgment about what is desirable.

5. "An increase in the money supply that outpaces real GDP
    growth leads to higher inflation."
   → P. A hypothesis about cause and effect; testable with data
     (you'll formalize this in Chapter 5's quantity theory).

6. "Canada should run a balanced federal budget every year."
   → N. A policy preference based on values about fiscal
     responsibility versus flexibility during downturns.
Value Judgment Smuggling A common pitfall: statements that sound factual but embed a normative judgment. "This monetary policy is too aggressive" seems positive, but "too aggressive" often conceals a normative standard (aggressive compared to what baseline? by whose weighting of inflation risk versus growth risk?). On the exam, look for loaded words: "too much," "too little," "should," "ought," "fair," "unfair," "better," "worse" — these signal normative content.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Opportunity Cost

Jordan has Saturday afternoon free. He can (A) attend a free info session on summer government internships he values at $35, (B) work a shift earning $65, or (C) watch a hockey game he values at $20. He chooses to work the shift. What is the opportunity cost of his decision?

Show answer
The opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone = the info session valued at $35. It is NOT $55 ($35 + $20), because Jordan can only do one thing with his afternoon. The hockey game ($20) is irrelevant once the info session ($35) is identified as the next best option.
Problem 2 — Marginal Analysis

A student can attend extra help sessions before the final exam. Session 1: MB=$22, MC=$5; Session 2: MB=$17, MC=$9; Session 3: MB=$13, MC=$13; Session 4: MB=$9, MC=$18. How many sessions should the student attend?

Show answer
Optimal: 3 sessions.
Session 1: MB $22 > MC $5 → Attend ✓
Session 2: MB $17 > MC $9 → Attend ✓
Session 3: MB $13 = MC $13 → Stop here (MB = MC is the optimum)
Session 4: MB $9 < MC $18 → Do not add this session ✗
Problem 3 — Positive or Normative?

Classify each statement as Positive (P) or Normative (N):
(a) "Suppose the Bank of Canada's overnight rate target is 3.00%."
(b) "The Bank of Canada should lower its overnight rate target."
(c) "A higher overnight rate reduces mortgage affordability."
(d) "Canada's federal debt is too high."

Show answer
(a) P — A stated fact about the rate (verifiable, no value judgment).
(b) N — "Should" is a value judgment.
(c) P — Empirical claim; testable with data (may be correct or incorrect).
(d) N — "Too high" embeds a value judgment about what is sustainable or fair.
Problem 4 — Economic Systems

For each situation, identify the economy type and explain how the three fundamental economic questions are answered: (a) the Bank of Canada's conduct of monetary policy; (b) Canada's retail and grocery industry; (c) North Korea's state-controlled currency and price system.

Show answer
(a) Mixed economy, with a technocratic public institution: the Bank of Canada's Governing Council — not a market price — sets the interest rate (how monetary conditions are "produced"), balancing inflation control against growth, within an otherwise market-based economy.
(b) Market economy: firms decide what to stock and how to price based on consumer demand and competition; who receives goods is determined by willingness and ability to pay.
(c) Centrally planned: the state directly controls currency issuance and prices rather than allowing markets to set them.
Problem 5 — Economics Application: Canada's COVID-19 Emergency Response Benefit (CERB)

In 2020, the federal government introduced the Canada Emergency Response Benefit (CERB), paying eligible workers $500/week regardless of their prior earnings. Between April and early October 2020, CERB paid out roughly $81.6 billion to about 8.9 million people.2 Using the concepts from this chapter, answer: (a) Identify ONE positive claim and ONE normative claim about CERB. (b) Using Idea 2 (people respond to incentives), explain why some workers who could earn less than $500/week from available work might have had a reduced incentive to accept a job offer while CERB was in effect. (c) What is the federal government's opportunity cost of the money spent on CERB?

Show answer
(a) Positive: "CERB paid out approximately $81.6 billion to about 8.9 million recipients between April and early October 2020" — testable, factual, and verifiable against government data.
Normative: "The federal government should have means-tested CERB more strictly to reduce its cost" — a value judgment about how the program ought to have been designed.

(b) Incentive effect: A worker who could only earn, say, $400/week at an available job compares the MB of accepting that job (still less income than the flat $500/week CERB payment, plus effort and loss of job-search flexibility) against the MC of continued search (forgone flexibility, but a guaranteed benefit that didn't shrink as long as they remained eligible). Because CERB paid a flat amount rather than tapering with part-time earnings, it predictably reduced the incentive to accept low-wage work at the margin, for the workers whose alternative wage fell below the benefit — a real-world illustration of Idea 2.

(c) Opportunity cost: The roughly $81.6 billion spent on CERB represents forgone alternative uses of federal funds and borrowing capacity — it could instead have funded expanded EI reform, health care capacity, or a smaller deficit and lower future debt-servicing costs. As with any government spending decision, the opportunity cost isn't "wasted money" — it's whatever the next-best use of those dollars would have been, a trade-off you'll examine formally in Chapter 12 (Fiscal Policy).
