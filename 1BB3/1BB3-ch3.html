Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every price you encounter — the price of lumber for a home renovation, the interest rate on a mortgage, the wage in a part-time job — is the outcome of supply and demand interacting in a market. Chapter 3 gives you the tools to explain those prices, predict how they change, and evaluate the consequences of any event that disrupts them. It is one of the most tested chapters in introductory economics, and it is the foundation everything else in this course is built on.

The framework you build here — demand curves, supply curves, equilibrium, and shifts — reappears constantly in macroeconomics: the labour market (Chapter 5), the money market (Chapter 10), and the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model for the entire economy (Chapter 9) all use this exact logic, just applied to a bigger, aggregated market. Understanding supply and demand is the lens through which economists interpret almost every economic question, from a single market to the whole country.

Chapter Roadmap This chapter covers: the demand curve and its shifters, the supply curve and its shifters, market equilibrium (graphical and algebraic), how equilibrium changes when curves shift, how to classify goods (normal/inferior, substitutes/complements), and how price ceilings and price floors override equilibrium. Term Test #1 tests Chapters 1–3 (15% of your grade, 30 multiple-choice questions).
Section 2

The Demand Curve: Consumers and Price

The quantity demanded of a good is the amount consumers are willing and able to buy at a specific price, holding all other factors constant (ceteris paribus). Quantity demanded answers the question: "At this price, how much will buyers purchase?"

The Law of Demand states that, all else equal, when the price of a good falls, quantity demanded rises; when price rises, quantity demanded falls. Two forces drive this:

  • Substitution effect: When a good becomes relatively cheaper, consumers switch toward it and away from substitutes.
  • Income effect: When price falls, consumers' purchasing power effectively rises — the same income now buys more — so they buy more of most goods including this one.

A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Plotting these pairs on a graph with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal gives a demand curve — which slopes downward because of the Law of Demand.

Worked Example — Reading a Demand Schedule: Lumber
The table below shows the weekly demand schedule for lumber (in thousands of units), given by Qd = 200 − 10P. Draw conclusions about buyer behaviour at each price.
  Price per unit    Quantity demanded (thousands)
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────
      $20               0
      $16              40
      $12              80
      $8              120
      $5              150

Reading the schedule:
  • At $20: buyers demand nothing — the price is too high
  • At $12: buyers demand 80 thousand units per week
  • At $5: buyers demand 150 thousand units — low enough that
    builders who wouldn't buy at $12 now expand purchases

Key insight: This is the MARKET demand — the sum of ALL buyers'
individual demands at each price.

Slope of this demand curve:
  ΔP / ΔQd = ($20 − $5) / (0 − 150) = −$0.10 per thousand units
  Equivalently: each $1 price decrease → 10 thousand more units demanded
Plotting Convention Economists plot price on the vertical (Y) axis and quantity on the horizontal (X) axis — the opposite of the mathematical convention where the independent variable goes on X. Price is the "independent" economic variable here, but by longstanding tradition it goes on Y. Always label your axes explicitly on any exam diagram.
Section 3

What Shifts the Demand Curve

A change in price causes a movement along the demand curve — the quantity demanded changes but the curve itself stays fixed. A change in any non-price factor causes a shift of the demand curve — the entire curve moves left or right, changing the quantity demanded at every price.

This distinction is the most commonly tested concept in Chapter 3. Always ask: did the price of this good change? If yes → movement. If no → look for one of the five shifters below.

Movement along the demand curveShift of the demand curve
Textbook nameA change in quantity demandedA change in demand
Caused byA change in the good's own price — nothing elseA change in any non-price variable (the five TRIBE shifters below)
On the graphSlide from one point to another on the same curveThe whole curve moves RIGHT (increase) or LEFT (decrease)
Exam wording“quantity demanded rises / falls”“demand rises / falls”, “demand increases / decreases”
Vocabulary Trap On multiple-choice questions, “an increase in demand” and “an increase in quantity demanded” are different answers. The first is a shift (a non-price cause); the second is a movement along the curve (the good's own price fell). If the question says the price of the good itself changed, the only correct description is a change in quantity demanded.

The Five Demand Shifters — TRIBE

T — Tastes and Preferences

A change in what buyers want shifts demand even though no price has moved. A trend toward larger homes and home offices raises demand for framing lumber. Tastes can push demand RIGHT or LEFT — read the scenario for direction.

R — Prices of Related Goods

Substitutes (goods that serve the same purpose): if a substitute's price rises, demand for this good shifts RIGHT. If a substitute's price falls, demand shifts LEFT. Complements (goods used together): if a complement's price rises, demand for this good shifts LEFT. If a complement's price falls, demand shifts RIGHT.

I — Income

Normal goods: income rises → demand shifts RIGHT (new-home construction, renovations). Inferior goods: income rises → demand shifts LEFT (lower-quality substitutes). The same income change can shift demand in opposite directions depending on the good.

B — Buyers: Population and Demographics

Population: more households → demand shifts RIGHT. Demographics (age, culture, household make-up): a wave of first-time homebuyers changes the composition of demand. The textbook counts these as one variable — the number and type of buyers in the market.

E — Expected Future Prices

If buyers expect prices to rise, they buy now (demand shifts RIGHT today); if prices are expected to fall, they delay purchases (shift LEFT). Expected future income works the same way for normal goods — a builder expecting a strong year orders lumber today.

Memory Aid — TRIBE and the Direction Shortcuts Demand is shifted by the TRIBE of buyers: Tastes · Related-good prices · Income · Buyers (population & demographics) · Expectations. If a scenario touches none of these five — and not the good's own price — nothing happens to demand at all. Once you've spotted the shifter, three shortcuts give the direction without re-deriving it:
  • Substitutes go the Same way; Complements go Contrary. Substitute's price ↑ → my demand ↑ (same direction). Complement's price ↑ → my demand ↓ (opposite).
  • Normal = Normal direction; Inferior = Inverse. Income ↑ → normal-good demand ↑. Income ↑ → inferior-good demand ↓.
  • Expect a rise, buy before it. Expected future price ↑ → buyers move purchases forward → demand ↑ today (and the reverse for an expected fall).
Before Term Test #1, cover the cards above and write out T-R-I-B-E with its direction rule from memory — the shifter questions are recognition, not calculation, so the list has to be automatic.
Worked Example — A Renovation Boom Shifts Lumber Demand
During 2020–2021, many Canadians stuck at home during the pandemic took on renovation projects (decks, fences, home offices) instead of travelling, while historically low interest rates made borrowing to renovate cheaper. How does this affect the market for lumber? Identify the demand shifter(s) and direction.
Shifters: TASTES (renovation trend) and INCOME/FINANCING EFFECT
(cheaper borrowing acting like higher effective income)

Direction: DEMAND SHIFTS RIGHT

Effect on demand curve:
  At every price, the quantity demanded INCREASES.
  Using Qd = 200 − 10P, a shift right might become Qd = 260 − 10P
  — at the same $12 price, quantity demanded rises from 80 to 140
  thousand units.

What did NOT change: the supply curve (no supply shifter here) —
this is a pure demand shift. What happens next depends on how
supply responds, which is exactly what Section 5 covers.

Section 4

The Supply Curve: Firms and Price

The quantity supplied is the amount a firm is willing and able to produce and sell at a specific price, all else equal. The Law of Supply states that, holding other factors constant, when price rises, quantity supplied rises; when price falls, quantity supplied falls. The logic is straightforward: higher prices make production more profitable, so firms expand output; lower prices reduce profitability, so firms cut back.

A supply schedule lists the quantity supplied at each price. Plotting these on a graph produces a supply curve that slopes upward — the opposite direction from the demand curve. The supply curve also represents the marginal cost of each additional unit: a firm will not supply the next unit unless the price covers its cost of production.

Worked Example — Reading a Supply Schedule: Lumber
The table below shows the weekly supply schedule for lumber, given by Qs = 10P − 40. What does each row tell you about producer behaviour?
  Price per unit    Quantity supplied (thousands)
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────
      $4                0
      $8               40
      $12              80
      $16             120
      $20             160

Reading the schedule:
  • At $4: no mills supply — price doesn't cover production costs
  • At $12: 80 thousand units supplied — profitable at this scale
  • At $20: 160 thousand units supplied — high price justifies
    running mills at full capacity, including higher-cost timber

Market supply = sum of ALL producers' individual supplies. If a
new sawmill opens, market supply increases — a SHIFT in the
supply curve, not a movement (existing mills haven't changed
their behaviour at each price).

Slope of this supply curve:
  ΔP / ΔQs = ($20 − $4) / (160 − 0) = +$0.10 per thousand units
  Equivalently: each $1 price increase → 10 thousand more units supplied
Section 5

What Shifts the Supply Curve

Just as with demand, a change in the price of the good causes a movement along the supply curve. A change in a non-price factor causes a shift of the supply curve — the entire curve moves, changing the quantity supplied at every price. The textbook vocabulary mirrors demand: a movement along the curve is a change in quantity supplied; a shift is a change in supply. There are five supply shifters — SPENT:

Supply ShifterDirection of ShiftExample
S — Price of a substitute in production risesLEFT (supply decreases)OSB panel prices rise → mills shift capacity from raw lumber to panels → less lumber supplied at every price
P — Prices of inputs riseLEFT (supply decreases)A mountain pine beetle infestation destroys standing timber → higher cost per usable log → less supplied
P — Prices of inputs fallRIGHT (supply increases)Cheaper diesel for logging equipment → lower cost → more lumber supplied at every price
E — Expected future price risesLEFT (supply decreases today)Mills hold inventory to sell later at a higher price → less on the market now
N — Number of firms increasesRIGHT (supply increases)New sawmills open in response to strong long-run demand → total market supply rises
T — Technology improvesRIGHT (supply increases)New automated milling equipment cuts cost per unit → more supplied at each price
Memory Aid — SPENT and the Cost Test Supply is shifted by what firms have SPENT: Substitutes in production · Prices of inputs · Expected future prices · Number of firms · Technology. You do not need a separate direction rule for each one — run the cost test: does this make producing the good cheaper or more profitable? Yes → supply shifts RIGHT. Does it make production costlier, or make something else more profitable to produce instead? → supply shifts LEFT.
  • Say left/right, never up/down. Because supply slopes upward, an increase in supply (shift RIGHT) looks like the curve moved down. Check by asking “is more supplied at every price?” — if yes, it shifted right.
  • Expectations split the market. An expected price rise shifts demand RIGHT (buyers rush in) but supply LEFT (sellers hold back) — the one shifter that pushes the two curves in opposite directions. A favourite trick question.
  • Substitutes in production are a supply story, not a demand story. If the question is about what the firm could make instead, it's the S in SPENT; if it's about what the buyer could buy instead, it's the R in TRIBE.
Worked Example — A Trade Dispute Redirects Supply
Canada and the United States have a long-running trade dispute over softwood lumber, with the US periodically imposing duties on Canadian lumber exports. When a new duty raises the cost of exporting to the US, Canadian producers redirect unsold volume toward the domestic Canadian market. Identify the effect on the domestic Canadian supply curve.
Event: New US duty raises the cost of exporting lumber to the US

Effect on DOMESTIC (Canadian) supply:
  Total Canadian production is roughly unchanged, but less of it
  is profitable to sell in the US, so more is redirected to
  domestic buyers at every domestic price.

Direction: Domestic supply curve shifts RIGHT
  More lumber is available to Canadian buyers at every price,
  even though nothing changed about Canadian production costs
  or technology — the shifter here is a change in the relative
  profitability of different markets for the same good.

Effect on the domestic market (preview of Section 6):
  → At the original equilibrium price, there is now a SURPLUS
  → This surplus pushes the domestic price DOWN
  → New domestic equilibrium: LOWER price, HIGHER quantity

This is why Canadian softwood lumber duties are a recurring
subject of trade negotiations — they change quantities and
prices in both the US and Canadian domestic markets.
Substitutes-in-Production (Supply Side) A "substitute in production" is a different good that uses the same inputs or production facilities. If the price of engineered wood products (like OSB panels) rises relative to raw lumber, mills may shift capacity toward panels. This means raw lumber supply shifts LEFT — even though the lumber price did not change. This is the supply-side equivalent of a substitute, and it often surprises students who confuse it with demand-side substitutes.
Section 6

Market Equilibrium: Where Supply Meets Demand

Market equilibrium is the price and quantity at which the quantity demanded exactly equals the quantity supplied. At equilibrium there is no shortage (no unsatisfied buyers) and no surplus (no unsold inventory). The market clears — every willing seller finds a buyer and every willing buyer finds a seller.

Markets are self-correcting: if price is above equilibrium, a surplus develops and sellers cut prices until equilibrium is restored. If price is below equilibrium, a shortage develops and sellers raise prices (or buyers bid prices up) until equilibrium is restored. This is why competitive markets tend to hover around equilibrium even without central coordination — the same price-mechanism logic from Chapter 2.

Worked Example — Finding Equilibrium Algebraically
The demand and supply for lumber are given by the equations below. Find the equilibrium price and quantity. Then find the shortage or surplus at a price of $8.
Given:
  Quantity demanded: Qd = 200 − 10P
  Quantity supplied: Qs = 10P − 40

Step 1 — Set Qd = Qs (equilibrium condition):
  200 − 10P = 10P − 40
  240 = 20P
  P* = $12

Step 2 — Substitute P* back to find Q*:
  Q* = 200 − 10(12) = 200 − 120 = 80 thousand units
  Check: Qs = 10(12) − 40 = 120 − 40 = 80 ✓

Equilibrium: P* = $12, Q* = 80 thousand units per week

Step 3 — Shortage/surplus at P = $8:
  Qd = 200 − 10(8) = 120 thousand units (demanded)
  Qs = 10(8) − 40 = 40 thousand units (supplied)
  Shortage = Qd − Qs = 120 − 40 = 80 thousand units

Interpretation: At $8 there is a SHORTAGE of 80 thousand units.
Buyers want more than mills are willing to supply. This shortage
creates upward pressure on price until it reaches $12.
Graphical vs. Algebraic Equilibrium On tests you will often need both methods. For graphs: draw both curves, find the intersection, drop dashed lines to both axes, and read off P* and Q*. For algebra: set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then substitute to find Q. Always verify by checking that Qd = Qs at your answer.
Section 7

Predicting Equilibrium Changes

Once you can identify whether a demand or supply shift occurred and which direction it moved, you can predict what happens to equilibrium price and quantity using four rules:

  • Demand shifts right (demand increases): at the old price there is now a shortage → price rises → P* rises, Q* rises.
  • Demand shifts left (demand decreases): at the old price there is now a surplus → price falls → P* falls, Q* falls.
  • Supply shifts right (supply increases): at the old price there is now a surplus → price falls → P* falls, Q* rises.
  • Supply shifts left (supply decreases): at the old price there is now a shortage → price rises → P* rises, Q* falls.

When both curves shift simultaneously, one of the two outcomes (price or quantity) is unambiguous while the other depends on the relative magnitudes of the shifts — a result that is indeterminate without more information.

Worked Example — The 2021 Lumber Price Spike
In February 2020, lumber traded at roughly $400 per thousand board feet. By May 2021, prices had spiked to over $1,600 — about four times higher — before falling back sharply later that year.1 Use supply and demand to explain this spike.
DEMAND side:
  Pandemic lockdowns pushed households into renovation projects
  (decks, home offices) instead of travel, while record-low
  interest rates made borrowing to renovate or build cheaper.
  → Demand shifted sharply RIGHT

SUPPLY side:
  Sawmills had cut production and furloughed workers early in
  the pandemic, anticipating a recession. When demand rebounded
  faster than expected, mills couldn't restart capacity quickly;
  labour shortages and transportation bottlenecks compounded
  the problem.
  → Supply shifted sharply LEFT

Combined effect (both rules from this section apply at once):
  Demand RIGHT + Supply LEFT → Price rises UNAMBIGUOUSLY
    (both shifts push price up — this matches the roughly
    4x price increase observed)
  Quantity effect → AMBIGUOUS in theory, but here demand's
    rightward shift was large enough that quantity built also
    rose before supply caught up later in 2021

Resolution: As mills ramped capacity back up through 2021
(supply shifting back right) and pandemic renovation demand
cooled, prices fell sharply back toward pre-pandemic levels —
exactly what the model predicts once the shifts reverse.
Section 8

Normal vs. Inferior Goods and Substitutes vs. Complements

Two classification frameworks appear repeatedly on exams. You need to apply them correctly — not just recite definitions but use them to predict which way demand shifts.

Normal vs. Inferior Goods

A normal good is one where demand increases when income rises (and falls when income falls). Most goods are normal: new construction, renovations, quality building materials. An inferior good is one where demand decreases when income rises. Consumers switch to better alternatives when they can afford to: lower-grade materials, older housing stock, generic-brand products.

"Inferior" is not a quality judgment — it just means the good has negative income elasticity. A basic starter home is perfectly functional housing; it becomes relatively less demanded once rising incomes let households afford larger or newer homes.

Substitutes vs. Complements

Substitutes are goods that satisfy the same need and can replace each other: lumber-framed vs. steel-framed construction, new lumber vs. engineered wood products. If the price of steel framing rises, demand for lumber framing increases (shift right). Complements are goods consumed together: lumber and drywall, lumber and nails/fasteners, a new home and the appliances that go inside it. If drywall prices rise sharply, builders complete fewer projects — and demand less lumber too (complement demand falls, shift left).

Worked Example — Pre-Fabricated Housing: Normal or Inferior Good?
Is pre-fabricated (modular) housing a normal good or an inferior good? How can the same product be both?
Answer: It depends on the local context and consumer income reference point.

In some rural or lower-income markets:
  → Modular housing is historically CHEAP relative to custom-built
  → As local incomes rise, buyers switch to custom-built homes
  → In this context: INFERIOR GOOD (demand falls with income ↑)

In many urban markets facing high construction costs and labour
shortages:
  → Modular housing is increasingly seen as an efficient,
    higher-tech alternative, sometimes at a PREMIUM
  → As incomes rise, more buyers can afford new construction
    generally, including modular options
  → In this context: NORMAL GOOD (demand rises with income ↑)

Key insight: Whether a good is normal or inferior depends on
consumers' income level and market context, not on the inherent
properties of the product.
Common Classification Errors Do not assume "expensive = normal good" or "cheap = inferior good." Classify based on the income effect only: does demand rise or fall when income increases? Also: substitutes and complements are demand-side concepts — they explain demand shifts, not supply shifts. A "substitute in production" (supply-side) is a completely different concept (Section 5).

Section 9

Price Ceilings and Price Floors

So far, prices have been free to adjust until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Governments sometimes override this process by legally fixing a price — with predictable consequences.

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. It only has an effect ("binds") if set below the equilibrium price — a ceiling above equilibrium is irrelevant, since the market would never reach that price anyway. A binding price ceiling creates a persistent shortage: at the capped price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, but price cannot rise to close the gap. Rent control in Ontario and other Canadian cities is the classic example — it caps rent increases on existing tenancies below what market conditions would otherwise support, producing exactly this kind of persistent shortage of available rental units.

A price floor is a legal minimum price. It only binds if set above the equilibrium price. A binding price floor creates a persistent surplus: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, but price cannot fall to close the gap. The minimum wage is the classic example — in the labour market, the "good" being bought and sold is labour, the "price" is the wage, workers are the suppliers, and firms are the buyers. A minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage creates a surplus of labour: more people want to work at that wage than firms want to hire. Economists call this surplus of labour unemployment — a concept you will study formally in Chapter 5.

Worked Example — Rent Control and Minimum Wage, Algebraically
(a) The market for rental apartments has Qd = 1000 − 20P and Qs = 20P − 200 (P in hundreds of dollars per month). A city imposes rent control at P = 20 (i.e., $2,000/month). Find the equilibrium and the resulting shortage. (b) The market for a certain labour category has Qd = 100 − 2W and Qs = 2W − 20 (W = wage in $/hour, Q = thousands of workers). A minimum wage of $40/hour is imposed. Find the equilibrium and the resulting surplus.
(a) Rent control:
  Equilibrium: 1000 − 20P = 20P − 200 → 1200 = 40P → P* = 30
  ($3,000/month), Q* = 1000 − 600 = 400 units

  At the rent-controlled price P = 20 ($2,000/month, below P*=30):
    Qd = 1000 − 400 = 600 units demanded
    Qs = 400 − 200 = 200 units supplied
    Shortage = 600 − 200 = 400 units

  The rent cap, set below equilibrium, is binding — it creates
  a persistent shortage of 400 rental units.

(b) Minimum wage:
  Equilibrium: 100 − 2W = 2W − 20 → 120 = 4W → W* = $30/hour,
  Q* = 100 − 60 = 40 thousand workers

  At the minimum wage W = 40 (above W*=30):
    Qd = 100 − 80 = 20 thousand workers demanded
    Qs = 80 − 20 = 60 thousand workers supplied
    Surplus = 60 − 20 = 40 thousand workers

  The minimum wage, set above equilibrium, is binding — it
  creates a surplus of 40 thousand workers: people willing to
  work at $40/hour who cannot find a job at that wage. This is
  unemployment caused by a price floor in the labour market.
Only Binding Ceilings and Floors Matter A price ceiling set ABOVE equilibrium has no effect — the market settles at equilibrium anyway, below the ceiling. A price floor set BELOW equilibrium has no effect for the same reason. Always check whether the ceiling/floor is actually binding (ceiling below P*, floor above P*) before concluding there's a shortage or surplus.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Demand Schedule to Curve

A demand schedule for electric scooters shows: $5→300 units, $4→400, $3→500, $2→600, $1→700. (a) Does this data obey the Law of Demand? (b) Write a linear demand equation in the form Qd = a − bP. (c) At what price is quantity demanded zero?

Show answer
(a) Yes — quantity rises as price falls (inverse relationship). ✓

(b) Each $1 price drop → 100 more units demanded, so b = 100. At P=$1, Qd=700: 700 = a − 100(1) → a = 800.
Qd = 800 − 100P

(c) Set Qd = 0: 0 = 800 − 100P → P = $8.00. Quantity demanded falls to zero at a price of $8.
Problem 2 — Demand Shifters

For each event, state whether it causes a movement along the lumber demand curve or a shift, and if a shift, which direction (left/right) and why:
(a) The price of lumber rises from $12 to $16.
(b) A wave of new household formation increases the number of first-time homebuyers.
(c) The price of steel framing (a substitute in construction) falls sharply.
(d) Mortgage rates rise sharply, reducing what buyers can afford (lumber is a normal good).

Show answer
(a) Movement along the curve (leftward). Price of THIS good changed → Qd falls. No shift.
(b) Shift RIGHT. Population/number-of-buyers increase — more households demanding new construction at every price.
(c) Shift LEFT. Steel is a substitute for lumber framing. A falling substitute price pulls some demand away from lumber toward steel.
(d) Shift LEFT. Effectively lower purchasing power, and lumber (via new construction) is a normal good → demand decreases at every price.
Problem 3 — Supply Shifters

For each event, state whether the supply of lumber shifts left or right:
(a) A mountain pine beetle infestation destroys standing timber across large areas of BC forest.
(b) A new automated milling technology is introduced industry-wide.
(c) A new US duty makes exporting to the US less profitable, redirecting volume to the domestic market.
(d) Mills expect lumber prices to rise significantly next quarter.

Show answer
(a) Shift LEFT — input (usable timber) cost rises → production costlier → less supplied at every price.
(b) Shift RIGHT — technological improvement → lower production cost → more supplied at every price.
(c) Shift RIGHT (domestically) — less profitable to export means more product redirected to the domestic market at every domestic price.
(d) Shift LEFT — mills hold inventory off the market now to sell at a higher price later → less supplied today.
Problem 4 — Algebraic Equilibrium

Market demand: Qd = 150 − 5P. Market supply: Qs = 5P − 50. (a) Find the equilibrium price and quantity. (b) Calculate the shortage or surplus at a price of $15. (c) If demand increases to Qd = 190 − 5P, find the new equilibrium.

Show answer
(a) Set Qd = Qs: 150 − 5P = 5P − 50 → 200 = 10P → P* = $20; Q* = 150 − 5(20) = 50 units. Check: Qs = 5(20)−50 = 50 ✓

(b) At P=$15: Qd = 150−75 = 75; Qs = 75−50 = 25. Shortage = 75 − 25 = 50 units. Price is below equilibrium → shortage → price will rise toward $20.

(c) New: 190 − 5P = 5P − 50 → 240 = 10P → P* = $24; Q* = 190 − 5(24) = 70 units. Demand increase → higher equilibrium price and quantity. ✓
Problem 5 — Economics Application: The 2021 Lumber Price Spike, Revisited

Recall from Section 7 that lumber prices rose roughly fourfold between February 2020 and May 2021, then fell sharply later that year.1 (a) Using the four rules from Section 7, explain why the combined demand-right/supply-left shift made the price increase unambiguous even though the quantity effect was theoretically ambiguous. (b) If a government had imposed a price ceiling on lumber at the pre-pandemic price of roughly $400/thousand board feet during the 2021 spike, what would you predict, using Section 9's framework? (c) Explain, using Section 5, why the eventual price collapse later in 2021 is consistent with the supply-and-demand model rather than contradicting it.

Show answer
(a) A rightward demand shift alone raises both price and quantity. A leftward supply shift alone raises price but lowers quantity. When both happen together, the price effects reinforce each other (both push price up), so the price increase is unambiguous — matching the observed ~4x spike. The quantity effects point in opposite directions (demand pulls quantity up, supply pulls it down), so in principle the net quantity change could go either way; which one dominates depends on the relative size of the two shifts.

(b) A ceiling at the old $400 price, held during a period when the equilibrium price had risen to over $1,600, would be a binding price ceiling (set far below the new equilibrium). Applying Section 9's model: quantity demanded at $400 would vastly exceed quantity supplied at $400, producing a severe and persistent shortage — likely worse than the high-price outcome that actually occurred, since builders and renovators would be unable to obtain lumber at any legal price, potentially pushing activity into unofficial/grey markets.

(c) The supply-and-demand model doesn't predict permanently high prices — it predicts that prices reflect whichever shifts are currently in effect. As mills ramped up capacity in 2021 (supply shifting back to the right, a Section 5 shifter: more capacity brought online) and pandemic-driven renovation demand cooled as life normalized (demand shifting back left), the model predicts exactly what happened: price fell substantially. The 2021 episode is a real-world illustration of Section 7's rules operating in sequence, not a violation of them.

Demand ShiftersTRIBE: Tastes, Related-good prices, Income, Buyers (population & demographics), ExpectationsSubstitutes Same / Complements Contrary; Normal = normal direction / Inferior = inverse; expect a rise → buy now
Law of SupplyPrice ↑ → Qs ↑; Price ↓ → Qs ↓ (ceteris paribus)Supply slopes upward — opposite of demand
Supply ShiftersSPENT: Substitutes in production, Prices of inputs, Expected future prices, Number of firms, TechnologyCost test: cheaper / more profitable to produce → RIGHT; costlier / something else more profitable → LEFT
Market EquilibriumPrice where Qd = Qs; no shortage or surplusFind algebraically (set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then Q); verify both equations give same Q
ShortageQd > Qs; price below equilibrium → price risesPrice below P* → shortage → upward pressure
SurplusQs > Qd; price above equilibrium → price fallsPrice above P* → surplus → downward pressure
Normal vs. Inferior GoodNormal: demand rises with income. Inferior: demand falls with income.Income ↑ + normal good → demand RIGHT; inferior good → demand LEFT
Substitutes / ComplementsSubstitutes: same use. Complements: used together.Substitute price ↑ → this good's demand RIGHT; Complement price ↑ → demand LEFT
Price CeilingLegal maximum price; binds only if below equilibriumBinding ceiling → persistent shortage (e.g., rent control)
Price FloorLegal minimum price; binds only if above equilibriumBinding floor → persistent surplus (e.g., minimum wage → unemployment)
