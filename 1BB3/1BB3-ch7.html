Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Compare your daily life with that of earlier generations: smartphones, instant communication with almost anyone, modern medicine and much longer life expectancy. Now compare it with the situation in many low-income countries, where poverty is still persistent. The slides' explanation is short: these differences exist because some countries have had sustained economic growth and others have not. This chapter is about what that growth is, where it comes from, and why it is so uneven.

Think of the last few chapters as measuring the economy. Chapter 4 measured GDP, Chapter 5 covered unemployment and inflation, and Chapter 6 introduced business cycles, the short-run ups and downs. Chapter 7 zooms out to decades and centuries and looks at the long-run trend of real GDP per person. Chapters 8 and 9 then return to the short run.

Chapter Roadmap

This chapter covers: how to measure growth (growth rates, average annual growth, the Rule of 70, potential GDP); growth over history and across countries; the Solow–Swan model and the three determinants of labour productivity; new growth theory; Canada's productivity record; catch-up and why it often fails; and the policies that promote growth.

Section 2

Measuring Growth: Two Definitions, Growth Rates and the Rule of 70

The slides begin by separating two meanings of growth and ask you to use them exactly as written, to avoid confusion.

  • Economic growth (textbook Chapter 4): the ability of an economy to produce increasing quantities of goods and services. This is the standard definition.
  • Long-run economic growth (textbook Chapter 6): the process by which rising productivity increases the average standard of living. The slides flag this as a non-standard definition.
Vocabulary Trap — 'economic growth' vs 'long-run economic growth'

The first is about the quantity of output rising. The second is specifically about productivity raising living standards, which means output per person. An economy can have economic growth (more total output, for example because it has more workers) without long-run economic growth in this sense (output per person is not rising).

Self-test: population grows 2% and real GDP grows 2%. Economic growth? Yes. Long-run economic growth (rising living standards)? No, because real GDP per capita is flat.

Growth Rates and Average Growth

The growth rate of real GDP (or of real GDP per capita) is the percentage change from one year to the next:

Growth rate = (this year − last year) ÷ last year × 100

To find an average annual growth rate over several years, the textbook simply averages the yearly rates: yearly rates of 2.0%, 3.0% and 1.0% average to 2.0%. Over very long spans the compound formula in Section 3 is more exact, but for a textbook-style question use the simple average.

The Rule of 70

Growth compounds, so the useful way to feel a growth rate is as a doubling time. The Rule of 70 says that the number of years for a variable to double is approximately 70 divided by its annual growth rate in percent. It is an approximation that works well for modest growth rates.

Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate (%)

The slides' own example: at 1.8% a year, living standards double roughly every 40 years (70 ÷ 1.8 ≈ 39).

What Long-Run Looks Like in Canada

The slides' Figure 6.1 (Statistics Canada data) shows Canada's real GDP per capita rising from roughly $20,000 in 1961 to roughly $59,000 by 2024, about triple. The line dips in recessions (most visibly 1981–82, 1990–91, 2008–09 and 2020), yet the trend rises. That is what long-run means: look through the business-cycle dips at the trend. (The dollar values are read off the slide chart, so treat them as rough.)

Potential GDP

Potential GDP is the level of real GDP attained when all firms are producing at capacity. Capacity means a normal workweek with a normal-sized workforce, not the absolute maximum. Potential GDP grows over time as the labour force, the capital stock and technology grow, while actual real GDP fluctuates around it: below it in recessions, and possibly above it in booms. It returns in Chapter 9.

Worked Example A — Growth Rates and Doubling Time
Real GDP per capita in a country is $50,000 in Year 1, $51,000 in Year 2, $51,510 in Year 3 and about $52,540 in Year 4. (a) Find each yearly growth rate and the average annual growth rate. (b) Use the Rule of 70 to estimate how long living standards take to double at that pace. (c) Compare with Canada's 0.3% pace in 2006–2020 and its 2.7% pace in 1950–1973 (Section 7).
(a) Yearly growth rates: (this year − last year) / last year
  Year 1 to 2:  (51,000 − 50,000) / 50,000 = 1,000 / 50,000 = 2.0%
  Year 2 to 3:  (51,510 − 51,000) / 51,000 =   510 / 51,000 = 1.0%
  Year 3 to 4:  (52,540 − 51,510) / 51,510 = 1,030 / 51,510 = 2.0%
                (51,510 × 1.02 = 52,540.2, so about 2.0%)

  Average annual growth = (2.0 + 1.0 + 2.0) / 3 = 5.0 / 3 = 1.67%

(b) Rule of 70: 70 / 1.67 = 41.9, so living standards double in
    about 42 years.

(c) Same rule at Canada's two paces:
    0.3% a year (2006-2020):  70 / 0.3 = 233 years to double
    2.7% a year (1950-1973):  70 / 2.7 = 25.9, about 26 years

A difference of 2.4 percentage points a year turns a 26-year doubling
into a 233-year one.
Section 3

Growth Over Time and Around the World

This section covers the slides' learning objectives 7.1 (global trends in growth) and 7.2 (why small differences in growth rates matter). For most of history there was essentially no sustained growth in living standards. The slides' Figure 7.1 (Maddison data) shows the world's average annual growth of real GDP per capita by era:

  • 1 CE to 1000: 0%
  • 1000 to 1820: 0.05%
  • 1820 to 1870: 0.62%
  • 1870 to 1950: 1.01%
  • 1950 to 2000: 2.20%
  • 2000 to 2021: 1.61%

The break point is the Industrial Revolution, which began in England in the second half of the 1700s. Use the Rule of 70 to make the contrast vivid: at 0.05% a year, living standards would double only every 1,400 years (70 ÷ 0.05); at 2.2% a year they double about every 32 years.

Economists group countries by income. High-income (industrial or developed) countries include Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, the United States and Western Europe. Lower-income (developing) countries are mostly in Africa, Asia and Latin America. A third group, the newly industrializing countries such as Singapore and South Korea, grew rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s. The slides' Figure 7.2 (World Bank) compares 2022 GDP per capita in US dollars adjusted for cost-of-living differences (PPP), and the gaps between these groups are huge.

Small Differences, Large Consequences

Why do growth rates of 0.4% versus 1.9% matter so much? Because growth compounds. If a variable starts at a value and grows at rate g per year, then:

Value after t years = starting value × (1 + g)^t

The slides use a two-country illustration. Countries G and M both have real GDP per capita of $5,000 in 1960. G grows 0.4% a year and M grows 1.9% a year. By 2025, G is at about $6,500 and M at about $17,000. The real-world version is Table 7.1, in 2017 US dollars:

CountryReal GDP per capita, 1960Real GDP per capita, 2019Average annual growth
Ghana$4,409$5,5470.4%
Mexico$6,472$19,3081.9%
Turkey$5,051$26,7002.7%

Notice that Turkey started behind Mexico in 1960 and ended well ahead of it, purely because it grew faster for decades. (The figures are as printed on the slide. The growth rates are rounded averages, so compounding them yourself lands near the 2019 values but not exactly on them, especially for Turkey.)

Worked Example B — Compounding: Table 7.1's Countries G and M
Countries G and M each start at $5,000 real GDP per capita in 1960. G grows 0.4% a year and M grows 1.9% a year. What is each country's real GDP per capita after 65 years (2025), and how much richer is M than G?
Country G (g = 0.4% = 0.004):
  5,000 × (1.004)^65 = 5,000 × 1.296 ≈ $6,481   → about $6,500

Country M (g = 1.9% = 0.019):
  5,000 × (1.019)^65 = 5,000 × 3.399 ≈ $16,995  → about $17,000

Gap: 17,000 / 6,500 ≈ 2.6, so M ends up about 2.6 times richer,
from a growth gap of only 1.5 percentage points a year.

Rule-of-70 cross-check:
  M: 70 / 1.9 ≈ 37 years per doubling
     65 / 37 ≈ 1.76 doublings, and 2^1.76 ≈ 3.4 times richer ✓
  G: 70 / 0.4 = 175 years per doubling
     so after 65 years G has not even completed half a doubling
Why Economists Care About a Single Percentage Point Growth compounds, so a one-point difference in the annual rate, sustained over a working lifetime, is the difference between a country doubling its living standards once and doubling them several times. The economist Robert Lucas made the point famously: once you start thinking about growth, it is hard to think about anything else. That is why the slides spend so much effort on what moves the growth rate by even a little.

Section 4

The Solow–Swan Growth Model: Building Blocks

Economic growth theory studies the long-run growth of real output and real output per capita, and what drives it. The slides' key claim (learning objective 7.3) is that increases in real GDP per capita depend on increases in labour productivity: the quantity of goods and services that one worker, or one hour of work, can produce. In the model, labour productivity is output divided by labour, Y/L.

The model was proposed independently in 1956 by Robert Solow of MIT (Nobel Prize, 1987) and by the Australian economist Trevor Swan, which is why it carries both names. It has three building blocks.

  1. Factors of production. Labour (L) is total hours worked. Physical capital (K) is manufactured goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, factories, trucks. Real economies also use natural resources and human capital, but the basic model keeps only K and L.
  2. The production function. It maps capital (K), labour (L) and the level of technology (A) into output (Y). Write it as Y = A × F(K, L). In words: output depends on technology, capital and labour. You do not need a formula with exponents.
  3. Capital accumulation. In a closed economy, total saving equals investment in physical capital (S = I). Investment adds to the capital stock, and depreciation (wear and tear) subtracts from it: Change in K = Investment − Depreciation. When investment equals depreciation, the capital stock stays constant.
Worked Example C — Capital Accumulation
A closed economy invests $1,000,000 in a year, and $100,000 of its capital wears out. What happens to the capital stock? Then repeat for (b) investment of $600,000 with depreciation of $600,000, and (c) investment of $400,000 with depreciation of $500,000.
Change in K = Investment − Depreciation

(a) 1,000,000 − 100,000 = +$900,000   → capital stock rises by $900,000
(b)   600,000 − 600,000 =         $0  → capital stock is constant
(c)   400,000 − 500,000 = −$100,000   → capital stock shrinks

Part (a) is the slides' own example. Part (b) shows that new
investment can be large and still produce no growth in capital:
what matters is investment compared with depreciation.
Stock vs Flow K is a stock: an amount at a point in time. Investment and depreciation are flows: amounts per year. This is the same distinction you met in Chapter 4, where GDP is a flow. The flows change the stock; they are not the stock.
Section 5

What Drives Labour Productivity: Tools, Tech, Training

The slides identify three determinants of labour productivity (Y/L). The key skill is knowing whether each one is a movement along the per-worker production function or a shift of the whole function.

1. Physical Capital per Hour Worked (K/L) — Tools

The capital-labour ratio is the quantity of physical capital per hour worked. Workers are more productive when they have more machines and tools to work with. Raising K/L is a movement along the per-worker production function.

But there are diminishing returns: the first units of capital are the most effective, and each further equal increase in K/L (holding other factors constant) raises Y/L by smaller and smaller amounts.

Slide Figure 7.3: K/L of $75, $100, $125 and $150 gives Y/L of $48, $53, $57 and $60. Each $25 step in capital per hour adds less output: +$5, then +$4, then +$3.

2. The Level of Technology (A) — Tech

Technology is the processes a firm uses to turn inputs into outputs. Technological change is an increase in the quantity of output firms can produce using a given quantity of inputs. It shifts the per-worker production function up. Long-run growth in living standards requires continuous technological progress.

Slide Figure 7.4: at K/L = $150, Y/L rises from $60 to $65, $70 and $75 as the function shifts from Production function 1 to 4.

Economists distinguish two kinds. Disembodied technological change is not tied to new equipment: for example, a manager rearranges a factory floor or a retail store layout to raise production and sales. Embodied technological change is built into new machinery, equipment or software. The slides say most technological change is embodied.

3. Human Capital — Training

Human capital is the accumulated knowledge and skills workers acquire from education, training or life experience. Like technological progress, more human capital shifts the per-worker production function up.

Worked Example D — Diminishing Returns on the Per-Worker Production Function
Use the slide numbers below. A firm is at K/L = $150. It can (i) add another $25 of capital per hour, or (ii) adopt a new process that shifts the function up to Production function 2, where output per hour at K/L = $150 is $65 instead of $60. Which choice raises output per hour more?
K/L (capital per hour)   Y/L (output per hour)   Change in Y/L
$75                      $48                     --
$100                     $53                     +$5
$125                     $57                     +$4
$150                     $60                     +$3

Equal $25 steps in K/L, but the gains shrink: +5, +4, +3.

Option (i): move ALONG the curve to K/L = $175.
  The gain will be less than +$3, because the pattern keeps falling.
Option (ii): SHIFT the curve up to Production function 2.
  At the same K/L = $150, Y/L rises from $60 to $65, a gain of +$5.

The shift (+$5) beats the move along the curve (less than +$3).
Memory Aid — the Three T's: Tools, Tech, Training
  • Tools = physical capital per hour worked (K/L) → move along the curve, with diminishing returns.
  • Tech = the level of technology (A) → shift the curve up.
  • Training = human capital → shift the curve up.

Direction shortcut: Tools slide along, Tech and Training lift up.

Common MC trap: a choice saying that an increase in capital per hour worked shifts the per-worker production function up is FALSE. More capital per hour is a movement along the function. Only technology and human capital shift it.

Self-test: cover the answers and say along or up? for each. New robots on the factory floor (along). A better inventory system that uses the same equipment (up, disembodied technology). A coding bootcamp for current employees (up, human capital). Faster new laptops (up, embodied technology, because the improvement is built into the machine; the extra quantity of laptops is a move along).

Vocabulary Trap — embodied vs disembodied technological change Embodied change comes inside new machines, equipment or software. Disembodied change is a better organization or process using the same equipment. The slides say most technological change is embodied, so if a question describes a new machine or program, think embodied.

Real-World Application: The Bank of Canada on Productivity

In a March 26, 2024 speech in Halifax, Bank of Canada Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers framed Canada's weak productivity as a problem serious enough that "it's time to break the glass." She named three ingredients of labour productivity. Capital intensity means equipping workers with better tools; in her snow-clearing example the progression runs from shovels to a snow blower to pickup trucks with plows. Labour composition is workers' skills and training. Multifactor productivity is how efficiently capital and labour are used, including competition, management and the adoption of new technology such as AI.

Those three line up with Tools, Training and Tech. She also noted that Canada produced 88% of US output per hour worked in 1984 but only 71% by 2022.

Section 6

The Model's Big Conclusion — and New Growth Theory

The Model's Bottom Line
  • Long-run growth in GDP per capita requires technological progress (or sustained growth in human capital).
  • Population growth and the accumulation of physical capital cannot, on their own, produce long-run growth in GDP per capita.

The reason is diminishing returns. Piling up capital moves the economy along an ever-flatter curve, until extra investment barely raises Y/L and mostly just replaces depreciated capital. Adding workers while the capital stock stays the same lowers K/L, so it pushes the economy back along the curve. Only an upward shift of the curve keeps Y/L rising year after year.

Worked Example E — Why Capital Alone Runs Out of Steam
A country builds more factories every year, raising capital per hour worked in $25 steps starting from K/L = $75. Using the Section 5 numbers, what happens to the gains, and what would a technology shift do instead?
Gain in Y/L from each extra $25 of K/L:
  $75 to $100:   48 to 53   = +5
  $100 to $125:  53 to 57   = +4
  $125 to $150:  57 to 60   = +3
  ...and the gains keep shrinking.

Technology shift at K/L = $150:
  Y/L rises from $60 to $65 (+5) with no further climb along the curve.

Conclusion: capital alone gives gains that fade away; sustained
growth in output per hour needs the curve itself to keep rising.

New Growth Theory

The Solow–Swan model has a shortcoming: it does not explain why technology (A) grows. Technology sits outside the model. New growth theory, associated with Paul Romer (Nobel Prize, 2018), tries to explain technological change from within the economy.

  • Knowledge capital is created by research and development (R&D). Once an idea exists, many firms can use it at the same time, and it is hard to keep others from using it (spillovers). Firms capture only part of the benefit of their R&D, so the market tends to under-invest in it.
  • Learning by doing: workers and firms become more productive as they gain experience producing something.

Because of the under-investment problem, new growth theory points to government policies such as patents and copyrights (which protect intellectual property), subsidies for R&D, and subsidies for education. Canada's federal Scientific Research and Experimental Development (SR&ED) program is an example of an R&D subsidy: it uses tax incentives, a deduction and an investment tax credit, to encourage businesses to do R&D in Canada. The Canada Revenue Agency's program page has the current details.

Section 7

Productivity Growth in Canada

This section covers learning objective 7.4. The slides' Figure 7.5 (Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database) shows the average annual growth of real GDP per person in Canada across six eras: 1.8% for 1870–1900, 1.9% for 1900–1949, 2.7% for 1950–1973, 1.5% for 1974–1995, 2.4% for 1996–2005 and 0.3% for 2006–2020.

The slides tell the story of each era:

  • 1870–1900 (Industrial Revolution era): capital accumulation and new technologies delivered about 1.8% a year. At that pace living standards double roughly every 40 years: steady, but slow.
  • The 20th century: technological change became institutionalized through corporate R&D, university research and government support, which accelerated growth (1.9% a year over 1900–1949).
  • 1950–1973 (postwar boom): the fastest growth of any era shown, 2.7% a year. Continuing technological change let Canada avoid diminishing returns to capital.
  • 1974–1995 (long slowdown): growth averaged over one percentage point a year lower than in 1950–1973.
  • Mid-1990s to 2005 (IT-driven rebound): information technology and other innovations lifted growth back to 2.4%.
  • 2006–2020 (historically weak growth): about 0.3% a year, the lowest in 150 years.
Quick Calculation — The Rule of 70 Across Canada's Eras
Convert each era's average annual growth rate into a doubling time. What does the pattern say about 2006–2020?
Era          Growth   70 / growth         Doubling time
1870-1900    1.8%     70 / 1.8 = 38.9     about 39 years
1900-1949    1.9%     70 / 1.9 = 36.8     about 37 years
1950-1973    2.7%     70 / 2.7 = 25.9     about 26 years
1974-1995    1.5%     70 / 1.5 = 46.7     about 47 years
1996-2005    2.4%     70 / 2.4 = 29.2     about 29 years
2006-2020    0.3%     70 / 0.3 = 233.3    about 233 years

From the postwar boom to the long slowdown, growth fell by
2.7 − 1.5 = 1.2 percentage points a year. That stretched the doubling
time from 26 years to 47. At 2006-2020's pace it would be over two
centuries.

Is Canada Headed for a Period of Slow Growth?

The slides present two camps (slides 23–24).

The Optimists

Productivity may be under-measured. Services and technology are hard to measure: the convenience of ATMs, free apps and other consumer benefits do not show up fully in GDP. New advances such as AI and semiconductors could also boost productivity after an adjustment period. This is the productivity J-curve: productivity can dip while firms reorganize around a new technology, and then rise.

The Pessimists (Skeptics)

The easy gains from the IT revolution happened in the 1990s. Newer technology mostly improves the consumer experience rather than labour productivity. Aging populations and regulation will keep growth low.

Whichever camp is right, Rogers' figures from Section 5 show how far Canada has slipped: its output per hour worked fell from 88% of the US level in 1984 to 71% in 2022.

Exam Trap — measured vs actual A statement that GDP growth understates improvements in living standards is describing the optimists' measurement argument. It is not claiming that productivity is truly falling. Read which camp each statement belongs to before choosing.
Section 8

Catch-Up: Theory and Evidence

This section covers learning objective 7.5. The model makes a clear prediction, set out on slides 25–26. Developing countries have lower capital per hour (K/L) and lower technology (A) than developed countries. Because of diminishing returns, an extra dollar of capital per hour raises output much more where K/L is low, on the steep part of the curve. Poor countries can also adopt technology that already exists rather than inventing it.

So poorer countries should grow faster than richer ones, and the gaps in GDP per capita should narrow. This is catch-up (also called convergence). If you plot each country's growth rate against its initial real GDP per capita, the model predicts a downward-sloping line, the catch-up line.

Evidence 1: Among High-Income Countries, Catch-Up Holds

The slides' Figure 7.7 (1960–2019) shows that the countries that were poorer in 1960, such as South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong, grew fastest. The richest in 1960, such as Switzerland, New Zealand and the United States, grew more slowly. Canada sits close to the catch-up line, with about 2% growth from roughly $15,000 in 1960.

Evidence 2: Across the Whole World, It Mostly Does Not

Figure 7.8 (1960–2017) includes all countries, and there is no clear downward pattern. Some poor countries grew very fast, with Botswana, Singapore, China and Thailand at roughly 4–6% a year. Others stagnated or shrank: Niger and the Democratic Republic of the Congo had negative average growth.

Evidence 3: Other Rich Countries Stopped Catching Up to the US

Figure 7.9 (OECD data) shows real GDP per capita relative to the United States. Between 1990 and 2022 it fell for the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Japan and Canada. Canada went from roughly 87% to 73% of the US level, and Japan from roughly 90% to 67%.

The slides (slide 30) give two reasons for the US advantage:

  • Flexible labour markets: hiring and firing is relatively unrestricted by regulation, and US workers tend to enter the workforce sooner and retire later than workers in Europe.
  • An efficient financial system: high trading volume means high liquidity, which makes the US an attractive place to invest. Small firms find it relatively easy to raise capital, helped by venture capital firms.
Worked Example F — Reading the Catch-Up Line
Country P has real GDP per capita of $4,000 and Country R has $40,000. (a) What does the Solow–Swan model predict about their growth rates, and why? (b) Suppose P grows 5% a year and R grows 1.5% a year. Use the Rule of 70 and then compounding to compare them after 47 years.
(a) Prediction: P grows faster than R.
    P has low K/L, so it is on the steep part of the curve and each
    extra dollar of capital per hour adds a lot (diminishing returns).
    P can also borrow technology that already exists. R has high K/L,
    is on the flat part of the curve, and must create new technology.
    The catch-up line slopes down, so the gap should narrow.

(b) Rule-of-70 doubling times:
      P: 70 / 5   = 14 years
      R: 70 / 1.5 = about 47 years

    After 47 years:
      P: 47 / 14 = about 3.4 doublings, roughly 10 times richer
         4,000 × 1.05^47 ≈ 4,000 × 9.91 ≈ $39,600
      R: about one doubling
         40,000 × 1.015^47 ≈ 40,000 × 2.013 ≈ $80,500

    The gap: 40,000 / 4,000 = 10 to 1 at the start, but
             80,500 / 39,600 = about 2 to 1 after 47 years.

But the evidence (Evidence 2) shows that many poor countries do NOT
grow faster. The next section explains why.

Section 9

Why Many Low-Income Countries Grow Slowly

The slides (slide 31) name four key factors. Their first letters spell SLEW, as in a slew of obstacles.

  • S — Saving and investment are low. Underdeveloped financial systems trap countries in a vicious cycle: low income leads to low saving, which leads to low investment, which keeps income low.
  • L — Law is weak. A weak rule of law, meaning insecure property rights and poorly enforced contracts, discourages entrepreneurship and investment.
  • E — Education and health are poor. Lower worker productivity limits growth.
  • W — Wars and revolutions. Political instability disrupts investment and technological progress. When peace comes, growth can resume.

Escaping the Low-Saving Trap: Foreign Investment

A poor country does not have to rely only on its own saving. The slides (slide 32) describe two forms of foreign investment:

  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm builds or purchases a facility in a foreign country.
  • Foreign portfolio investment (FPI): an individual or firm buys stocks or bonds issued in another country.
Vocabulary Trap — FDI vs FPI

FDI means a firm owns or runs a facility abroad (a factory, mine or store), which brings capital and technology and management know-how. FPI means buying financial assets (stocks or bonds). It provides funds but no control.

Self-test: Toyota builds a plant in Thailand (FDI). A Canadian pension fund buys Indian government bonds (FPI). A Canadian buys 100 shares of a Brazilian mining company (FPI). Walmart buys a retail chain in Mexico (FDI).

Globalization

From the 1940s to the 1970s, many developing countries closed their economies with tariffs and restrictions on foreign investment, which limited their growth. Since the 1980s, globalization (greater openness to trade and investment) has led to faster growth by improving access to technology and capital. The slides add that anti-globalization policies, such as trade wars and broad tariffs, create uncertainty, discourage investment and can harm developing countries' growth prospects.

Memory Aid — SLEW of Obstacles

Saving and investment are low · Law is weak · Education and health are poor · Wars and revolutions.

Direction rule: each letter is a brake on growth, and the vicious cycle is the chain to remember: low income → low saving → low investment → low income. Fix any link (say, with foreign investment) and the cycle can start running the other way.

Common MC trap: an answer choice saying a poor country is poor only because it has too little capital is half-right at best. The model says capital alone cannot sustain growth, and weak institutions (the L in SLEW) are a main reason capital does not arrive in the first place.

Self-test: a country has independent courts and high school enrolment, but almost no saving and a very weak banking sector. Which letter is the problem? (S.) A civil war halts all factory construction. (W.)

Section 10

Pro-Growth Policies

This section covers learning objective 7.6. The slides (slide 33) list four policy areas that spell LETS (as in LETS grow).

  • L — Law. Strengthen property rights and the rule of law, with independent courts and less corruption. This encourages investment.
  • E — Education and health. Improving them boosts productivity and creates spillover benefits (an educated worker makes co-workers more productive too). It also helps reduce brain drain.
  • T — Technology. Promote technological change. Access to new technology, often through foreign direct investment, is crucial for growth.
  • S — Saving and investment. Encourage them by eliminating corruption and using tax incentives, such as tax-deferred savings and investment tax credits. Canadian examples of tax-advantaged saving are RRSPs (tax-deferred) and TFSAs. On the investment side, SR&ED from Section 6 is an example.

Notice that the policy list is largely the obstacle list flipped. The table lines them up.

Memory Aid — SLEW in, LETS Grow Out

Law · Education and health · Technology · Saving and investment.

Direction rule: diagnose with SLEW, then flip each obstacle into its policy: weak Law becomes stronger Law, poor Education and health becomes better schools and clinics, low Saving becomes saving incentives. Technology is the extra letter, because it is what the model says sustains growth.

Common MC trap: there is no W in LETS. Wars and revolutions are an obstacle with no slide policy, so do not pick reduce wars as one of the four. Also, a policy that only adds capital (the S) is not enough on its own, because of diminishing returns; look for the T.

Self-test: tax-deferred savings plans (S). Independent courts that enforce contracts (L). A national school-lunch program that improves children's health (E). Inviting a foreign firm to build a plant and bring its methods (T, through FDI).

Worked Example G — Prescribing Growth Policy
A country has a high saving rate, but its courts rarely enforce contracts and foreign-owned factories are frequently expropriated. Its schools are good. Diagnose the problem, predict the consequence and prescribe a policy.
Diagnose (SLEW):
  Saving: fine (high).  Education: fine.  Law: the BINDING CONSTRAINT.
  Weak contract enforcement and expropriation make property insecure.

Predict:
  Foreign firms stay away, so FDI is low; with no FDI, little
  technology transfer; with little technology and weak investment
  incentives, catch-up stalls even though domestic saving is high.

Prescribe (LETS):
  Law first: independent courts, anti-corruption measures and
  protection against expropriation. Then FDI arrives, bringing
  Technology along with capital.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Growth Rates and the Rule of 70

Real GDP per capita is $40,000 in Year 1, $41,200 in Year 2, $41,612 in Year 3 and about $42,860 in Year 4. Find each yearly growth rate, the average annual growth rate, and the approximate doubling time.

Show answer
Year 1 to 2: (41,200 − 40,000) / 40,000 = 3.0%
Year 2 to 3: (41,612 − 41,200) / 41,200 = 1.0%
Year 3 to 4: (42,860 − 41,612) / 41,612 = 3.0% (check: 41,612 × 1.03 = 42,860.4)
Average = (3.0 + 1.0 + 3.0) / 3 = 2.33%
Rule of 70: 70 / 2.33 ≈ 30 years to double.
Problem 2 — Compounding

Two countries each start at $8,000 of real GDP per capita. Country A grows 1% a year and Country B grows 3% a year. Using the Rule of 70, how long does each take to double? Roughly how much is each worth after 70 years, and how do they compare?

Show answer
A: 70 / 1 = 70 years to double, so after 70 years it has doubled once: about $16,000 (exact: 8,000 × 1.01^70 ≈ $16,050).
B: 70 / 3 ≈ 23.3 years to double, so 70 years is about 3 doublings (8,000 × 2 × 2 × 2): about $64,000 (exact: 8,000 × 1.03^70 ≈ $63,300).
B ends up about 4 times richer than A, from a gap of just 2 percentage points a year.
Problem 3 — Along or Up?

For each event, say whether it is a movement along the per-worker production function or an upward shift, and name the determinant.
(a) A trucking firm buys 10 more trucks of the same model for the same drivers.
(b) A grocer redesigns its store layout and sells more per hour with the same staff and equipment.
(c) A hospital replaces its old MRI machines with a new model that scans twice as fast.
(d) Nurses complete an advanced certification.
(e) The population grows while the capital stock stays the same.

Show answer
(a) Along: more physical capital per hour worked (K/L), with diminishing returns.
(b) Up: disembodied technological change (a better process, same equipment).
(c) Up: embodied technological change. The shift comes from the better technology built into the machine.
(d) Up: human capital.
(e) Along, down the curve: more workers with the same capital means K/L falls, so Y/L falls.
Problem 4 — Capital Accumulation and Diminishing Returns

(a) Investment is $2.5 million and depreciation is $0.4 million. What is the change in the capital stock? (b) Investment is $0.4 million and depreciation is $0.4 million. (c) Capital per hour of $75, $100, $125 and $150 gives output per hour of $48, $53, $57 and $60. What is the extra output from each $25 step, and what does the pattern show?

Show answer
(a) 2.5 − 0.4 = +$2.1 million: the capital stock rises.
(b) 0.4 − 0.4 = $0: the capital stock is constant.
(c) 53 − 48 = +$5, 57 − 53 = +$4, 60 − 57 = +$3. Equal increases in capital per hour give smaller and smaller gains in output per hour: diminishing returns, which is a movement along the curve.
Problem 5 — Application: Northland's Growth Plan

Northland is a low-income country. Its real GDP per capita is $3,000 and it is growing at 0.5% a year. Its saving rate is very low, its judges are often overturned by politicians, foreign-owned firms are frequently expropriated, and only 60% of children complete primary school. The government proposes to borrow heavily and build state-owned factories, and to do nothing else.

(a) Using the Solow–Swan model, what happens to output per hour in the short run and the long run under this plan? (b) Use SLEW to diagnose which obstacles apply. (c) Use LETS to prescribe a better plan, and explain how FDI links L and T. (d) Suppose reforms raise growth from 0.5% to 3.5%. Compare the doubling times, and estimate real GDP per capita after 40 years at each rate.

Show answer
(a) The plan raises only physical capital per hour worked (K/L), a movement along the production function. Output per hour rises at first, but diminishing returns mean each extra dollar of capital adds less, and some of the new investment just replaces depreciated capital. The gains shrink and fade. Without technological progress or more human capital, there is no sustained growth. Weak property rights would also discourage private investment around the state factories.

(b) S: saving is very low. L: weak rule of law (judges overturned, expropriation). E: primary-school completion of 60% signals poor education. W: no war or revolution is mentioned, so it does not apply.

(c) Law first: independent courts, an end to expropriation and anti-corruption measures. That makes foreign firms willing to build facilities, so FDI links L and T: secure property rights attract FDI, and FDI brings new technology and management know-how as well as capital (which also helps with S). Then E: invest in schooling and health. Then S: tax-advantaged saving. The key is that Law and Technology make capital productive, instead of just piling it up.

(d) Doubling time at 0.5%: 70 / 0.5 = 140 years. At 3.5%: 70 / 3.5 = 20 years.
After 40 years: $3,000 × 1.005^40 ≈ $3,660 versus $3,000 × 1.035^40 ≈ $11,880. At 3.5% the country has doubled twice (40 / 20 = 2 doublings, about $12,000), while at 0.5% it has barely moved.
