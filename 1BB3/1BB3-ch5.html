Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

In 2024 the Canadian labour market was under strain. The unemployment rate rose from 5.7% in January to 6.5% in November. Employment was higher in November than in January. But jobs did not grow fast enough to absorb everyone who wanted one.

The same year, inflation fell from 2.9% in January to 1.9% in November. Prices were still rising, just more slowly.

Each headline number hides a lot of variety. Look at the November 2024 details:

Unemployment rate, by provinceNewfoundland and Labrador 10.2%Saskatchewan 5.1%

One number squeezes a varied economy into a single figure. This chapter shows how Statistics Canada builds the two big numbers, what they capture and what they miss.

Economist Arthur Okun, in the 1960s, added the two together as a rough scorecard. His misery index is the inflation rate plus the unemployment rate. Both matter. Even so, the long-run success of an economy is judged mainly by real GDP per person (Chapter 6 and Chapter 7).

Section 2

Measuring Unemployment: The Labour Force Survey

Statistics Canada (StatCan) releases last month’s unemployment rate on the first or second Friday of each month, at 7:00 a.m. A surprise moves how investors and firms see the economy.

The number comes from the Labour Force Survey (LFS). It is a monthly survey of about 56,000 households. It covers the working-age population: people aged 15 and over who are legally entitled to work in Canada.

Everyone in that group lands in exactly one of three categories:

Employed

Anyone with a job. That includes paid work, self-employment and unpaid work in a family business. It also includes people who normally work but were absent (illness, vacation, strike or lockout).

Unemployed

No job, available to work, and searched for work in the last four weeks. People on temporary layoff count. So do people who will start a job within four weeks.

Key test: the four-week job search.

Not in the Labour Force

Unable or unwilling to do paid work. This includes discouraged workers: people who want a job but gave up looking.

The labour force is employed plus unemployed. It is everyone working or actively looking.

Three Formulas

Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100

Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100

Employment–population ratio = employed ÷ working-age population × 100

Worked Example A — November 2024
Use the Figure 1 numbers to find the unemployment rate, the participation rate and the employment–population ratio.
Unemployment rate = 1,433,000 / 22,090,500 × 100 = 6.5%
Participation rate = 22,090,500 / 34,057,600 × 100 = 64.9%
Employment–population ratio = 20,657,500 / 34,057,600 × 100 = 60.7%

Check: labour force = 20,657,500 + 1,433,000 = 22,090,500 ✓
Memory Aid: Watch the Bottom of the Fraction

The unemployment rate divides by the labour force. The participation rate and the employment–population ratio divide by the working-age population.

Shortcut: U/L, L/P, E/P (Unemployed over Labour force, Labour force over Population, Employed over Population).

Vocab Trap: “Not Working” Is Not “Unemployed”

A retiree, a full-time student who is not searching and a discouraged worker are all not in the labour force. They are not counted as unemployed.

Section 3

What the Unemployment Rate Misses

The rate is useful. It is not an exact measure of joblessness. It can be too low and too high at the same time.

It understates joblessness because…It overstates joblessness because…
Discouraged workers stopped searching in a recession. They count as “not in the labour force”, not as unemployed.The LFS does not verify answers. Some people say they are searching to stay eligible for Employment Insurance (EI), though they are really out of the labour force.
Anyone with any job counts as employed. That includes part-timers who want full-time work. Underemployment is invisible.Some people work in illegal activity, or hide legitimate work to avoid taxes, yet say they are unemployed.

Trends in Participation

A higher participation rate means more labour is available. That raises GDP and GDP per person. Here is what happened to adults aged 15 and over (Fig 5.2):

  • Men: fell from a high of 78.4% in 1981 to 69.2% in November 2024. Reasons: earlier retirement, young men staying in school longer, and more young men neither working nor studying.
  • Women: rose from 45.7% in 1976 to a peak of 62.2% in 2007.
  • Overall: rose from 61.5% in 1976 to a peak of 67.6% in 2007. The women’s rise outweighed the men’s fall.
  • COVID-19: from 2019 to 2020, men fell from 70.0% to 68.8% and women from 61.3% to 59.5%. Many people left the labour force instead of looking for work while the economy was closed. By November 2024 there was no full recovery. Men were at 69.2% (below 70.0%) and women at 61.0% (below 61.3%).

Jobs Are Created and Destroyed All the Time

In November 2024, employment was 605,000 above a year earlier. So at least 605,000 jobs were created. Gross creation was larger, because some firms cut jobs or closed.

  • By province, October to November 2024: Alberta +24,300, Ontario −5,700, Newfoundland and Labrador −1,900.
  • By industry: manufacturing −28,500, educational services +15,400.

Even when the rate rises, some firms are hiring. Even when it falls, some are laying people off.

Miss the discouraged and the part-timers: the rate is too low. Believe the EI claimants and the underground workers: the rate is too high.

Section 4

The Four Types of Unemployment

The Canadian unemployment rate follows the business cycle. It rises in recessions and falls in expansions. It never reaches zero. It has not been below 4.8% in the last 48 years.

There are four types. Each has a different cause.

Frictional

Short-term unemployment from the time it takes to match workers with jobs. Workers and jobs differ in skills, pay and conditions, so both sides spend time searching. There is always some, since jobs are created and destroyed constantly.

Good news: the search time produces better matches and more productive workers.

Structural

A lasting mismatch between workers’ skills or location and what jobs require. It lasts longer than frictional unemployment because retraining takes time.

Examples: the decline of newspapers left reporters and editors needing new skills. A mismatch can also be about place (reporter jobs in Ottawa, you live in Winnipeg) or law. When the Newfoundland and Labrador cod fishery closed, fishers and plant workers were not legally allowed to do that work. Many moved to the Prairies for labouring jobs.

Cyclical

Job losses caused by a recession. Sales fall, so firms cut production and lay off workers.

Example: in February 2009, Chrysler temporarily closed its Brampton, Ontario assembly plant because of low sales. The laid-off workers were cyclically unemployed.

Seasonal

Caused by weather or the calendar. Ski resorts hire less in summer. Department stores hire in November and December and cut staff after New Year’s. Fisheries hire when the season opens. Construction slows in winter.

Seasonal adjustment: StatCan publishes seasonally adjusted and unadjusted rates. Economists use the seasonally adjusted one. The “unemployment rate” in the news is the seasonally adjusted rate.

Memory Aid: Find · Skill · Cycle · Season

Each word names the cause. Finding a match = frictional. A Skill mismatch = structural. The business Cycle = cyclical. The Season = seasonal.

Full Employment and the Natural Rate

At the peak of an expansion, cyclical unemployment falls to zero. Frictional and structural unemployment remain. Full employment means only those two are left.

The natural rate of unemployment is the full-employment rate. It equals frictional plus structural unemployment. The ups and downs of the actual rate come mainly from cyclical changes.

  • Economists disagree about its value, and it changes over time.
  • 2019 Bank of Canada economists estimated Canada’s natural rate fell between 1989 and 2018. They put it between 5.6% and 6.7% in 2018.
  • US estimates are commonly 4–5%.
  • The natural rate is a theory. StatCan’s official rate measures reality, including cyclical unemployment. The two are equal only when cyclical unemployment is zero.
Memory Aid: Natural = F + S

At full employment, cyclical = 0, but unemployment is not 0. Full employment does not mean everyone has a job.

Apply the Concept: Will Chatbots Cause Permanent Structural Unemployment?

OpenAI released ChatGPT in 2022. In May 2023, Geoffrey Hinton (University of Toronto, a pioneer of the ideas behind modern AI) left Google. He worried that chatbots could replace paralegals, personal assistants, translators and others who do routine tasks.

The worry has a long history. Machines replaced cotton weavers working at home in 1700s England. Ford’s moving assembly line (1913) needed fewer workers per car. Horse transport vanished, and so did work for blacksmiths and carriage builders. More recently, online shopping closed Eatons, Sears and Nordstrom in Canada. Streaming ended DVD rental: Blockbuster had over 400 Canadian stores in the early 2000s and now has none.

The counterpoint. Workers who lose jobs usually find new ones within months, except in severe recessions. Technology also creates jobs. More auto jobs appeared than carriage jobs were lost. New “prompt engineer” jobs pay up to $300,000. Over the long run, employment grows about as fast as the population. So far there is no big rise in structural unemployment.

“This time is different?” Two arguments say it might be. AI and robotics may disrupt more areas at once than past waves. And it may be harder for structurally unemployed workers to find new jobs. But predictions of permanent technological unemployment go back to the Luddites, who smashed textile machines in 1700s England. They have been wrong so far. The question stays open.

Section 5

What Determines the Unemployment Rate

Workers search with resumés, job sites and referrals. Firms use ads, online postings, job fairs and campus recruiting. The government can help:

  • Speeding up matching lowers frictional unemployment.
  • Funding retraining lowers structural unemployment.

Other policies push unemployment up. They (a) lengthen the search, (b) discourage firms from hiring, or (c) hold wages above the equilibrium wage.

Employment Insurance (EI)

Think about the opportunity cost of searching. After losing a job, you could take a low-wage job at once. If you keep searching for a better one, the cost is the pay you give up. That cost grows the longer you search.

EI is Canada’s program for the unemployed. Rules and payments vary by region. It replaces 55% of earnings, up to a maximum of $668 per week (2024). EI lowers the opportunity cost of searching. Recipients search longer, so the measured unemployment rate is higher.

Most economists still support EI:

  • Without it, unemployed workers’ spending would fall sharply and recessions would get worse.
  • It eases hardship.
  • Longer searches give better worker–job matches and higher productivity.

Wages Above Equilibrium

Minimum Wage Laws

Each province and territory sets its own. In December 2024 the highest was $19 (Nunavut) and the lowest was $15 (Saskatchewan). A minimum wage above the equilibrium wage creates a surplus of labour. Teenagers are hit hardest: some studies find a 10% rise in the minimum wage cuts teen employment by about 2%. Most economists think the effect on the overall rate is small.

Labour Unions

Unions bargain for higher wages. In unionized industries the wage is typically above equilibrium, so employers hire fewer workers. In 2023, 30.4% of Canadian workers were union members: 76.7% in the public sector and 15.5% in the private sector. Most economists say the effect on overall unemployment is small. Workers shut out of union jobs mostly find work elsewhere.

Efficiency Wages

A firm voluntarily pays above the market wage to get more effort. Workers are hard to monitor (“this call may be monitored”). A high wage makes losing the job costly, because other jobs pay less. So workers try harder. The productivity gain can more than cover the higher wage, so cost per unit may fall. Labour supplied exceeds labour demanded, so there is some unemployment even in a boom.

Memory Aid: MUE

Three ways wages sit above equilibrium: Minimum wage, Unions, Efficiency wages.

And remember: EI doesn’t raise the wage. It stretches the search.

Exam Trap: Efficiency Wages Are Voluntary

A minimum wage is imposed by law. A union wage is bargained. An efficiency wage is chosen freely by the firm because it pays off.

Section 6

Measuring Inflation: The CPI

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. The price level is the average price of goods and services in the economy. The inflation rate is the percentage increase in the price level from one year to the next.

You met the GDP deflator in Chapter 4: nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100. It is the broadest price measure. But it is not the best one for households. It includes goods households never buy, such as large electric generators. It includes exports. It leaves out imports. So we turn to the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks the prices an average household faces.

StatCan builds a basket of goods from sources such as retail scanner data and surveys. It checks prices in stores each month. The weights come from household spending. Here are the 2023 weights:

The CPI Formula

Pick a base year and set its CPI to 100. For any other year:

CPI = (cost of the basket this year ÷ cost of the same basket in the base year) × 100

The basket is held fixed. Quantities bought in later years do not matter. CPI values are index numbers. They have no units. They show change over time, not the absolute price level.

Inflation rate = (CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year × 100

Worked Example B — A Three-Good Basket
A typical family’s monthly basket is 1 case of root beer, 20 pizzas and 20 movies. The base year is 2002. Find the CPI for 2022 and 2023, and the inflation rate for 2023.
Item2002 (base) expenditure2022 expenditure2023 expenditure

Pizza (20)$200 ($10 each)$300 ($15 each)$280 ($14 each)
Movies (20)$500 ($25 each)$500 ($25 each)$550 ($27.50 each)

Don’t Let This Happen to You: Inflation Is Not the Rise Since the Base Year

CPI was 158.8 in 2023 and 161.8 in 2024. The tempting answer is 161.8 − 100 = 61.8%. That is wrong. It is the rise since the base year 2002.

Inflation is the change from last year: (161.8 − 158.8) ÷ 158.8 × 100 = 1.9%.

Is the CPI Accurate?

Many people rely on it. Policymakers track the economy with it. Firms set prices and wages with it. The federal government raises payments to seniors each year using it. Four biases make measured inflation too high:

  • Substitution bias. The CPI assumes fixed quantities. In reality households buy less of what gets expensive. If apples rise and oranges fall, you buy more oranges, so your real cost rises less.
  • Quality bias. Part of a higher price is a better product (safer cars, faster computers), not pure inflation.
  • New product bias. New goods, such as iPads, are not in the basket until it is updated.
  • Outlet bias. Shifting to discount stores such as Walmart and Costco, and to online shopping, lowers what people pay. The CPI misses it if the basket is not updated.

Economists once thought these biases overstated inflation by 0.5 to 1 percentage point. A measured 3% might really be 2% to 2.5%. StatCan now updates basket weights every year, which shrinks most of the problem. The 2023 update added small cooking appliances (juicers, air fryers) and removed DVD players.

Memory Aid: SQNO — “So Quality’s Never Obvious”

Substitution, Quality, New products, Outlets. All four push measured inflation up, never down.

The Producer Price Index (PPI)

StatCan also publishes producer price indexes. They track the prices firms receive at various stages of production. Examples are the Industrial Product Price Index (IPPI), the New Housing Price Index (NHPI) and the Wholesale Services Price Index. A PPI gives early warning of CPI changes and helps locate the source of inflation.

Vocab Trap: Three Price Measures

StatCan compiles three: the CPI (households’ purchases, best for cost of living), the GDP deflator (everything produced) and the PPI (prices producers receive).

Section 7

Adjusting for Inflation: Real vs Nominal Values

A dollar bought more decades ago, because prices were lower. A price index lets us compare dollars from different years.

A nominal value is measured in the prices of the year it was earned. A real value is corrected for inflation, so it is in base-year dollars. The CPI base year is 2002.

Real value = nominal value ÷ price index × 100

To move dollars between two years:

Value in year B dollars = value in year A dollars × (CPI of year B ÷ CPI of year A)

Worked Example C — A 1993 Salary in 2024 Dollars
Your mother earned $30,000 in 1993. The CPI was 86.3 in 1993 and 161.8 in 2024. What is that salary worth in 2024 dollars?
161.8 / 86.3 = 1.87  (prices were 1.87 times higher in 2024)

Value in 2024 dollars = 30,000 × 161.8 / 86.3 = $56,246

A $56,246 salary in 2024 buys about what $30,000 bought in 1993.
Memory Aid: Put the Year You Want on Top

Want 2024 dollars? Put the 2024 CPI in the numerator and the starting year’s CPI underneath. Prices rose, so the answer must be bigger than the starting amount. If it is smaller, you flipped the fraction.

Solved Problem 5.2 — Real Hourly Wages
Your professor says students today have it easy. In 1992 the professor’s minimum-wage job paid $5 an hour. Where you live, the minimum wage is $17.20, more than three times as much. Is the professor right? (CPI: 84 in 1992, 161.8 in 2024.)
Real wage = nominal wage / CPI × 100

1992: 5 / 84 × 100 = $5.95   (2002 dollars)
2024: 17.20 / 161.8 × 100 = $10.63   (2002 dollars)

10.63 / 5.95 = about 1.8

Today’s earner is better off in real terms. But the gain is about 1.8 times, not 3 times.

Exam Trap: Compare Reals With Reals

A real value is in base-year dollars. Never compare a real wage with a nominal wage. Put both in the same year’s dollars first.

Section 8

Real versus Nominal Interest Rates

The nominal interest rate is the stated rate on a loan. The real interest rate corrects it for inflation. It measures the extra buying power the lender receives and the borrower pays.

Worked Example D — The Coffee Example
Coffee is the only good. It costs $2.00 a cup at the start of the year. You lend $1,000 for a year at 6%. Inflation is 2.5%. What is your real return?
Start: $1,000 / $2.00 = 500 cups
End:   you receive $1,000 × 1.06 = $1,060
       coffee now costs 2.00 × 1.025 = $2.05
       $1,060 / $2.05 = 517 cups

517 / 500 = 1.034, so you can buy about 3.4% more coffee.

Approximation:  r = i − π = 6% − 2.5% = 3.5%

The approximation is real rate = nominal rate − inflation rate, or r = i − π. It works well when inflation is low. The small gap (3.5% vs 3.4%) is rounding. The course and Tutorial 3 use r = i − π, so use it on the test.

If the nominal rate is fixed, higher inflation means a lower real rate. Suppose the lender expected 2.5% inflation, but the actual rate was 5%. Then the real rate is 6% − 5% = 1%, not 3.5%. The lender loses and the borrower gains.

Memory Aid: Surprise Inflation Is a Gift to Borrowers

Inflation higher than expected: the real rate falls, the borrower gains, the lender loses. Inflation lower than expected: the reverse.

Canadian Interest Rates

  • Prime rate: the rate for the most credit-worthy businesses.
  • Conventional mortgage rate: the rate for the most credit-worthy people buying a house.
  • Overnight rate: what banks charge each other for 24-hour loans.
  • Bank rate: what the Bank of Canada charges when it lends to commercial banks.

All of them tend to rise when the bank rate rises and fall when it falls.

What Fig 5.5 Shows (January 1980 to January 2022)

The graph plots the bank rate as the nominal rate. Inflation is the percentage change in the CPI from a year earlier. Real rate = nominal − inflation.

  • Early 1980s: high inflation, high nominal rates, a big gap between nominal and real.
  • 2008–09 recession: low inflation, low nominal rates, a small gap.
  • After COVID-19: inflation above normal while rates were held near zero. Real rates were negative for about two and a half years.
  • August 1980: nominal 10.45% looks high, but the real rate was about −0.5%. You repaid less buying power than you borrowed.
  • June 1994: nominal almost 6% with inflation near zero. The real rate was about 6%. Borrowing was expensive.
Exam Trap: A High Nominal Rate Is Not Always Expensive Borrowing

Always subtract inflation. A nominal 10.45% with high inflation can be cheaper than a nominal 6% with no inflation.

Section 9

What Inflation Costs

A thought experiment. Overnight, every price doubles and every wage doubles. An iPhone goes from $1,200 to $2,400. Your pay goes from $25,000 to $50,000. Your purchasing power is unchanged.

Why? Spending equals income (Chapter 4). The extra $1,200 you pay becomes income for Apple workers, the manufacturer, Best Buy staff and shareholders. On average, if inflation is 10%, prices and incomes both rise 10%, and goods are as affordable as before.

So why do people dislike inflation? Because almost nobody is “average”.

Inflation Redistributes Income

Some incomes rise faster than inflation. Others rise slower. People on fixed incomes are hurt most. A retiree with a fixed $3,000 a month pension loses purchasing power every year. Many people see that as unfair. How much it matters depends on whether the inflation was expected.

Costs of Anticipated Inflation

Suppose everyone knows inflation will be 10% a year for 10 years. Workers ask for raises of at least 10%. Firms agree because they can raise prices. Lenders charge higher interest rates. Borrowers accept. There seem to be few costs, but some remain:

  • Some redistribution, because some incomes do not keep up.
  • The cost of holding paper money. Cash loses value at the inflation rate each year, so people and firms hold as little as possible.
  • Menu costs. Firms pay to change prices: new stickers and shelf labels, reprinted menus. Small at moderate inflation. Large when inflation is high.
  • The tax effect. Investors are taxed on nominal interest, not real interest. Even anticipated inflation raises their taxes and raises the cost of capital for business investment.

Costs of Unanticipated Inflation

Contracts fix payments for years. University professors sign wage contracts of three to four years. Mortgages usually have a 25-year amortization and are renewed several times. Both sides must forecast inflation.

  • Wage contract: a firm expects 6% inflation over three years and signs 8% annual raises. Inflation turns out to be 2%. Paying 8% raises can crush profits or sink the firm.
  • Mortgages: 1980s mortgage rates of 18% or more look huge next to today’s 4–5%. But 1980s inflation was about 10%, versus 2–3% now.

When actual inflation differs from expected inflation, some people win and some lose, purely by surprise. That arbitrary redistribution is the key reason people dislike unanticipated inflation.

Expected inflation mostly costs time and money (menus, cash). Surprise inflation moves wealth between people for no good reason.

Economics in Your Life: The Answer

Should you switch from energy to economics and finance if you graduate in a recession? Unemployment is higher and layoffs are more common in recessions. But you are only in second year. By graduation the recession is probably over.

Find out whether the energy layoffs are cyclical (a short-term dip from the recession) or structural (the industry is permanently smaller). If mostly cyclical, there is no need to change your path. If the industry will be permanently smaller, a change may be in your best interest.

Conclusion. Inflation and unemployment decide elections. Over the long run, though, the most important goal is sustained growth in real GDP per person. Only that raises the standard of living. That is the topic of Chapter 6 and Chapter 7.

Problem 3 — Build a CPI

The basket is 20 loaves of bread, 50 litres of gas and 2 haircuts. Base year 2024 prices: $2.50, $1.80, $30. In 2025: $2.75, $1.90, $30. In 2026: $2.80, $2.00, $33. Find the CPI for 2025 and 2026, and the 2026 inflation rate.

Show answer
2024 (base): 20 × 2.50 + 50 × 1.80 + 2 × 30 = 50 + 90 + 60 = $200, so CPI = 100
2025: 55 + 95 + 60 = $210, so CPI = 210 / 200 × 100 = 105
2026: 56 + 100 + 66 = $222, so CPI = 222 / 200 × 100 = 111
Inflation 2026 = (111 − 105) / 105 × 100 = 5.7%
Trap: it is not 11%. That is the rise since the base year.
Problem 4 — Inflation Adjustments

(a) Tuition was $2,500 in 1993 (CPI 86.3). What is that in 2024 dollars (CPI 161.8)? (b) A wage was $15.00 in 2015 (CPI 126.6). In 2024 it is $17.20 (CPI 161.8). Did the real wage rise or fall?

Show answer
(a) 2,500 × 161.8 / 86.3 = $4,687. The 2024 CPI goes on top.
(b) Real 2015 wage = 15.00 / 126.6 × 100 = $11.85
Real 2024 wage = 17.20 / 161.8 × 100 = $10.63
The real wage fell, even though the nominal wage rose.
Problem 5 — Interest Rates and Surprise Inflation

You lend $10,000 for one year at a 7% nominal rate. You expect 3% inflation.

(a) What real rate do you expect? (b) Actual inflation is 5%. What is the actual real rate? (c) Who gained, the lender or the borrower? (d) What if inflation had been 1%? (e) If a lender expects higher inflation, why does she ask for a higher nominal rate? (f) Check (b) exactly: what is $10,700 worth in today’s purchasing power if prices rose 5%?

Show answer
(a) 7% − 3% = 4%.
(b) 7% − 5% = 2%.
(c) The borrower gained. Inflation was higher than expected, so the real rate was lower than the lender planned.
(d) 7% − 1% = 6%. The lender gains.
(e) To keep her real rate the same. A higher expected π needs a higher i to hold r = i − π steady.
(f) $10,700 / 1.05 = $10,190 in today’s purchasing power. That is about 1.9% more than $10,000, close to the 2% approximation.

Common Exam Traps
  • Discouraged workers are not unemployed.
  • The participation rate divides by the working-age population, not the labour force.
  • Employment and the unemployment rate can both rise.
  • Natural rate = F + S. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • The quoted rate is seasonally adjusted.
  • Inflation is the change from last year, not from the base year.
  • All four CPI biases overstate inflation.
  • Put the target year’s CPI on top when moving dollars between years.
  • Real rate: r = i − π.
  • Surprise inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.
  • Efficiency wages are paid voluntarily by the firm.
  • EI lengthens search. It does not raise wages.
