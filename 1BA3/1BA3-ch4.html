Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every other chapter in this course asks what employees do — how they perceive, decide, communicate, lead, and perform. This chapter asks a prior question: why do they do it? Values are the deep, largely stable preferences a person carries into every job, every team, and every negotiation — the quiet standard against which a raise, a policy, or a boss's behaviour gets judged as fair or unfair, worth staying for or worth quitting over. Attitudes are what values become once they meet a specific job: job satisfaction and organizational commitment are the two most-measured variables in the entire field of organizational behaviour, because they are the closest thing managers have to an early-warning system for the outcomes they actually care about — absence, turnover, performance, and the informal extra effort (or its opposite, active sabotage) that no job description ever captures.

This chapter also does something most of the course does not: it takes you outside your own cultural assumptions. Hofstede's five dimensions of national culture describe why a leadership style that works in a Canadian office can misfire in a Manila or Moscow one — and Hofstede's list is, without much competition, the single most verbatim-tested framework on the midterm. If you memorize nothing else from this chapter, memorize the five dimensions and Canada's position on each.

Chapter Roadmap This chapter is taught in Week 2 (Sept 14–18) — the same week you are also assigned Chapter 2.1–2.3 (Personality) and, out of sequence, Chapter 5.2's sections on General Cognitive Ability and Emotional Intelligence. That means three chapters are genuinely running in parallel this week; don't let the fact that this page is numbered "Chapter 4" on the site make you think you're behind — you are exactly on schedule if you're reading this in Week 2, before Chapter 3. It is tested on the Midterm (Oct 29) and is part of Group Assignment #1 (in-class, week of Oct 5–9, covering Chapters 1–6). Budget roughly 2.5–3 hours: this chapter is dense with named, multi-part frameworks — Hofstede's five dimensions, the three fairness types, the three commitment types — that are prime verbatim-recall material.
Section 2

What Are Values? Generational and Person–Organization Fit

A value is a broad tendency to prefer certain states of affairs over others. Two things about that definition matter more than they first appear to. First, values are motivational — they signal what to seek and what to avoid — and normative — they signal how you believe you should and should not behave. Second, and more easily forgotten under exam pressure, values are broad. Valuing achievement, autonomy, or social welfare tells you something real about a person, but it does not reliably predict what that person will do in any one specific situation. That gap between a broad value and a specific behaviour is exactly the gap the rest of this chapter exists to close, one link at a time. People organize their values around things like achievement, power, autonomy, conformity, tradition, and social welfare, and most of that organization happens early: values are learned mainly through the reinforcement processes covered in Chapter 2 — parents, teachers, and religious or community figures shaping what a person comes to treat as simply obvious.

The textbook profiles five generations, each stamped by the formative events of its early adulthood: Traditionalists (born 1925–1945, shaped by the Depression and WWII), Baby Boomers (1946–1964, shaped by Vietnam and the civil rights era), Generation X (1965–1980, shaped by the dot-com boom), Millennials (1981–2000, shaped by 9/11 and the Great Recession), and Generation Z (2001–2020, shaped by the internet and school-shooting-era anxieties). It is tempting to stop there and treat the five as fixed personality types you can staff a team around. The exam-relevant finding cuts the other way: research shows more similarity than difference across generations in the things that matter most — core work attitudes, motivation, and work ethic. Where real differences show up, they are narrower than the stereotype suggests: later generations report placing somewhat more value on rapid career growth, status, leisure, and work–life balance, and the same underlying value can be expressed differently across generations — "respect," for instance, tends to mean being deferred to for an older worker and being genuinely listened to for a younger one. The values are more constant than the vocabulary.

The concept that actually does predictive work here is person–organization fit: the match between an individual's own values and the values the organization enacts day to day. Good fit predicts positive attitudes and behaviour, including lower quit rates, which is precisely why organizations bother tailoring job design, leadership style, and benefits to a generationally mixed workforce — not because the generations need fundamentally different management, but because fit, not birth year, is what drives the outcomes that matter. Cross-national research adds a further layer: work centrality, how central work is to a person's overall identity, varies by country far more than it varies by generation within a country. Japan scores highest on work centrality, Britain lowest, with the US and Belgium roughly average — and higher work centrality correlates with working more hours and taking less vacation, a pattern the chapter later reconnects to Japan's culture of karoshi, death from overwork.

Worked Example — Two Offers, Two Value Profiles
Priya and Owen are both offered the same entry-level analyst role at a fast-growing logistics startup. The job comes with a high base salary, unpredictable 55-hour weeks during peak season, minimal structure, and rapid opportunities to take on ambiguous, high-visibility projects. Priya, who values stability, predictable routine, and clearly defined authority, turns the offer down for a lower-paying role at an established insurer. Owen, who values autonomy and rapid skill growth over security, accepts immediately. Explain their decisions using person–organization fit — without assuming either of them is simply "wrong" about the job.
STEP 1 — separate the job's actual attributes from either
  candidate's reaction to them.
  The startup role: high pay, high autonomy, high ambiguity,
  weak structure, unpredictable hours. Neither candidate is
  misreading these facts; they differ only in how they value
  them.

STEP 2 — apply person-organization fit, not "who made the
  smarter choice."
  Priya's values (stability, predictability, clear authority)
  are a poor match for a low-structure, high-ambiguity startup
  -> low predicted person-organization fit -> her decision to
  decline predicts exactly what the theory would predict:
  had she taken it, expect lower satisfaction and a higher
  quit risk. Owen's values (autonomy, rapid growth) fit the
  same role well -> high predicted fit -> higher predicted
  satisfaction and retention in that same job.

STEP 3 — conclude.
  There is no universally "better" job here, only a better or
  worse match. A recruiter using only salary and title to
  predict who will thrive would get this exactly backwards;
  fit, not the job's objective attractiveness, is what should
  drive the hiring conversation.
Exam Trap — The Generational Stereotype Is Itself a Stereotype It is tempting to treat "Millennials want flexibility, Boomers want stability" as settled science. The textbook's own evidence says the opposite: documented generational differences in core work values are smaller than popular belief, and using birth-year categories to predict an individual's attitudes is a textbook case of the stereotyping bias from Chapter 3 — categorizing first, assuming shared traits second, and applying the assumption to an individual you haven't actually assessed. If a question describes a manager making staffing or motivation decisions purely on generational assumptions, the correct diagnosis is a stereotyping error, not sound workforce planning.
Section 3

Cultural Differences in Values: Hofstede's Dimensions

Dutch social scientist Geert Hofstede questioned more than 116,000 IBM employees across 40 countries about their work-related values, later extending the sample by another 36 countries and regions. Four dimensions emerged from the original analysis — power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity/femininity, and individualism/collectivism — and a fifth, long-term/short-term orientation, was added through subsequent work with Canadian researcher Michael Bond, whose data better captured Eastern cultural patterns. Together the five give you a vocabulary for describing how entire societies differ in what they expect from work, authority, and each other — and Canada and the US turn out to sit close together on four of the five, which is exactly the pattern the exam likes to test.

Power distance is the extent to which a society accepts an unequal distribution of power. In small-power-distance cultures, inequality is minimized, superiors are accessible, and power differences are downplayed — Denmark, New Zealand, Israel, and Austria anchor this end. In large-power-distance cultures, inequality is accepted as natural and superiors are comparatively inaccessible — the Philippines, Russia, and Mexico anchor this end. Out of 76 countries and regions ranked, Canada and the United States fall 15th and 16th — both clearly on the low-power-distance side of the average (rank 38).

Uncertainty avoidance is the extent to which people are uncomfortable with uncertain or ambiguous situations. Strong-uncertainty-avoidance cultures stress rules, conformity, security, and treat hard work as a virtue — Japan, Greece, and Portugal anchor this end. Weak-uncertainty-avoidance cultures are less concerned with rules and conformity and are comparatively comfortable with risk — Singapore, Denmark, and Sweden anchor this end. The US and Canada both rank well below average here (13th and 15th of 76) — both weak-uncertainty-avoidance cultures.

Masculinity/femininity is the dimension students most often mis-define, so read the actual definition twice. More masculine cultures sharply differentiate gender roles, support the dominance of men, and stress economic performance; more feminine cultures accept fluid gender roles, stress sexual equality, and value quality of life. Slovakia and Japan rank most masculine; the Scandinavian countries rank most feminine. Canada sits roughly mid-pack; the US is fairly masculine, falling about halfway between Canada and Japan — the one dimension on which the US and Canada visibly diverge.

Individualism/collectivism describes whether a culture stresses independence, individual initiative, and privacy (individualist) or interdependence and loyalty to one's family or clan (collectivist). The US, Australia, Great Britain, and Canada are among the most individualistic societies in Hofstede's data; Venezuela, Colombia, and Pakistan are among the most collective, with Japan falling roughly mid-pack. Canada and the US are both highly individualistic.

Long-term/short-term orientation, the dimension added with Bond's research, distinguishes cultures that stress persistence, thrift, and close attention to status differences (long-term) from those that stress personal steadiness, face-saving, and social niceties (short-term). China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, and South Korea characterize the long-term end; the United States, Canada, Great Britain, Zimbabwe, and Nigeria characterize the short-term end. Hofstede and Bond argue that long-term orientation partly explains prolific East Asian entrepreneurship.

Hofstede's work has since been verified and extended by the GLOBE project, led by Robert House, which surveyed more than 17,000 managers across 62 societies. GLOBE split masculinity into two more precise components — assertiveness and gender egalitarianism — and split individualism into resource distribution and group loyalty. A related but distinct idea is cultural distance: the extent to which two cultures differ in values. Canada is culturally much closer to the US than to Japan, and greater cultural distance predictably makes communication, negotiation, mergers, acquisitions, and expatriate adjustment harder — which is part of why firms tend to under-invest in culturally distant markets even when the business case looks strong. Cultural tightness versus looseness is a separate dimension again: tight cultures (Pakistan, Singapore, South Korea) maintain strong, clearly sanctioned norms for behaviour; loose cultures (the Netherlands, Brazil, Australia, Canada, the US) are more flexible and more forgiving of deviation. Finally, cultural intelligence (CQ) is the capability to function and manage well in culturally diverse environments, built from knowledge, motivation, and behaviour; people high in CQ tend to score higher on intercultural adjustment, global leadership, and performance in intercultural settings. A Léger Marketing poll cited in the textbook suggests Canadians may be more inclined toward CQ than Americans, British, or French respondents — plausibly a byproduct of the multicultural makeup of Canada's largest cities.

These dimensions aren't trivia — the textbook draws four practical implications from them. Exporting OB theories: North American practices like participative decision-making assume low power distance and may not translate to high-power-distance cultures, where employees may be more comfortable simply deferring to the boss. Importing OB theories: Japanese-inspired practices such as quality circles and just-in-time production have had mixed success in North America partly because Japan's traditionally high employment security made continuous-improvement suggestion schemes far less threatening there than they can feel to North American workers uncertain about their own job security. Appreciating global customers: cross-cultural blind spots cause real, costly errors — Disneyland Paris's rocky early reception and Samsung's recalled calendar (pulled after a product-display image, intended innocuously, offended some North American recipients) are the textbook's examples. Developing global employees: firms build cultural intelligence deliberately, for instance the design studios Japanese and Korean automakers built in California specifically to help their designers internalize North American tastes.

Worked Example — A Canadian Manager Posted Abroad
Aisha, a Canadian operations manager used to flat hierarchies and individual recognition, is posted for two years to lead a plant in a country that scores high on power distance and collectivism relative to Canada. Using only Hofstede's dimensions, predict three specific friction points she is likely to encounter in her first few months, before she has time to adjust her style.
FRICTION POINT 1 — power distance mismatch.
  Aisha's instinct to invite open disagreement in meetings may
  be read locally not as empowering but as a manager abdicating
  her role; local staff may expect her to decide and direct,
  and may interpret her open-floor style as a lack of
  confidence rather than a strength.

FRICTION POINT 2 — individualism/collectivism mismatch.
  Aisha's instinct to publicly praise a single top performer
  (standard in individualistic Canada) may embarrass that
  employee in front of the group and read as favouritism that
  damages group cohesion, rather than as motivating.

FRICTION POINT 3 — expecting the same predictors of fit.
  Aisha may assume the local staff want the same autonomy and
  individual-recognition-based job design Canadian person-
  organization fit research would predict they want; if local
  values instead favour group-based rewards and clear
  direction, importing a Canadian-style job design could
  directly reduce, not improve, fit and satisfaction.

CONCLUSION — none of these are errors of effort; they are
  errors of exporting an OB theory built on one cultural
  value profile into a plant with a different one.
Exam Trap — Two Dimensions Students Consistently Misread Masculinity/femininity is not about gender roles in the colloquial sense — it is about whether a culture's values emphasize achievement, performance, and dominance (masculine) versus quality of life, fluid roles, and nurturing (feminine). A country can be "feminine" on this dimension without that describing anything about its actual gender-role politics. Uncertainty avoidance is not risk aversion in the everyday sense either — it specifically measures discomfort with ambiguity and a preference for rules, structure, and security, not a general unwillingness to take financial or physical risks. Both distinctions are reliably tested, usually by offering the colloquial (wrong) definition as a distractor answer.

Lecture Supplement — Cultural Intelligence: The Four Sub-Skills

The four-part breakdown below comes from Dr. Kassaye’s Week 2 lecture slides (slides 7–8), which treat cultural intelligence as one of three abilities alongside cognitive and emotional intelligence. The textbook covers CQ more briefly, in this chapter’s section on cultural values. Because the slides go further than the text, learn the four-part version.

The lecture defines cultural intelligence (CQ) as a person’s capability to function effectively in situations characterized by cultural diversity, and then breaks that capability into four distinct sub-skills. The slides pose the question “what jobs would require high CQ?” — expatriate managers, international sales, global project leads, and anyone serving a multicultural customer base are the obvious answers.

CQ-Strategy
How a person interprets and understands intercultural experiences — the planning, monitoring, and sense-making that happens before and during a cross-cultural interaction. (Elsewhere this is called metacognitive CQ.)
CQ-Knowledge
A person’s understanding of how cultures are similar and different — the content knowledge of norms, values, practices, and conventions. This is where Hofstede’s dimensions live.
CQ-Motivation (also called Drive)
A person’s interest in experiencing other cultures and interacting with people from different cultures — the energy and confidence to engage rather than avoid.
CQ-Behaviour (also called Action)
A person’s capability to modify their own verbal and nonverbal behaviour so that it is appropriate for different cultures — the part other people actually see.
How the Textbook and the Lecture Differ on CQ Johns & Saks define cultural intelligence as “the capability to function and manage well in culturally diverse environments” and say it encompasses three things: knowledge, motivation, and behaviour. The lecture uses the four-factor model from the CQ research literature, which keeps those three (renaming motivation Drive and behaviour Action as alternatives) and adds a fourth, CQ-Strategy — the metacognitive skill of planning for and reflecting on cross-cultural encounters. The two versions do not contradict each other; the lecture’s is simply the finer-grained one. For a multiple-choice question: if the options are three components, answer with the textbook’s knowledge/motivation/behaviour; if the options name four sub-skills or use the labels Strategy, Knowledge, Drive/Motivation, and Action/Behaviour, answer with the lecture’s version. The one item that only appears in the lecture version is Strategy, which makes it the most likely “which of the following is NOT…” distractor in either direction.

Section 4

What Are Attitudes? From Belief and Value to Behaviour

An attitude is a fairly stable evaluative tendency to respond consistently to some specific object, situation, person, or category of people. Notice how this differs from a value: where a value is broad and general (valuing "interesting work" in the abstract), an attitude is specific to a particular object (satisfaction with this job, on this team, under this supervisor). You can value work highly in the abstract and still dislike your particular job intensely — the value doesn't determine the attitude, and the attitude, in turn, is only weakly linked to behaviour. Attitude best predicts behaviour when a person has direct experience with the attitude object and holds the attitude strongly and confidently; a vague, second-hand, or weakly held attitude is a poor behavioural predictor.

The textbook's core model for how an attitude forms is a simple chain: belief + value → attitude → behaviour. A belief is what a person thinks is true about the object ("my job interferes with my family"); a value is what the person cares about ("I dislike anything that hurts my family"); combined, they produce an attitude ("I dislike my job," i.e., low job satisfaction); the attitude, held strongly enough and paired with direct experience, produces a behaviour ("I'll look for another job," i.e., turnover). This chain is the scaffolding for the rest of the chapter: job satisfaction and organizational commitment are both attitudes, and everything from Section 5 onward is really an elaboration of what feeds into that middle step of the chain, and what comes out the other end of it.

Worked Example — Reacting to a New Remote-Work Policy
Daniel's employer announces that everyone must return to the office five days a week, ending the hybrid arrangement Daniel has used for two years to manage a long commute and childcare pickup. Trace Daniel's likely reaction through the belief-value-attitude-behaviour chain, and identify what would make his behaviour less predictable from his attitude alone.
BELIEF: "This policy will make it impossible for me to manage
  pickup and will add two hours to my daily commute."

VALUE: "I place a very high priority on being reliably present
  for my family in the evenings."

-> ATTITUDE: strong dissatisfaction with the new policy,
  specifically (a facet attitude, not necessarily overall job
  dissatisfaction yet).

-> BEHAVIOUR (predicted): raising a formal objection, seeking
  an exception, or beginning a job search -- IF the attitude
  is strongly held and Daniel has direct, immediate experience
  of the conflict (which he does, starting week one).

WHAT WOULD WEAKEN THE LINK: if Daniel has few alternative job
  options, strong continuance commitment (Section 7) or
  situational constraints could produce the SAME attitude
  without the predicted behaviour -- a reminder that attitude
  and behaviour are linked, not identical, and other variables
  can intervene between the two.
Section 5

Job Satisfaction and Its Determinants

Job satisfaction is a collection of attitudes people hold about their jobs. It helps to distinguish facet satisfaction — satisfaction with one specific aspect, such as pay, supervision, co-workers, the work itself, career opportunities, benefits, working conditions, or company policy — from overall satisfaction, a summary attitude across facets. Two employees can report identical overall satisfaction scores for entirely different underlying reasons, which is why the standard measurement tools — the Job Descriptive Index and the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire — both measure at the facet level before summarizing.

What determines satisfaction? The textbook organizes the answer around five interacting determinants. Discrepancy theory holds that satisfaction is a function of the gap between the outcomes a person wants from a job and the outcomes they perceive they are actually receiving. Two people in objectively identical jobs can land at very different satisfaction levels because they perceive the job differently (a difference in beliefs) or because they want different things from it (a difference in values) — discrepancy theory is fundamentally a comparison against your own wants, not against another person.

Fairness is the second determinant, and it splits into three genuinely distinct types — the single most heavily tested distinction in this chapter. Distributive fairness concerns the outcomes themselves: did you receive what you think you deserve? It is explained by equity theory, which holds that people compare their own outcome/input ratio to the ratio of some relevant comparison other — a co-worker, a group of co-workers, or even a general conception of everyone in one's occupation. Equity is perceived when my outcomes / my inputs = other's outcomes / other's inputs. Inputs are anything a person contributes to the exchange — education, seniority, effort, training; outcomes are anything the organization provides in return — pay, recognition, working conditions, career opportunities. Inequity is dissatisfying, and being on the "short end of the stick" — high inputs relative to outcomes, compared to the other person's ratio — is the most dissatisfying position of all, more provoking than being over-rewarded.

Procedural fairness concerns the process used to decide outcomes, not the outcome itself. The textbook's illustrative case is Greg, whose boss informs him — entirely without warning or consultation — that his performance evaluation is complete and he's getting a healthy raise. Greg is pleased with the raise (distributive fairness is satisfied) but vaguely unhappy that the process excluded him, since his previous employer used a two-way process of independent self- and manager-evaluation followed by a joint discussion. Four factors drive perceptions of procedural fairness: managers following consistent procedures over time and across people; managers using accurate, unbiased information; allowing two-way communication during the allocation process; and welcoming appeals of the procedure or the outcome. Procedural unfairness is especially dissatisfying when distributive fairness is also low — which is exactly why Greg's peers who received smaller raises are likely to resent the process far more than Greg does himself.

Interactional fairness concerns the interpersonal treatment and communication used when an outcome is explained — respectful communication is sincere, polite, and treats the person with dignity; informative communication is candid, timely, and thorough. It is entirely possible for a fair outcome, decided by a fair process, to still feel unfair if it is explained coldly or dismissively. A useful diagnostic: people who experience procedural unfairness tend to blame "the system," while people who experience interactional unfairness tend to blame the boss personally — even a manager with no control over the underlying procedure almost always has control over how respectfully and thoroughly they explain it.

Distributive Fairness
Judged via equity theory — my outcome/input ratio versus a comparison other's. The question is what I got.
Procedural Fairness
Consistency, accuracy/lack of bias, two-way communication, and the right to appeal. The question is how it was decided.
Interactional Fairness
Respectful (sincere, dignified) and informative (candid, timely) treatment when the outcome is explained. The question is how I was treated.

The remaining two determinants are more personal. Disposition contributes a genuine personality-based component to satisfaction: identical twins raised apart show similar satisfaction levels, satisfaction is fairly stable even across employer changes, and disposition measured in adolescence predicts adult job satisfaction. Extraversion and conscientiousness correlate with higher satisfaction; neuroticism with lower. Mood and emotion operate through affective events theory (Howard Weiss and Russell Cropanzano), which treats a job as a stream of discrete events that provoke short, intense emotions or longer, diffuse moods, both of which feed into overall satisfaction. Two specific mechanisms matter here: emotional contagion, where moods spread between people (including to and from customers), and emotional labour, the requirement to conform to emotional "display rules" regardless of how you actually feel — either exaggerating positive emotion you don't fully feel or suppressing negative emotion you do feel. Suppressing genuine negative emotion is draining and reliably reduces satisfaction while increasing stress; expressing genuine positive emotion tends to boost satisfaction, including via contagion to the people around you.

Across all of this, a handful of facets consistently show up as the biggest contributors to satisfaction: mentally challenging work, meaningful work (positive impact on others), adequate compensation, career opportunities, and friendly, helpful people. Interestingly, people tend to overestimate how much pay matters to their satisfaction and underestimate how much meaningful, challenging work matters. Even dirty work — jobs carrying physical, social, or moral stigma, such as cleaners, prison guards, or debt collectors — can produce genuinely high satisfaction, sustained by occupational solidarity among co-workers and realistically calibrated outcome expectations.

Worked Example — An Equity Comparison With Numbers
Two lab assistants, Farah and Devon, do identical work and are paid the same $24/hour. Farah has a bachelor's degree and three years of relevant experience; Devon has a college diploma and no prior experience. Farah has become visibly disengaged and told a coworker, "I don't know why I bother working so hard here." Diagnose Farah's reaction using equity theory.
STEP 1 — identify the ratio comparison.
  Farah's inputs: bachelor's degree + 3 years' experience.
  Devon's inputs: college diploma + 0 years' experience.
  Both receive the same outcome ($24/hour) -> Farah's
  outcome/input ratio is lower than Devon's.

STEP 2 — classify the inequity.
  Farah has higher inputs for the same outcome as Devon --
  the "short end of the stick." This is the specific pattern
  equity theory identifies as most dissatisfying.

STEP 3 — predict the behavioural response.
  Equity theory predicts Farah will act to restore balance,
  most likely by reducing her inputs (working less hard,
  disengaging) rather than more visible tactics like leaving
  outright -- consistent with what the coworker is observing.

STEP 4 — recommend.
  The supervisor should review whether pay is tied to
  relevant inputs like education and experience; if a pay
  adjustment isn't feasible, transparent communication about
  how compensation is determined (procedural + interactional
  fairness) can offset some of the distributive inequity,
  since Farah's perception of unfairness, not the pay gap by
  itself, is driving her withdrawal.
Worked Example — A Layoff Diagnosed Across All Three Fairness Types
A division is being downsized. Employees are told individually, in ten-minute meetings, that their position is eliminated effective immediately, with no advance notice and minimal explanation of how the affected positions were chosen. Severance is generous — above the legal minimum and above what employees expected. Diagnose the situation across all three fairness types.
DISTRIBUTIVE FAIRNESS — likely perceived as HIGH.
  The outcome itself (generous, above-expectation severance)
  is favourable relative to what employees believe they
  deserve.

PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS — likely perceived as LOW.
  No advance notice, no visible consistent criteria for which
  positions were cut, no two-way communication, no apparent
  avenue to appeal the decision.

INTERACTIONAL FAIRNESS — likely perceived as LOW.
  Ten-minute meetings with minimal explanation are informative
  in name only; there is little evidence of respectful,
  thorough communication given the significance of the news.

CONCLUSION — generous severance (distributive fairness) will
  NOT fully offset the anger from a poorly run, poorly
  explained process. Expect affected employees to blame "the
  system" for the process and their direct manager personally
  for how it was delivered -- and expect remaining employees'
  organizational commitment and OCB (Section 6) to take a
  hit even though they personally lost nothing.
Exam Trap — Distributive vs. Procedural vs. Interactional When a scenario question describes a fairness complaint, ask what is actually being judged: the outcome itself (distributive), the process that produced it (procedural), or the manner in which it was communicated (interactional). A classic trap pairs a good outcome with a badly run process — as in the layoff example above — specifically to test whether you'll conflate a fair result with a fair process. They are independent, and a scenario can be high on one and low on another simultaneously.

Section 6

Consequences of Job Satisfaction

Satisfaction matters because of what it predicts downstream, but the strength of each link varies more than students usually assume. The satisfaction–absence link is real but weak, constrained by unavoidable absence (illness, family emergencies), formal attendance-control policies, and informal workplace "absence culture" norms that can make even a satisfied employee take a sick day everyone else is taking. The satisfaction–turnover link is moderately strong and runs through a full process model: job satisfaction and organizational commitment, together with sudden "shocks" such as a marital breakup or an unsolicited job offer, feed into turnover intentions, which lead to actual turnover either impulsively or through a deliberate evaluation of the utility of quitting followed by an active job search. A useful pattern buried in this model is the honeymoon–hangover effect: satisfaction typically rises right after a job change (the honeymoon) and then often falls again as the new job's real flaws become visible (the hangover) — which is exactly why a friend who just quit a bad job may sour on the replacement within months.

The satisfaction–performance link is real but modest, and it is strongest for the content of the work itself and for complex, high-autonomy jobs, where people have more actual control over their performance level. There is also a causality wrinkle worth remembering: contemporary evidence suggests satisfaction is more often the cause of good performance (via the rewards good performance generates) than the reverse, though many organizations under-reward good performance enough that the loop weakens. The stronger positive link by far is with organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB) — voluntary, informal behaviour that contributes to organizational effectiveness and typically isn't captured or rewarded by the formal evaluation system. The textbook's example: a colleague, herself busy on a deadline, stops to help you debug difficult software for ten minutes with no expectation of credit. Four defining features mark true OCB: it is voluntary (not in the job description), spontaneous (no one ordered it), genuinely beneficial to the organization (not just a personal favour), and unlikely to be explicitly picked up by performance evaluation. The textbook identifies four recognizable forms: helping behaviour (offering assistance to others), conscientiousness (attention to detail and not wasting organizational resources, like showing up on the worst weather day of the year), being a good sport (tolerating the inevitable minor frustrations of organizational life without complaint), and courtesy and cooperation (small acts like warning a colleague about an incoming rush job). Procedural and interactional fairness, more than distributive fairness, drive OCB — when a manager strays from fair process, an employee may find it too risky to formally under-perform, but easy to quietly withdraw the informal extras that OCB consists of.

The mirror image of OCB is counterproductive work behaviour (CWB): intentional verbal or physical behaviour meant to harm the organization or the people in it — theft, abusive supervision, incivility, bullying. CWB is rooted in dissatisfaction interacting with personality and situational context; unlike the OCB withdrawal sequence, CWB is an active, not merely a withheld, response. Speaking of that sequence: when dissatisfaction sets in, the textbook's withdrawal model describes a typical progression from reduced OCB, to lateness, to absenteeism, to eventual turnover — reduced OCB is usually the earliest and least visible warning sign, well before more dramatic outcomes appear on anyone's radar. Finally, satisfaction connects to customer satisfaction and profit through several converging channels at once: reduced absence and turnover keep experienced staff serving customers, higher OCB and teamwork improve service quality, and mood contagion carries an employee's affective state directly to the customers they interact with.

Worked Example — Will a Satisfaction Investment Pay Off?
A retail chain is deciding whether to invest $2 million in a program aimed at raising front-line employee satisfaction (better scheduling predictability, faster escalation of customer complaints to a supervisor, and a peer-recognition program). Using the consequences covered in this section, trace how — and how reliably — this investment could show up in the chain's bottom line.
WEAKEST, LEAST RELIABLE PATHWAY: reduced absence.
  Some improvement likely, but the satisfaction-absence link
  is weak, so this alone won't justify the spend.

MODERATE PATHWAY: reduced turnover.
  A moderately strong link; lower turnover reduces hiring and
  training costs directly -- a defensible partial justification
  on its own.

STRONGEST PATHWAY: OCB and service quality.
  The strongest positive link in the whole model. Front-line
  staff who feel fairly treated (note the program's design
  leans heavily on procedural/interactional fairness -- faster
  complaint escalation, transparent recognition) are likely to
  show more spontaneous helping and cooperation, which shows
  up in smoother customer interactions.

CONVERGING ON PROFIT: customer satisfaction.
  OCB improvements plus reduced turnover (more experienced
  staff on the floor) plus positive mood contagion to
  customers together make the strongest combined case for a
  profit effect -- notably NOT primarily through a direct
  satisfaction -> individual performance link, which is only
  modestly related for routine retail tasks.

RECOMMENDATION: justify the investment primarily through OCB,
  turnover, and customer-facing service quality -- not through
  an assumed large jump in individual task performance, which
  the evidence does not support as strongly.
Exam Trap — Satisfaction → Performance Is Weaker Than You'd Guess Students default to assuming "a happy worker is a productive worker" is the chapter's headline finding. It isn't: the satisfaction–performance correlation is real but modest, and it's OCB, not formal task performance, that shows the strongest positive link to satisfaction. If an exam question asks which consequence of job satisfaction is most strongly supported by the evidence, OCB — not performance — is very often the intended answer.
Section 7

Organizational Commitment

Organizational commitment is an attitude reflecting the strength of the linkage between an employee and an organization — and, like job satisfaction, it is a strong predictor of whether someone stays or leaves. Canadian researchers John Meyer and Natalie Allen identified three genuinely distinct types, and the shorthand is worth memorizing cold: affective commitment ("I want to stay") is based on identification and involvement with the organization, best predicted by interesting, satisfying work, role clarity, and having one's expectations met after hiring. Continuance commitment ("I have to stay") is based on the costs of leaving — a pension that would be forfeited, side bets built up over a career, being well integrated into the local community — or simply a lack of suitable alternatives; it predictably increases with tenure. Normative commitment ("I ought to stay") is based on ideology or a felt obligation, fostered by benefits like tuition reimbursement, strong identification with the organization's mission, or socialization practices that explicitly emphasize loyalty.

The three types don't operate in isolation — real employees carry a commitment profile combining all three at varying levels. Research identifies several recurring profiles: fully committed (high on all three), weakly committed (low on all three), affective dominant, and affective/normative dominant, with continuance dominant and affective/continuance dominant somewhat less common. The profiles research shows genuine synergy: being fully committed or affective/normative dominant outperforms being affective dominant alone, meaning the types reinforce rather than simply substitute for each other.

Consequences matter enormously here, and they cut against the everyday assumption that "commitment" is uniformly good news for an employer. All three types reduce turnover intentions and actual turnover — that part is consistent. But affective commitment is positively related to performance, because it focuses attention on organizational goals and genuinely enhances motivation, while continuance commitment is negatively related to performance — the burned-out employee who stays only because they can't afford to leave is a familiar, unproductive pattern. The worst combination for both employee and employer is high continuance commitment paired with low affective commitment: people locked into organizations they actively dislike, a pattern that becomes especially common during economic downturns when alternatives dry up. Very high commitment of any kind carries its own downsides too: family–work conflict, complicity in unethical behaviour (the textbook cites a General Electric price-fixing conspiracy as a real example of commitment curdling into loyalty-driven wrongdoing), and resistance to organizational change that the business genuinely needs to make.

Worked Example — Three Employees Who All Stay
A department has three long-tenured employees who have all turned down other job offers this year. Meiling speaks enthusiastically about the mission and mentors junior staff unasked. Robert says openly he'd leave "in a heartbeat" if he could find a comparable pension elsewhere. Aiden says he stays because the company invested heavily in his professional certification and he'd feel he was letting the team down if he left mid-project. Identify each employee's dominant commitment type and recommend how their manager should treat each differently.
MEILING — affective commitment ("I want to stay").
  Enthusiasm + unasked mentoring signal identification and
  involvement. MANAGEMENT IMPLICATION: protect what's driving
  it -- interesting work, role clarity -- since this is the
  type most linked to performance and OCB; don't take it for
  granted or assume it's permanent regardless of treatment.

ROBERT — continuance commitment ("I have to stay").
  Explicitly stated: would leave for a comparable pension
  elsewhere. MANAGEMENT IMPLICATION: this is the type
  negatively related to performance -- expect disengagement,
  not loyalty-driven effort; a manager who mistakes Robert's
  presence for satisfaction will be surprised by his output.

AIDEN — normative commitment ("I ought to stay").
  Explicitly framed around a felt obligation from the
  certification investment and not letting the team down.
  MANAGEMENT IMPLICATION: normative commitment is fostered
  further by continued identification with the mission and
  clear socialization -- but a manager should not assume it
  will convert to affective commitment automatically.

TAKEAWAY: identical tenure and identical "staying" behaviour
  mask three very different underlying attitudes, with three
  different performance implications -- treating them as
  interchangeable "loyal employees" would be a mistake.

The workplace itself keeps changing, and Meyer, Allen, and Laryssa Topolnytsky trace the impact of demographic shifts, technology, and global competition on commitment through three main areas: changes in the nature of each type of commitment following organizational change (maintaining high affective commitment through a major change is particularly difficult); changes in the workforce's overall commitment profile; and the ongoing challenge of fostering commitment in a workforce with more contingent, less continuous employment relationships than a generation ago.

Exam Trap — Which Commitment Type Hurts Performance? "Commitment" sounds uniformly positive in everyday language, which is exactly what makes this a favourite trap. If a question asks which type of commitment can be negatively related to performance, the answer is continuance commitment — not affective, and not normative. Don't let the generally positive connotation of the word "commitment" talk you out of the correct, counterintuitive answer.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Name the Dimension

A Canadian firm opens a subsidiary in a country where employees routinely wait for the most senior person in the room to speak first, rarely question a manager's decision in a meeting, and are visibly uncomfortable when asked to challenge a superior's plan directly. Which Hofstede dimension best explains this pattern, and where would this country sit on it relative to Canada?

Show answer
This is power distance. The described behaviours — deferring to the senior person, not questioning authority, discomfort challenging a superior — are the textbook markers of a large-power-distance culture, where inequality is accepted as natural and superiors are comparatively inaccessible. Canada ranks on the low-power-distance side of the average (15th of 76 countries measured), so this country would sit well above Canada on the power-distance spectrum, similar to the pattern Hofstede documents for the Philippines, Russia, or Mexico.
Problem 2 — Which Is NOT

Which of the following is NOT one of the textbook's key contributors to job satisfaction?
a) Mentally challenging work   b) Adequate compensation   c) Job title prestige   d) Career opportunities

Show answer
(c) Job title prestige is not one of the textbook's named contributors. The actual list is mentally challenging work, meaningful work, adequate compensation, career opportunities, and friendly/helpful people. "Job title prestige" is a plausible-sounding distractor because status-related concerns are discussed elsewhere in the chapter (e.g., generational differences), but the textbook does not list title prestige itself among the core satisfaction contributors — and people specifically tend to overestimate status- and pay-related factors while underestimating meaningful, challenging work.
Problem 3 — Compare Two Concepts

A manager says: "I don't understand why Chidi is dissatisfied — he's paid exactly what the job posting promised." Explain why this reasoning conflates discrepancy theory with equity theory, and describe a scenario where the two theories would predict different satisfaction outcomes for the same employee.

Show answer
The manager is reasoning purely in discrepancy theory terms — comparing what Chidi was promised (wanted) to what he received — and concluding there's no gap, so satisfaction should follow. But equity theory asks a separate question: how does Chidi's outcome/input ratio compare to a relevant comparison other's ratio? Chidi could receive exactly what he was promised (zero discrepancy) and still be dissatisfied if he perceives a coworker with lower inputs (less experience, less education) receiving the same or better outcomes — a "short end of the stick" inequity that discrepancy theory alone would completely miss, because discrepancy theory never references another person's ratio at all. The two theories are both "gap" theories but compare against fundamentally different reference points: your own wants (discrepancy) versus someone else's ratio (equity).
Problem 4 — Theorist Attribution

Match each concept to the researcher(s) the textbook credits: (a) the three-component model of commitment (b) the original four (later five) dimensions of national culture    Researchers: (i) Geert Hofstede (ii) John Meyer and Natalie Allen

Show answer
(a) The three-component model of commitment → (ii) John Meyer and Natalie Allen, the Canadian researchers who identified affective, continuance, and normative commitment as distinct types. (b) The original four (later five, with Michael Bond) dimensions of national culture → (i) Geert Hofstede, based on his survey of more than 116,000 IBM employees. A common distractor pairing swaps these two credits, or attributes the GLOBE project (Robert House) to either researcher — keep all three names and their specific contributions separate.
Problem 5 — Application: Rising Turnover After an Acquisition

A mid-size Canadian firm acquired a smaller competitor eight months ago. Since the acquisition, employee turnover at the acquired subsidiary has nearly tripled. Exit interviews reveal a consistent story: the new parent company standardized pay scales (most acquired employees' pay stayed the same or rose slightly), but decisions about which roles would be eliminated or restructured were made at head office with no local consultation and communicated via a company-wide email with no individual follow-up. Several long-tenured employees who kept their jobs have told their manager, "I'm still here, but only because I can't afford to lose my pension right now." Using concepts from this chapter, diagnose what is driving the turnover and the disengagement among those who stayed, and recommend specific fixes.

Show answer
Diagnosis — fairness: Distributive fairness is largely intact (pay stayed the same or improved), but procedural fairness is low — decisions were made centrally with no local consultation, no visible consistent criteria, and no opportunity to appeal, violating the core procedural-fairness factors (consistency, unbiased two-way communication, appeal rights). Interactional fairness is also low — a company-wide email with no individual follow-up is neither respectful nor genuinely informative communication about a change this significant to individual employees. This matches the pattern where a fair-looking outcome fails to prevent dissatisfaction because the process and the treatment around it were poor.

Diagnosis — consequences of dissatisfaction: The turnover spike is consistent with the textbook's turnover model: procedural/interactional unfairness lowers satisfaction and commitment, which (combined with the "shock" of the acquisition itself) pushes turnover intentions into actual departures. The employees who stayed but say they can't afford to leave are describing continuance commitment with likely low affective commitment — precisely the combination the textbook flags as worst for both employee and employer, since continuance commitment is negatively related to performance. Expect reduced OCB among this group even before any further turnover shows up, since withdrawal of informal citizenship behaviour is typically the earliest response to unfair treatment.

Recommendations: Rebuild procedural fairness going forward — involve local managers in any further restructuring decisions, apply consistent, explained criteria, and create a real channel to raise concerns or appeal decisions. Address interactional fairness immediately: replace mass-email communication with direct, individual conversations for any decision affecting a specific employee, delivered by someone empowered to actually answer questions. For the "trapped" continuance-committed group, the fix is not more pay (distributive fairness is not the core problem) but rebuilding affective commitment through role clarity, meaningful involvement in the post-acquisition plan, and consistent follow-through on any commitments made — converting "I can't afford to leave" back into "I want to stay" is the only lever likely to also restore their performance and OCB, not just their attendance.
