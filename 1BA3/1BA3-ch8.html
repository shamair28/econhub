Why This Chapter Matters

The chapter opens at Steam Whistle Brewing, an independent Toronto microbrewery started by three friends in 1998 in the historic John Street Roundhouse. From day one the founders wanted a work-hard-play-hard culture in which hard work is paired with fun and friendships, and they concluded that to make a great beer they first had to build an industry-leading culture. Leaders’ desks sit in the same open-concept area as everyone else’s, and brewery tours walk straight through the offices: a visible sign of openness and collaboration. CEO Greg Taylor stresses being accessible, admitting mistakes and spending most of his time one-on-one with people, because a culture like this is easy to set up but hard to maintain.

Maintaining it starts with hiring. The process can involve up to five interviews, improv games and a meet-and-greet with future co-workers, with a heavy focus on culture fit; the company leans on employee referrals and promotion from within, wants outgoing, hard-working people who care about quality (“The Good Beer Folks”), and values storytelling. After a year, employees can buy shares and join the profit-sharing program, and Steam Whistle will work patiently with people who drift from its values—but will let them go if they do not come back. Retention is high, with 5- and 10-year anniversaries celebrated by team trips to Munich for Oktoberfest.

Almost every idea in this chapter is visible in that story. The founders’ imprint, the shared values and the stories are culture (Sections 8–9). The careful hiring, the meet-and-greet and the ownership stake are socialization (Sections 3–7). The way co-workers and leaders shape what is acceptable is social influence (Section 2). The chapter simply takes these in order: how people influence each other, how newcomers are socialized, and how culture is built, kept and read.

Social Influence in Organizations (8.1)

Chapter 7 showed that groups exert influence over their members’ attitudes and behaviour. Chapter 8 asks what accounts for that influence, and the short answer is dependence: in many social settings, especially groups, people are highly dependent on others, and that dependence sets the stage for influence. The text names two kinds.

Information dependence
Reliance on others for information about how to think, feel and act. We often cannot get objective, concrete answers to questions like “How satisfying is my job?” or “How much work should I take home this weekend?”, so we rely on what others tell us, and that gives others the opportunity to influence us through the signals they send. This works through social information processing theory: organizational members use information from others to interpret events and to develop expectations about appropriate and acceptable attitudes and behaviours. We compare our own thoughts, feelings and actions with those of others, and the effect of social information can be as strong as, or stronger than, objective reality.
Effect dependence
Reliance on others due to their capacity to provide rewards and punishments. It involves two complementary processes: (1) the group often has a vested interest in how members think and act, because that can affect the group’s goal attainment; and (2) members often desire the group’s approval. Managers have promotions, raises and more or less favourable tasks. Co-workers have an even larger informal toolkit: praise, friendship and a helping hand for cooperation; punishment or exclusion for non-cooperation.

The Social Influence Process and Conformity

One obvious result of information and effect dependence is that group members conform to the social norms their group has established. (Chapter 7 covered how norms develop; this chapter explains why they are supported.) Much of the information and many of the effects that members depend on are aimed at enforcing norms. The text separates three motives for social conformity and follows one person through all three: Mark, an idealistic social work graduate who loves helping people but hates his agency’s red tape.

1. Compliance
Conformity to a social norm prompted by the desire to acquire rewards or avoid punishment. It is the simplest, most direct motive and primarily involves effect dependence. Mark follows the rules to the letter to win his boss’s and co-workers’ approval and avoid trouble. Complying individuals adjust their behaviour, but they do not really subscribe to the beliefs, values and attitudes underlying the norm.
2. Identification
Conformity to a social norm prompted by perceptions that those who promote the norm are attractive or similar to oneself. Mark starts to identify with his boss and senior co-workers, who control the rewards and punishments he cares about: to be “one of them,” he must think and feel like them. It is often revealed by imitation, with established members serving as models. There are elements of effect dependence, but information dependence is especially important: if someone is similar to you, you are motivated to rely on that person for information about how to think and act.
3. Internalization
Conformity to a social norm prompted by true acceptance of the beliefs, values and attitudes that underlie the norm. Mark is promoted and tells a new hire that the rules and paperwork really are important. Conformity happens because it is seen as right, not to win rewards, avoid punishment or please anyone: it is due to internal rather than external forces, and (as the chapter summary puts it) the person is no longer conforming simply because of social dependence.
Memory Aid — Carrot → Crush → Creed Compliance = Carrot (and stick): you conform for rewards and to avoid punishment; nothing changes inside you. Identification = Crush: you conform because you admire or resemble the person modelling the norm, so you imitate them. Internalization = Creed: the norm has become your own belief, so you would follow it unobserved. The dependence cue runs along the same line: carrot is effect dependence; crush leans on information dependence; creed has outgrown dependence. Exam trap: read for why the person conforms, not for what they do—the same behaviour (following the rules) can be compliance, identification or internalization. Self-test: Mark follows the rules (a) to avoid trouble, (b) because he wants to be like his boss, (c) because he now thinks they matter. Name each.
Vocabulary Trap — Identification vs. Organizational Identification Identification here (Section 2) is a motive for conforming: you copy people you find attractive or similar. Organizational identification (Section 3) is a socialization outcome: the extent to which you define yourself in terms of the organization and what it represents. The words overlap, the concepts do not. Also keep compliance apart from agreement: a complying person need not believe a word of it.

Organizational Socialization (8.2)

Socialization is the process by which people learn the attitudes, knowledge and behaviours that are necessary to function in a group or organization. It is a learning process: new members must acquire information and knowledge, change their attitudes and perform new behaviours. Acquiring information matters because newcomers face high uncertainty about their job and organization. This is uncertainty reduction theory: newcomers are motivated to reduce their uncertainty so that the work environment becomes more predictable and understandable. Information, resources and interaction with organizational members all reduce that uncertainty and help newcomers adjust. Socialization is also the primary means by which organizations communicate their culture and values to new members.

What Newcomers Learn: Four Domains

Learning during socialization is often described by content area. Newcomers must learn the knowledge and skills for their duties (task), the appropriate behaviours and expectations of their position (role), the norms and values of their work group, and the history, traditions, language, politics, mission and culture of the organization. As they learn each area they master their tasks and integrate with others, which also helps reduce role ambiguity and role conflict (the Chapter 7 concepts): one goal of socialization is to give new hires the information about their roles that prevents those problems and gives them role clarity (13th ed.), a clear understanding of their responsibilities, goals and priorities. Role clarity is a key indicator of newcomer adjustment and a predictor of distal outcomes.

Role clarity is the opposite of the Chapter 7 role ambiguity: ambiguity is lack of clarity about goals or methods; clarity is a firm grasp of responsibilities, goals and priorities.

Research finds that PJ, PO and PG fit are strongly influenced by the socialization process and are related to job attitudes and behaviours. A primary goal of socialization is also that new employees learn and understand the key beliefs, values and assumptions of the culture and define themselves in terms of the organization and what it is perceived to represent: organizational identification, a distal outcome that reflects an individual’s learning and acceptance of the culture.

Vocabulary Trap — PJ, PO and PG Are Easy to Swap Ask what is being matched to what. PJ = skills to the job. PO = values to the organization. PG = values to the work group. Only PJ is about abilities; both PO and PG are about values, and they differ in whose values (the whole organization’s or the immediate group’s). Example: a brilliant programmer who loves the work but dislikes the company’s sales-first values has high PJ and low PO fit; one who shares the company’s values but clashes with her own team’s way of working has high PO and low PG fit.

Socialization is an ongoing process, continuing through interaction with others, but it is most potent during membership transitions: being promoted, being assigned to a new work group or department, and especially joining a new organization.

Stages of Socialization (Feldman)

Because socialization is ongoing, it helps to divide it into three stages (Exhibit 8.2, based on Feldman): one before entry, one right after, and one after the person has been a member for some time. In a sense, the first two stages are hurdles to be cleared for passage into the third.

  1. Anticipatory socialization (pre-entry). A great deal of socialization happens before a person joins. Some is formal skill and attitude acquisition (attending college or university); some is informal (summer jobs, or television and movie portrayals of organizational life). Some organizations start socializing candidates before hiring, at recruitment events where representatives discuss the organization. Organizations vary in how much they encourage it, and not all anticipatory socialization is accurate or useful.
  2. Encounter. The recruit, armed with expectations, meets the day-to-day reality. Formal aspects include orientation programs and rotation through parts of the organization; informal aspects include getting to know the style and personality of the boss and co-workers. The organization wants an acceptable degree of conformity to norms and the gradual acquisition of appropriate role behaviour; recruits want their personal needs and expectations met. If it goes well, the recruit will have complied with critical norms and should begin to identify with experienced members.
  3. Role management (settling in). Having survived the encounter and acquired basic role behaviours, attention shifts to fine-tuning and actively managing the role. After some conformity to group norms, the newcomer may be able to modify the role to better serve the organization (which may mean forming connections outside the immediate work group), and must balance the now-familiar work role against non-work roles and family demands. These experiences provide further socialization, and the newcomer begins to internalize prominent norms and values.
Vocabulary Trap — Which Stage Is This? Exam stems describe a situation, not a stage name. Before the first day (college, summer jobs, TV, recruitment events, a job preview) = anticipatory. First weeks, orientation, reality meeting expectations, learning the boss’s style, the organization wanting conformity = encounter. Settled, tweaking the job, building contacts beyond the team, juggling work and family = role management. Notice that the stages line up with Section 2: encounter is where compliance and early identification happen; role management is where internalization begins. Unrealistic Expectations and the Psychological Contract (8.3)

People seldom join organizations without expectations about what membership will be like and what they will get for their efforts; those expectations are part of why they choose one career, job or organization over another. Management majors have expectations about being managers; even 18-year-old army recruits have notions about military life. Unfortunately, these expectations are often unrealistic, and agreements between new members and organizations are often breached.

Unrealistic Expectations and Reality Shock

People entering organizations hold many expectations that are inaccurate and often unrealistically high. Once inside, they find that their expectations are not being met and experience reality shock: when the reality of a new job and organization is inconsistent with, and does not meet, one’s expectations. In a study of telephone operators, many perceptions of the actual job shortly after starting were less favourable than expectations held before; the same was found for students entering an MBA program. The extent to which expectations are met matters: newcomers with higher met expectations have higher job satisfaction, organizational commitment, job performance and job survival, and lower intentions to quit.

The text gives three reasons new members have unrealistic expectations:

  • Occupational stereotypes (Chapter 3), often spread by the media: a person entering nursing training might form expectations of hospital life from Grey’s Anatomy.
  • What educators communicate: after four years of study, the new management trainee at IBM may be dismayed to find the emphasis is on trainee rather than management.
  • Overzealous recruiters who paint rosy pictures to attract candidates.

Together these show the need for socialization and for helping recruits acquire more realistic expectations.

The Psychological Contract

When people join organizations they have beliefs about what they will receive in return for what they give. The psychological contract is the beliefs held by employees regarding the reciprocal obligations and promises between them and their organization; for example, expecting bonuses and promotions in return for hard work and loyalty. Perceptions of psychological contract breach arise when employees perceive that the organization has failed to fulfill one or more of its promises or obligations. Breach is common: one study found that 55 percent of MBA graduates reported that some aspect of their psychological contract had been broken by their employers. It often produces anger and betrayal.

A review of the research found that breach is related to three groups of outcomes, all due to negative emotions that stem from feelings of violation and mistrust toward management:

Type of outcomeEffect of perceived breach
Affective reactionsHigher feelings of contract violation and mistrust toward management
Work attitudesLower job satisfaction and organizational commitment; higher turnover intentions
Work behavioursLower organizational citizenship behaviour and job performance

Breach has also been associated with less innovation-related behaviour (such as coming up with new ideas) and lower customer satisfaction, both costly to organizations.

Memory Aid — Breach Hits Your ABCs Affect (violation, mistrust) → Attitudes (less satisfaction and commitment, more intention to quit) → Behaviours (less citizenship behaviour, lower performance, and less innovation). Feelings come first and drive the other two. Trap: turnover intentions are an attitude; actual performance and citizenship are behaviours. Self-test: a worker promised training that never comes says “I don’t trust them anymore” (A), “I’m updating my résumé” (A), and stops volunteering for extra tasks (B).

Why Breach Occurs and How to Reduce It

Breach happens for three reasons. Recruiters are tempted to promise more than the organization can provide to attract the best applicants. Newcomers often lack the information to form accurate perceptions, so employee and organization understand the promises differently (incongruence). And organizational changes such as downsizing and restructuring can lead organizations to knowingly break promises they are unable or unwilling to keep. To reduce it, organizations should communicate truthful, accurate information about promises and obligations before and after people join (many terms of the contract are set during anticipatory socialization). Breach is less likely where socialization is intense; what organizations actually give employees is most important; and newcomers with higher personal obligations are more inclined to take part in, and benefit from, socialization programs.

Vocabulary Trap — Reality Shock vs. Psychological Contract Breach Reality shock is about expectations: the job is not what you pictured (the expectations may have come from TV, stereotypes or a recruiter’s sales pitch, with no specific promise). Breach is about promises and obligations: you perceive that the organization failed to deliver something it owed you. They overlap (a recruiter’s inflated pitch can cause both) but they are not the same: a layoff can break a promise even when your expectations were perfectly realistic. And a realistic job preview (Section 5) is the fix for the first, with some evidence that it also helps prevent the second. When a stem says the organization “failed to keep a promise,” answer breach.

Section 5

Methods of Socialization I: Realistic Job Previews, Orientation and ROPES (8.4)

Organizations differ in who does the socializing, how it is done and how much is done. Some rely on other organizations for anticipatory socialization: hospitals depend on medical schools to socialize future doctors, and business firms rely on business schools. Others, like police forces, the military and religious institutions, run their own (police academies, boot camps, seminaries). Organizations that handle their own socialization are especially interested in the continuity and stability of job behaviours and culture; those that rely on external agencies are oriented toward preserving the potential for creative, innovative behaviour (less “inbreeding”). External reliance has its own risks (the engineer taught to respect design elegance may struggle with cost limits), so such organizations always add formal training and orientation or on-the-job training.

Most organizations use several methods: realistic job previews, employee orientation programs, socialization tactics and mentoring (Exhibit 8.1 adds newcomers’ own proactive behaviours). What they share is that each provides resources to newcomers. According to socialization resources theory, providing newcomers with resources throughout the socialization process facilitates their adjustment and successful socialization. The text lists the resources: information, feedback, supervisor support, recognition, training, challenging assignments and developing relationships with organizational members (13th ed.) (the 12th-ed. list also named orientation and socialization agents; the 13th drops them). As you read each method, ask which resources it delivers and when.

Realistic Job Previews (RJPs)

New members often have inflated expectations; when the job fails to live up to them they experience reality shock, dissatisfaction and, most likely among new employees, costly turnover. For the organization that is a socialization failure. Organizations cannot control all sources of unrealistic expectations (television, glorified stereotypes), but they can control the ones generated during recruitment. A realistic job preview is the provision of a balanced, realistic picture of the positive and negative aspects of a job to applicants: a “corrective action” at the anticipatory stage.

To design one, organizations gather the views of experienced employees and HR staff on the pros and cons and turn them into booklets or videos (for example, interviews with job incumbents discussing both sides), or have managers and employees describe the job in person. RJPs have been designed for bank tellers, life insurance salespeople, U.S. Marine Corps recruits and supermarket workers. Some use simulations that let applicants sample the work: the Ontario Provincial Police ran recruiting camps for women with a handgun session, 6 a.m. fitness drills and mock crimes.

Research evidence. RJPs are effective in reducing inflated expectations and turnover and in improving job performance. They cause people not cut out for the job, or with low PJ and PO fit perceptions, to withdraw from the application process (self-selection), so applicants who perceive a good fit are more likely to accept and stay. Organizations that provide RJPs are seen by applicants as more honest and trustworthy, which encourages them to stay once hired. The turnover reductions are small but can mean substantial financial savings, so an RJP is a low-investment strategy; there is also some evidence it can help prevent perceived psychological contract breach.

Employee Orientation Programs and ROPES

Once newcomers enter, encounter-stage socialization usually begins with an orientation program: a program designed to introduce new employees to their job, the people they will work with, and the organization. Typical content: health and safety, terms and conditions of employment, and information about the organization such as its history and traditions. Orientation also begins to convey and form the psychological contract and teaches newcomers to cope with stressful situations. Most programs happen in the first week and last one day to one week.

An orientation program designed to help newcomers cope with stress is the Realistic Orientation Program for Entry Stress (ROPES). Like an RJP, ROPES gives newcomers realistic information about tasks and the organization, but it also teaches them cognitive and behavioural coping techniques for managing workplace stressors.

Examples and evidence. Capital Power (an Edmonton power producer) built an orientation program called “Strong Start,” combining interactive e-learning modules with a day-and-a-half classroom session that includes a talk from the CEO or another senior executive, and requiring each new hire to make a 100-day development plan with their manager; the company saw a 30 percent increase in organizational knowledge among participants. Research shows orientation has an immediate effect on learning and a lasting effect on attitudes and behaviours: oriented employees were more socialized and reported higher commitment, and at Corning those who completed a full orientation were 69 percent more likely to remain with the company for at least three years. Research on ROPES finds it lowers participants’ expectations and stress and improves adjustment and retention.

Vocabulary Trap — RJP vs. Orientation vs. ROPES RJP = a balanced preview given to applicants, before the job offer is accepted (anticipatory stage). Orientation program = an introduction given to new employees once they have entered (encounter stage). ROPES = an orientation program that adds coping techniques for entry stress to realistic information; it is not “orientation with a fancier name.” Also note a slide typo: slide 10 spells ROPES as “Employee Orientation Program for Entry Stress.” The textbook term, and the one to use on the exam, is Realistic Orientation Program for Entry Stress.

Research Evidence: Why Institutionalized Tactics Work

  • Institutionalized tactics are related to proximal outcomes such as lower role ambiguity and role conflict and more positive PJ and PO fit perceptions, and to distal outcomes such as greater job satisfaction, organizational commitment and job performance, and lower stress and turnover.
  • They result in a custodial role orientation, in which new hires accept the status quo and the requirements of their tasks and roles. Individualized tactics result in an innovative role orientation, in which recruits may change or modify how they perform their tasks and roles.
  • Reasons: they give newcomers information that reduces uncertainty, and they also satisfy newcomers’ basic psychological needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness (13th ed.) (self-determination theory, Chapter 5).
  • Among the six, the social tactics (serial–disjunctive and investiture–divestiture) are the most strongly related to socialization outcomes, consistent with findings that successful socializers help newcomers build a broad network of relationships with co-workers.
  • Institutionalized socialization is always followed by some individualized socialization when new members join their regular work unit: rookie police officers partnered with experienced officers begin to develop individuality in their style.

Mentoring and Proactive Socialization (8.4)

The tactics show that supervisors and peers play a big part in socialization. One relationship is especially important: between a newcomer, or protégé, and a mentor: an experienced or more senior person in the organization who provides a junior person with guidance and special attention, such as giving advice and creating opportunities, especially in the early stages of the junior person’s career. Someone other than the junior person’s boss can be the mentor, but the supervisor is often in a unique position to do it. Mentoring is a developmental relationship that benefits a protégé’s work and/or career, and for mentors to be effective they must perform two types of developmental functions. (Slide 18 gives the same definition and abbreviates the two functions to “career (coach, feedback)” and “psychosocial (role model, counseling).”)

Formal Mentoring Programs and Developmental Networks

Mentoring was long informal: the two people chose each other without the organization. Formal mentoring programs are organization-sponsored programs in which seasoned employees are recruited as mentors and matched with protégés, and have become increasingly popular. Telvent Canada Ltd. (Calgary) began one for new hires and later opened it to all employees; Bell Canada launched an online program called Mentor Match on its intranet, where employees applied to be a mentor or protégé.

Many newcomers have more than one mentor. Developmental networks are groups of people who take an active interest in a protégé’s career and act to advance it by providing developmental assistance. Unlike traditional one-mentor, one-protégé mentoring, a protégé can have multiple developers inside and outside the organization (including family and community) and from different levels (peers, superiors, subordinates, senior managers). With a network, a newcomer is more likely to receive different kinds of support (career and psychosocial) and to realize a broader range of career outcomes.

Women, Race, Ethnicity and Mentoring

  • Women. One factor that has inhibited women’s career development is the difficulty of finding a mentor. The senior people best placed to mentor are frequently men, who are more likely to serve as mentors; a woman’s concerns also differ from those her male mentor faced, so the strategies he models may have limited relevance. Cross-gender pairs were less likely to share informal after-work activities (which build relationships with influential people) and less likely to see the mentor as a role model. A survey of female professionals found only 27 percent felt they had a mentor. However, formal programs have removed many barriers: men and women are equally likely to have been protégés; female protégés report equal career mentoring and more psychosocial support; and the negative effects of cross-gender pairs fade as the relationship develops. Mentoring appears even more critical to women’s career success (women who reach executive positions have invariably had a mentor, versus one-half to two-thirds of male executives; 61 percent of women have had a mentor and 99 percent of those say it affected their advancement), and in male-dominated industries women benefit most from a senior male mentor.
  • Diversity Focus: Mentoring in the Wake of #MeToo. (The 13th ed. labels this box a Diversity Focus; the 12th called it a Research Focus.) A study by French, Mortensen and Timming surveyed female employees and male and female managers. A majority of the women were willing to be mentored by an older male co-worker or manager, but most were unwilling or uncomfortable with more intimate one-on-one interactions. Male managers were significantly less likely than female managers to mentor female employees or work one-on-one with them behind closed doors, so the authors concluded that women may face a mentoring disadvantage in the wake of #MeToo.
  • Race and ethnicity. Limited diversity at higher levels constrains mentoring for younger employees from diverse backgrounds. Mentors tend to select protégés similar to them in race, nationality and gender, and cross-ethnic pairs tend to report less assistance. Cross-race relationships focus on career (instrumental) functions and provide fewer psychosocial functions (role modelling, counselling), so diverse members need to build extra peer networks for emotional support and role modelling, and organizations should offer mentoring and networking as part of diversity strategy (the Black Female Lawyers Network is an example of a support group).

Research evidence. A review found that mentored individuals had higher objective career outcomes (compensation, promotions) and higher subjective outcomes (satisfaction with job and career, career commitment), and were more likely to believe they would advance. Mentoring relates more strongly to subjective than to objective outcomes. Comparing the two functions, the psychosocial function is more strongly related to satisfaction with the mentoring relationship, while the career function is more strongly related to compensation and advancement; both are equally important for positive attitudes toward job and career. Formal programs are just as beneficial as informal mentoring (and better than none), and work best when mentor and protégé have input into the matching and receive high-quality training beforehand.

Research Focus (13th ed.): Socialization Programs and Newcomer Retention

A 2024 meta-analysis by Liu and colleagues pooled 83 field experiments with employees, students and military recruits and found that socialization programs made the odds of newcomer retention 1.46 times higher. The programs had six components, and all six worked:

  1. Presenting a realistic job preview.
  2. Improving coping skills.
  3. Encouraging proactivity.
  4. Cultivating organizational knowledge and identity.
  5. Identifying effective task behaviours.
  6. Facilitating social integration.

The most effective bundle was encouraging proactivity + identifying effective task behaviours + facilitating social integration, because together they meet newcomers’ needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness (self-determination theory, Chapter 5). Delivery also mattered: programs delivered in person beat those that were not, and staggered, multi-session programs beat one-shot ones. Exam angle: the finding ties RJPs (component 1), ROPES-style coping (2) and the proactive behaviours (3) together, and supports the Section 6 point that meeting the three psychological needs is why structured socialization works.

Organizational Culture and Strong Cultures (8.5)

Socialization both depends on and shapes the organization’s culture, a concept that has attracted researchers and managers alike. Culture has strong effects on attitudes and behaviour, and many employees place more emphasis on a strong organizational culture than on compensation. The text opens with a story: on the day before game seven of the 2011 Stanley Cup final, a customer-service representative emailed Coast Capital Savings CEO Tracy Redies asking to close extended-hours branches early so staff could watch the game with their families. Caring is part of Coast Capital’s culture, so within hours the branches closed early and call-centre staff got large-screen TVs and pizza, and the hundreds of thrilled replies show how culture shows up in everyday decisions.

What Is Organizational Culture?

Informally, culture is an organization’s style, atmosphere or personality; the contrast between Amazon, Uber, WestJet, Google Canada and Steam Whistle makes it obvious, and culture gives organizations uniqueness and social identity. More formally, organizational culture is the shared beliefs, values and assumptions that exist in an organization; these in turn determine the norms that develop and the behaviour patterns that follow. “Shared” does not necessarily mean members closely agree: it means they have had uniform exposure and have some minimum common understanding. Slide 3 gives the same definition, plus the uniqueness-and-social-identity point and the first two characteristics below. The text lists five characteristics:

  • A true “way of life” for members, who often take its influence for granted; it often becomes obvious only when contrasted with other organizations or when it changes.
  • Fairly stable over time, because it involves basic assumptions, values and beliefs; once established it persists despite membership turnover, providing social continuity.
  • Its content can be internal (supporting innovation, risk taking or secrecy) or external (“putting the customer first,” or behaving unethically toward competitors).
  • Culture is a resource or “toolkit” (13th ed.) that members draw on to solve problems, make decisions and build strategies of action.
  • It can have a strong impact on both organizational performance and member satisfaction.

Culture is a social variable, not an automatic result of technology, products or size: large organizations tend toward bureaucracy, but one large organization’s culture may still be informal and non-bureaucratic.

Subcultures, and the Gap Between Envisioned and Experienced Culture

Subcultures are smaller cultures that develop within a larger organizational culture and are based on differences in training, occupation or departmental goals (slide 20 adds that they might differ across faculties or departments). A study of Silicon Valley computer companies found “hardware types” (split into engineers and technicians) and “software types” (software engineers and computer scientists), each with its own values and assumptions. Effective organizations build an overarching culture that manages such divisions; for instance, a shared norm of fighting hard until a design is chosen and then pulling together.

There can also be a gap between the culture leaders envision and the culture employees experience. In one survey, leaders wanted a culture valuing innovation, initiative, candour and teamwork, while employees said they actually experienced obedience, predictability, deference to authority and competition with peers. Such a gap is unlikely to produce a strong culture. (Slide 21 added this point to the lecture.)

The “Strong Culture” Concept

In a strong culture, the beliefs, values and assumptions are both intense and pervasive across the organization: strongly supported by the majority of members, even cutting across subcultures, so there is great consensus about “what the organization is about.” In weak cultures they are less strongly ingrained or less widely shared; weak cultures are fragmented and have less impact on members. All organizations have a culture, though the details of weak ones are hard to detect. The text offers sketches of strong cultures:

  • Hilti Canada: a can-do culture built around “Gung Ho!” and the “Our Culture Journey” program; most employees take a mandatory two-day Culture Journey, and recruits get two days of culture training before product and sales training.
  • Google Canada: collaboration, a flat structure, small focused teams, shared kitchens, and “20 percent time” for passion projects; it even has a chief culture officer.
  • Shopify: a director of culture, a culture team and a culture specialist in each main office; new-hire orientation opens with a culture discussion; open spaces and quarterly “hack days.”
  • Steam Whistle (the opening vignette) and, in the lecture, WestJet Airlines (slide 21), whose culture the text ties to its profitability in the “financial success” asset below.
Vocabulary Trap — What “Strong” Does and Does Not Mean The text makes three points: (1) an organization need not be big to have a strong culture; (2) a strong culture does not necessarily mean blind conformity (3M’s strong culture supports and rewards nonconformity in the form of innovation); and (3) strong cultures are generally associated with greater success—but see the liabilities below, because a strong culture can also be a bad one. “Strong” = intense and pervasive; it does not mean “good,” “large” or “uniform behaviour.”

Assets and Liabilities of Strong Cultures

Asset 1: Coordination
The overarching values of a strong culture help the right hand (finance) know what the left hand (production) is doing, so parts of the organization learn from each other and coordinate; especially important in decentralized, team-oriented organizations. At Google Canada: places to socialize, weekly TGIF meetings and 20 percent time.
Asset 2: Conflict resolution
Intensity might seem likely to produce friction, but shared core values can be a “light in a storm.” A firm with the core value of excellent customer service may still disagree on a customer problem, but the value suggests the mechanism: let the person closest to the customer make the final decision.
Asset 3: Financial success
Strong cultures contribute to financial success when the culture supports the mission, strategy and goals of the organization. WestJet: a universal desire to maximize profits, the most successful low-cost carrier in Canadian history, and executives consistently rank it among the most admired corporate cultures in Canada. The most admired cultures had three-year average revenue growth that outpaced the S&P/TSX compound annual growth rate by over 600 percent.
Liability 1: Resistance to change
A strong culture suited to past success may not support a new mission or strategy, and strong consensus can resist change, damaging the firm’s ability to innovate. IBM’s strong mainframe culture bred complacency and limited its competitiveness in desktop computing, software and systems compatibility; Oracle’s hyperaggressive sales culture became its own worst enemy when the industry changed, and Larry Ellison scrapped the incentive system that encouraged furious sales pushes.
Liability 2: Culture clash
Strong cultures can mix “as badly as oil and water” when a merger or acquisition puts two under one banner. When HBC bought 50 Towers stores for conversion to Zellers, almost every Towers store manager quit, because Towers treated employees very positively and they feared being forced to treat them badly. Hewlett-Packard and Compaq differed (careful and engineering-oriented versus fast and sales-oriented), and Compaq had already suffered a clash in its 1998 merger with Digital Equipment Corporation.
Liability 3: Pathology
A strong culture can threaten effectiveness because it is in some sense pathological: built on beliefs that support infighting, secrecy or paranoia. Enron (valued lies and deception), WorldCom (secrecy and blind obedience), Livent (a “cheating culture” in the judge’s words), NASA before the Columbia disaster (downplayed risk and suppressed dissent), and the RCMP (a culture of silence around harassment).

Contributors to the Culture and Diagnosing a Culture (8.6–8.7)

How are cultures built and maintained? The text names two key factors: the founder’s role and socialization. (Take a moment to find both in the Steam Whistle vignette.)

The Founder’s Role and Top Management

Cultures can emerge without a key individual, but many strong cultures reflect their founder’s values: Walt Disney at Disney, Sam Walton at Walmart, Ray Kroc at McDonald’s, Thomas Watson at IBM, Michael Lee-Chin (“doing well and doing good”) at Portland Holdings, John Stanton at The Running Room, Mary Kay Ash at Mary Kay and Bill Gates at Microsoft, as well as the founders of Steam Whistle. That imprint is often kept alive by stories about the founder passed to successive generations of employees. Top management also strongly shapes culture: it tends to emulate what top management “pays attention to.” IBM under Thomas Watson reflected his personality and was a culture of conformity; under Louis Gerstner, Jr. (CEO 1993–2002), who made diversity a top priority, it became a more people-friendly culture that values individuals’ unique traits and contributions.

Socialization: Pascale’s Seven Steps

Socialization is a key to the culture that emerges, because it is one of the main ways people learn the culture’s beliefs, values and assumptions. Weak or fragmented cultures often feature haphazard selection and a nearly random series of job assignments that give the new hire no coherent experience. By contrast, Richard Pascale notes that strong cultures take great pains to put employees through a careful step-by-step process (Exhibit 8.7):

  1. Selecting employees. Choose people able to adapt to the existing culture, and use realistic job previews so candidates can deselect themselves (self-selection). Procter & Gamble uses individual interviews, group interviews and tests for brand management; Google Canada involves employees in selection; Steam Whistle uses up to five interviews, improv games and a meet-and-greet. More organizations now select for fit with values and culture (PO fit).
  2. Debasement (divestiture socialization tactics). Provoke humility so new hires are open to the organization’s norms.
  3. Training “in the trenches.” Begin at the bottom to master a core area: even experienced MBAs start low, and new MBAs at Lincoln Electric spend eight weeks on the welding line.
  4. Reward and promotion. Carefully reinforce employees who perform well in areas that support the organization’s values and goals (Agrium aligned recognition programs with corporate values; Kicking Horse Coffee gives an award to employees who exhibit cultural values).
  5. Exposure to core culture. Again and again, assert the core beliefs, values and assumptions as guidance for behaviour, to show that the personal sacrifices of socialization have a true purpose.
  6. Organizational folklore. Expose members to stories that reinforce the culture (see Stories below).
  7. Role models. Identify “fast trackers” whose actions and views fit the culture, as tangible examples for newcomers to imitate.

Pascale stresses that it is the consistency among the steps and their mutually reinforcing properties that make for a strong culture. Disney uses many: rigorous selection, grooming standards that serve as mild debasement, everyone starting at the bottom, low pay with promotion tied to performance, folklore stressing core values (“Walt’s in the park”), and better performers as role models at Disney University or in paired training. At Four Seasons, every new hire from hotel managers to dishwashers goes through four interviews and then a three-month socialization program, because the company wants them to buy into the team philosophy and a “service mindset.”

Diagnosing a Culture: Symbols, Rites, Rituals and Ceremonials, and Stories

Culture is a “way of life” that can be hard for uninitiated outsiders to read, even when strong. One way to grasp it is to examine the symbols; rites, rituals and ceremonials; and stories that characterize it (the 13th-ed. section title). For insiders, they are mechanisms that teach, communicate and reinforce the culture.

Symbols
A symbol is any object, act, event, quality or relation that conveys meaning, usually by representing something (13th ed.). At Chaparral Steel (Texas), employees walk through the human resources department to reach their lockers, a powerful symbol of how much the company values HR. A hospital CEO removed the doors from his office and hung them in the lobby, signalling openness and transparency (13th ed.). IBM’s “respect for the individual” held strong symbolic value until its first-ever layoffs shook it. Wells Fargo CEO Carl Reichardt, a fanatical cost cutter, received budget requests while sitting in a tatty chair and picked at its exposed stuffing, a deliberate symbol of austerity (managers also paid for their own coffee and Christmas decorations).
Rites, rituals and ceremonials
Three related terms (13th ed.): rites are elaborate, dramatic, planned activities that consolidate several cultural expressions into one event, carried out through social interaction for an audience; rituals are standardized, detailed sets of techniques, actions and behaviours performed in recognition of an event or accomplishment (a team leader’s company-wide shout-out when someone hits a sales target; a team lunch in a new hire’s first week); a ceremonial is a system of several rites tied to a single occasion. Rituals reinforce norms and values and create psychological safety and purpose, which raises performance: the text’s “three Ps of rituals.” Three kinds of rite: a rite of passage helps a person move into a new social role and often signals the end of socialization; a rite of integration binds members together (Disney picnics, beach parties and employee nights); a rite of enhancement makes people feel good about themselves (Mary Kay’s Hollywood-style “seminars” and pink Cadillacs). More examples: Steam Whistle’s year-end party, BBQ, sports teams and talent show; Flight Centre’s monthly “buzz nights” that recognize accomplishments; Coast Capital’s executives in costumes at the recognition awards event. Rituals need not be exotic: the annual performance review can be an act of feedback and development in one company and an exercise in punishment and debasement in another.
Stories
A story is a narrative based on true events, often a mix of truth and fiction (13th ed.). Stories about past events are told repeatedly to successive generations of new employees to communicate “how things work.” At FedEx, the tale is told of a driver whose drop-box lock froze: he cut the legs off the box and took it to the airport so the packages got there on time (13th ed.). Ray Kroc is said to have cancelled a franchise after finding a single fly in the restaurant: true or not, it shows McDonald’s fanatical dedication to clean premises. At the Rocky Mountain Soap Company, core-value stories are presented at meetings and on posters.

Research finds a few recurring themes under many organizational stories: Is the big boss human? Can the little person rise to the top? Will I get fired? Will the organization help me when I have to move? How will the boss react to mistakes? How will the organization deal with obstacles? Issues of equality, security and control underlie these themes, and stories often come in a “good” version (things turn out well) and a “bad” version (they go sour). The Kroc fly story is a sour ending to the “how will the boss react to mistakes?” theme.

Memory Aid — See It, Do It, Tell It Symbols you see (layouts, objects, a tatty chair, doors in a lobby); rites, rituals and ceremonials you do (awards nights, parties, shout-outs, team lunches); stories you tell (folklore about founders and past events). In a scenario, ask what the observer is doing: looking at a thing (symbol), taking part in an event (rite, ritual or ceremonial) or listening to a retelling (story). The three rite types spell PIE: Passage (into a new role), Integration (binds members), Enhancement (feel good about yourself). Trap: a story need not be true, and a performance review conveys culture through what it is like. Folklore is both a diagnostic tool here and Step 6 of Pascale’s model. Self-test: sort the fly tale, a CEO’s tatty chair and a costume awards night.
Vocabulary Trap — Rite vs. Ritual vs. Ceremonial (13th ed.) A ritual is a standardized set of actions performed to recognize an event or accomplishment (a shout-out, a first-week lunch). A rite is bigger: an elaborate, dramatic, planned event that combines several cultural expressions for an audience. A ceremonial is bigger still: a system of several rites tied to one occasion. Do not confuse the rite types either: a Disney picnic binds members together (integration); a Mary Kay seminar makes people feel good about themselves (enhancement); an event that moves someone into a new role and marks the end of socialization is a rite of passage.

Problem 1 — Why Is She Conforming?

Name the motive for conformity in each case and say which kind of dependence it mainly reflects: (a) A new bank teller follows the dress code exactly because her manager checks and promotions depend on it, though she thinks it is silly. (b) A new auditor starts using the same phrases and note-taking habits as the senior partner he admires, who is a lot like him. (c) After two years, a supervisor insists on the safety checklist even when no one is watching, saying it is simply the right way to work. (d) Exam-style multiple choice: A new paralegal imitates a senior partner mainly because the partner is warm, similar in background and easy to talk to; the paralegal has not yet adopted the firm’s values and is not chasing a specific reward. This is: (A) compliance, (B) identification, (C) internalization, (D) reality shock.

Show answer
(a) Compliance: conformity to win rewards and avoid punishment, with no belief in the norm; mainly effect dependence. (b) Identification: conformity because the norm-promoter is attractive or similar, shown by imitation; information dependence is especially important (with some effect dependence). (c) Internalization: true acceptance, seen as right and driven by internal forces rather than rewards, punishment or pleasing others; it has outgrown social dependence. (d) (B) identification: attraction and similarity with imitation. (A) is ruled out because the question says no reward is being chased (compliance is reward and punishment, effect dependence); (C) is ruled out because the paralegal has not yet adopted the values; (D) is not a conformity motive at all (it is the gap between expectations and reality). Memory check: Carrot, Crush, Creed.
Problem 2 — Broken Promise or Reality Shock?

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: A new hire at a consulting firm is told during recruiting that the job involves “some travel,” but in month one she is on the road four days a week, every week. She feels blindsided and starts job hunting. This best illustrates: (A) role ambiguity, (B) psychological contract breach, (C) the sucker effect, (D) institutionalized socialization. (b) A former co-op student says the recruiting pitch stressed “creative freedom and mentorship,” but the job was repetitive data entry with no mentor and little contact with senior staff. Using two named concepts from the chapter, explain what went wrong on the employer’s side and recommend one fix.

Show answer
(a) (B) psychological contract breach: she perceives the organization failed to fulfill a promise or obligation made in recruiting, producing mistrust and turnover intentions. (A) is wrong: her expectations were clear, just violated. (C) is a Chapter 7 social loafing idea. (D) describes a structured onboarding pattern, not a broken promise. (b) Concept 1, no realistic job preview: the employer oversold the positives instead of giving the balanced picture (positive and negative) that an RJP provides, so the student’s inflated expectations collided with reality shock. Concept 2, psychological contract breach: the recruiting message created a perceived promise of creative work and mentoring that was not delivered, damaging trust and satisfaction, and raising turnover intentions (the ABCs: affect, attitudes, behaviours). Fix: give a genuine RJP before offers (for example, current interns describing both the rewarding and the repetitive parts) and assign a real mentor, even an informal one, from day one, so that the contract formed in anticipatory socialization matches the experience.
Problem 3 — Which Pattern? (And the Slide 16 Trap)

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: Which is an example of an institutionalized tactic? (A) A new hire is paired with a mentor for an open-ended, unstructured period with no set timeline. (B) A cohort of 30 new hires goes through the same fixed-length training program together in a pre-set sequence. (C) A new hire is left to figure out the job largely by trial and error with no assigned guide. (D) A new hire’s onboarding timeline changes unpredictably depending on how quickly they pick things up. (b) A bank trains 40 new tellers together in a classroom for six weeks, in a fixed order of modules, then pairs each with a veteran who shows them the ropes; it hires for the attitudes it already wants. Name each of the six tactics, the overall pattern and the role orientation it should produce. (c) A classmate says “slide 16 says institutionalized socialization uses divestiture.” Which is right for the final, and how should you write it in a Group Assignment answer?

Show answer
(a) (B): collective, formal, sequential and fixed tactics together. (A) is individual, informal and variable (a one-on-one mentor is serial, but the unstructured, untimed set-up makes the whole item individualized); (C) is disjunctive (no one shows the ropes) and individual; (D) is variable. (b) Collective (a cohort together), formal (classroom, apart from regular staff), sequential (fixed order of modules), fixed (six weeks), serial (a veteran shows the ropes), investiture (hires for the attitudes it wants and affirms them). That is institutionalized socialization, which should produce a custodial role orientation: loyalty, uniform service and acceptance of the status quo. (c) The textbook is right: institutionalized = collective, formal, sequential, fixed, serial and investiture; individualized ends with divestiture (Exhibits 8.4–8.5). Slide 16 shows the two swapped. On the final, answer with the textbook. In a Group Assignment, name the tactic and define it (investiture affirms the newcomer’s identity; divestiture, also called debasement, strips it) so your answer is correct whichever labelling the marker has in mind. Hook: IN-stitutionalized keeps you IN-vested.
Problem 4 — Asset or Liability?

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: A firm’s strong culture of rapid, aggressive sales tactics was highly profitable for a decade, but when the market shifted to relationship-based selling, the same culture made change very hard. This illustrates which liability? (A) culture clash, (B) pathology, (C) resistance to change, (D) subculture fragmentation. (b) Classify each as an asset (coordination, conflict resolution or financial success) or a liability (resistance to change, culture clash or pathology): (i) A manager dispute is settled by “let the person closest to the customer decide.” (ii) After a merger, most store managers from the acquired chain quit. (iii) Executives hide information from auditors and are told to follow orders. (iv) Finance and production share information easily because they hold the same core values. (c) True or false, and why: “A strong culture requires a large organization and produces uniform conformity.” (d) (13th ed.) Name the type of rite: (i) a company picnic and beach party for all staff; (ii) a Hollywood-style awards seminar with pink Cadillacs for top sellers; (iii) a ceremony marking the end of a new manager’s first-year program as she takes on her permanent role. What do the three Ps of rituals add up to?

Show answer
(a) (C) resistance to change: a culture that fit past conditions blocks adaptation and damages the ability to innovate (like IBM’s mainframe culture or Oracle’s sales culture). (A) is two cultures colliding, typically in a merger; (B) is a culture whose values are toxic from the start; (D) is not one of the three liabilities. (b) (i) Asset: conflict resolution (shared core values as a tiebreaker). (ii) Liability: culture clash (Towers and Zellers). (iii) Liability: pathology (secrecy and blind obedience, as at WorldCom). (iv) Asset: coordination. Memory check: Coordinate, Calm, Cash; Rigid, Clash, Sick. (c) False on both counts. The text says an organization need not be big to have a strong culture (a small business, school or agency can), and strong cultures do not necessarily produce blind conformity (3M rewards nonconformity in the form of innovation). “Strong” means intense and pervasive beliefs, values and assumptions. (d) (i) Rite of integration (binds members together, like Disney picnics); (ii) rite of enhancement (makes people feel good about themselves, like Mary Kay); (iii) rite of passage (moves a person into a new social role and often signals the end of socialization). The three Ps of rituals: they reinforce norms and values and create psychological safety and purpose, which raises performance.
Problem 5 — Application: Brightline Software’s Leaky Onboarding

Brightline Software, a 150-person company, wants a stronger, more unified culture. Its founder says she wants a culture of “ownership and candour,” yet staff say day to day they experience “do what you are told.” New hires get a laptop and a chat-app invite on day one and nothing else. During recruiting, candidates were promised “creative problem solving and a mentor,” but the typical first job is repetitive support work and no mentors are assigned. About a third leave within six months. One who stayed said: “Nobody told us how things work here, so I copy what the loudest people do and hope it is right.” Write a Group Assignment-style answer that (1) names and defines the relevant concepts, (2) applies them to Brightline, and (3) recommends what the company should change, using Pascale’s steps for at least three of your recommendations.

Show answer
Name & define: (1) Organizational socialization: the process by which people learn the attitudes, knowledge and behaviours necessary to function in an organization, driven by newcomers’ need to reduce uncertainty (uncertainty reduction theory). (2) Unrealistic expectations and reality shock: inflated expectations that the job then fails to meet. (3) Psychological contract and breach: beliefs about reciprocal obligations and promises; breach is the perception that the organization failed to fulfill them. (4) Realistic job preview (RJP): a balanced picture of the positive and negative aspects of a job given to applicants. (5) Individualized socialization tactics (individual, informal, random, variable, disjunctive and divestiture): relative absence of structure that creates ambiguity and encourages newcomers to question the status quo; and organizational culture: shared beliefs, values and assumptions, with social information processing (people use others’ cues to decide what is appropriate) showing how the culture is actually learned. (6) Pascale’s seven steps: the mutually reinforcing process by which strong cultures socialize members.

Apply: Brightline’s onboarding is almost pure individualized (disjunctive, random, informal) socialization with no resources (no orientation, no mentor, no feedback), so newcomers face high uncertainty and fill the gap with information dependence: they copy “the loudest people.” Whatever those people model becomes the culture, which is why the experienced culture (“do what you are told”) drifts from the one the founder envisions (“ownership and candour”): the envisioned-versus-experienced gap that prevents a strong culture. The recruiting pitch was not an RJP; it oversold, creating unrealistic expectations, and the promised mentor and creative work not delivered are a psychological contract breach, whose predicted results (mistrust, lower satisfaction and commitment, higher turnover intentions) match the one-third who quit. The culture is at risk of being weak and fragmented, without the careful step-by-step socialization of strong cultures.

Recommend: (a) Pascale Step 1, selection with an RJP: select for fit with ownership and candour and give candidates an honest preview of the repetitive early work so poor-fit candidates deselect themselves, and the recruiting promises can be kept. (b) Step 3, training “in the trenches”: have every hire, whatever the level, start in front-line support so they master a core discipline and share an understanding of the work. (c) Step 4, reward and promotion: visibly tie rewards to ownership and candour (an award for the person who raised a hard truth), because employees learn what matters from what is rewarded. (d) Add (e.g., Steps 5–7) repeated exposure to the core values, stories that illustrate them, and chosen role models. (e) Add a structured orientation (consider ROPES to build coping with entry stress), an assigned mentor providing career and psychosocial functions, and more institutionalized elements (a cohort, a fixed 90-day plan) so newcomers stop guessing; encourage proactive behaviours (feedback and information seeking). Expect better fit, commitment and retention, with the trade-off that more structure encourages a custodial orientation; keep room for individualized elements where innovation is wanted.
