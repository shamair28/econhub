Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every chapter so far has quietly assumed that managers make sensible choices: they pick the right motivational tool, the right person, the right structure. Chapter 11 asks what actually happens inside a decision, and the answer is uncomfortable. People rarely decide the way a textbook economist says they should. They define problems badly, search for information selectively, anchor on the first number they hear, stick with failing projects because of what they have already spent, and let their mood tip the balance. The chapter gives you a clean model of good decision making (the rational model) and then spends most of its length showing you where real people depart from it, and why.

The textbook opens with a Canadian case that it keeps returning to: the Phoenix payroll system. In 2009 the federal government began centralizing payroll for a workforce of about 290,000 (annual payroll of $22 billion) and replacing a 40-year-old system used by 101 departments and agencies. The new system took seven years and $310 million to implement and was supposed to save about $70 million a year. It had to handle roughly 80,000 pay rules from 105 collective agreements, which meant 200 custom programs bolted onto the PeopleSoft base. Problems were obvious by early 2016, yet the government went ahead with the second phase. People were overpaid, underpaid or not paid for months; there were mortgage and car-loan defaults and maxed-out credit cards. By late 2017 the auditor general counted 494,500 outstanding pay requests and $520 million in outstanding pay errors; in one pay period an estimated 51 percent of employees had errors in their paycheques. He also noted that no governance structure had been set up to find root causes 16 months in, whereas Queensland Health in Australia, facing similar problems, did so within four months. A 2020 agreement between the Treasury Board and the union included a lump-sum damage payment and the chance to claim restitution for damaged credit ratings and defaulted mortgages.

Phoenix Is the Chapter’s Running Case Watch for it: the text uses Phoenix as its example of perceptual defence (bureaucrats not “seeing” warning signs), of the not-invented-here bias (ignoring Australia’s similar failure), of unintended consequences from too much complexity, of escalation of commitment (pressing on with phase two), and of the difference between a well-structured problem (paying people their salaries) and an ill-structured one (building the system to do it). Any exam scenario about a large failed project is a Phoenix-style question.

From Dr. Kassaye’s Week 5A lecture. The lecture opens with two everyday dilemmas: buying a chocolate cake or a different dessert, or studying for an exam versus working an extra shift. Both are small decisions, but they contain everything the chapter is about: alternatives, a goal, a trade-off, and a commitment of scarce resources (time and money). He then makes the managerial point: good decision making is vital because it determines how an organization solves problems, uses its resources and reaches its goals.

Section 2

What Is Decision Making? (11.1)

Decision making is the process of developing a commitment to some course of action. The textbook flags three things as noteworthy about that definition, and exam questions like to test each of them:

  1. It involves a choice among several alternatives. A store can carry more or less inventory; a mental health centre can go at the north or south end of town.
  2. It is a process. It is more than the final pick. If you accept a new job, we want to know how you reached that decision.
  3. It involves a commitment of resources such as time, money or personnel. A large inventory, for example, ties up cash.

The text then describes decision making as problem solving. A problem exists when a gap is perceived between an existing state and a desired state. The word perceived matters: the gap is in someone’s head until they notice it. In the textbook’s example, a philosophy department chair sees a projected rise in enrolment and an incomplete course schedule (existing state) and wants enough Philosophy 200 sections to serve the students while giving the dean a timely, sensible schedule (desired state). Decision making is then the perception of the existing state, the conception of the desired state, and the steps taken to move from one to the other (Figure 1).

Well-Structured Problems and Programmed Decisions

For a well-structured problem, the existing state is clear, the desired state is clear, and how to get from one to the other is fairly obvious. These problems are simple, repetitive and familiar, so their solutions arouse little controversy. The text’s examples: an assistant bank manager choosing which of 10 car-loan applications to approve, a welfare officer deciding how much assistance a client receives, a courier choosing a delivery route.

Because deciding takes time and invites error, organizations program these decisions. A program is a standardized way of solving a problem; it lets the decision maker jump straight from problem identification to solution. Programs go by names such as rules, routines, standard operating procedures and rules of thumb. Some live only in people’s heads; others are formal, like the fixed formula banks use to “score” routine loan applications from income, debt and credit history. UPS routes its drivers to avoid left-hand turns so trucks do not idle waiting for traffic, which saves roughly $300 to $400 million a year in fuel, wages and running costs. The warning: programs are only as good as the decision process that created them (“garbage in, garbage out”). Critics of the computer algorithms some U.S. judges use to predict reoffending argue that they inherit bias from historical data, which is really an argument that sentencing is an ill-structured problem being treated as a well-structured one.

Ill-Structured Problems and Non-Programmed Decisions

In the extreme case of an ill-structured problem, the existing and desired states are unclear and the method of getting to the desired state, even if clarified, is unknown. The text’s example is a marketing vice-president with a vague feeling that sales are too low but no precise figure for market share, no benchmark competitor, and no idea how to raise sales. Ill-structured problems tend to be unique (unusual, not encountered before), complex and uncertain, so they frequently arouse controversy and conflict among interested parties. Examples: whether to vaccinate against a new flu strain with possible side effects, whether to attempt a risky hostage rescue, where to build a new design centre. They cannot be solved with programmed decisions; decision makers must use non-programmed decision making, gathering more information and being more self-consciously analytical. Ill-structured problems can carry high risk and strong political pressure, as in Phoenix, and they are the focus of the rest of the chapter. The learning-objectives checklist words it more loosely: an ill-structured problem has some combination of an unclear existing state, an unclear desired state or unclear methods.

Lecture Supplement — Week 5A: How Dr. Kassaye Frames Decision Making

From Dr. Kassaye’s Week 5A slide 3 and recorded lecture. Examinable in group assignments; the ideas overlap with the textbook but the wording differs.

The lecture adds a second, plainer definition: a decision is a choice made from available alternatives, and decision making is the process of identifying problems and opportunities and then resolving them by looking at alternatives. His sequence for decision making as problem solving is: define the problem, examine the alternatives, select a course of action, then commit the resources to carry it out. He describes the two problem types this way:

  • Well-structured: a clear starting point, clear goals, an established procedure for finding solutions, the information you need is available, and you can check whether the answer meets the goals. It requires finding the right answer.
  • Ill-structured: something is unclear, undefined or incomplete, there are several possible solutions, and people may disagree about what a good outcome looks like. It requires defining the problem and making a judgment among multiple possible solutions.

He is careful to add that ill-structured does not mean poorly managed or impossible to solve; understanding and defining the problem is simply part of the job. The lecture also treats opportunities as well as problems as triggers for decisions, a nuance the textbook’s “gap” definition does not state.

The Compleat Decision Maker: Perfect vs. Bounded Rationality (11.2)

Exhibit 11.1 gives the rational decision-making process, the model of how a good decision maker would handle an ill-structured problem (Week 5A slide 6 shows the same seven steps). When a problem is identified, a search for information begins; the information clarifies the problem and suggests alternatives; the alternatives are carefully evaluated and the best is chosen; the chosen solution is implemented and then monitored over time. If difficulties arise anywhere, the process recycles.

  1. Identify the problem (find the gap between existing and desired states).
  2. Search for relevant information (clarify the problem and start to suggest solutions).
  3. Develop alternative solutions to the problem.
  4. Evaluate the alternative solutions.
  5. Choose the best solution.
  6. Implement the chosen solution.
  7. Monitor and evaluate the chosen solution (then recycle if needed).
Memory Aid — “I See Dave Eat Cake In Montreal” Identify · Search · Develop · Evaluate · Choose · Implement · Monitor — seven steps, seven words. Sorting rule: steps 1–2 are about understanding (problem and facts), 3–5 are about options (generate, judge, pick), 6–7 are about action (do it, check it). Trap: “Evaluate” appears twice in the model, once for the alternatives (step 4) and once for the chosen solution after implementation (step 7); and step 2 happens before you have any alternatives. Perfect Rationality: The Economic Person

The prototype of perfect rationality is the Economic Person (formerly Economic Man) from introductory economics: the perfect, cool, calculating decision maker. Three traits define it:

  • Completely informed. Gathers information about problems and solutions at no cost.
  • Perfectly logical. If A is preferred to B, and B to C, then A is necessarily preferred to C.
  • One criterion: economic gain.

Economic Person is useful theoretically, but these characteristics do not exist in real decision makers. Nobel Prize winner Herbert Simon recognized this and suggested that managers use bounded rationality: they try to act rationally but are limited in their capacity to acquire and process information, and time constraints and political considerations (such as the need to please others in the organization) also bound their rationality. Framing, cognitive biases and the effects of emotion and mood are all illustrations of this.

Framing and Cognitive Biases (11.3)

Two ideas run underneath everything that follows, so define them first.

Framing
The (sometimes subtle) aspects of the presentation of information about a problem that are assumed by decision makers. A frame can include assumptions about the boundaries of a problem, the possible outcomes of a decision, or the reference points used to judge whether a decision succeeded (also Week 5A slide 9).
Cognitive biases
Tendencies to acquire and process information in an error-prone way. They are assumptions and shortcuts that can improve decision-making efficiency but frequently lead to serious errors in judgment. Intelligence does not cancel them: more and less smart people are equally prone.

Framing is invisible to the person doing it, which is what makes it powerful. When a problem is identified it is necessarily framed in some way. The text’s examples: a $10,000 expenditure can be framed as a cost (to be avoided) or an investment (to be pursued); a new product launch can be framed as a military campaign against competitors or a crusade to help customers. The facts are the same, but the decisions may differ greatly. The text’s advice is to (1) be self-conscious about how you framed the problem (“we have assumed this is a product innovation problem”), (2) try out alternative frames (“let’s imagine we don’t need a new product here”), and (3) avoid overarching, universal frames: “put customers first” is a good motto, but not every problem is a customer-service problem.

Lecture Supplement — Week 5A: Availability vs. Representativeness

From Dr. Kassaye’s Week 5A slide 9 and recorded lecture. The slide names two cognitive biases: availability and representativeness. The textbook covers availability but never uses the term representativeness, so treat representativeness as lecture-only and examinable in group assignments.

Availability bias
Judging how likely or important something is by how easily examples come to mind. Textbook wording: the tendency to favour information that can be accessed quickly and easily, so we remember vivid, recent events. Lecture example: one customer loudly complains about price; because the complaint is memorable, you conclude price is the main reason customers leave. A memorable complaint does not tell you what most customers are concerned about.
Representativeness bias (lecture only)
Judging something by how closely it resembles a familiar category or stereotype, while overlooking other evidence. Lecture example: “this restaurant looks like other successful restaurants, so it must give excellent service.” The shortcut simplifies the decision but can steer you away from the actual evidence about service.
Vocabulary Trap — Availability (Ease of Recall) vs. Representativeness (Similarity to a Category) Both are mental shortcuts, and both are easy to mislabel. Availability asks “what can I remember quickly?” (vivid, recent, loud). Representativeness asks “what does this look like?” (it fits the stereotype or prototype). A second trap is where the textbook and lecture file availability: the text discusses it under information search (too little information, Section 5), while the lecture lists it as a cognitive bias in its own right (source #2 of nine). Either way the definition is the same.

Problems at Each Stage of the Process (11.3 continued)

The perfectly rational Economic Person sails through every stage of the model. Real decision makers do not. This section follows the model in order and names what goes wrong at each stage; Exhibit 11.2 (end of Section 7) puts it all in one table. Figure 3 maps the first two stages.

Stage 1: Problem Identification

Gaps can be signalled by dissatisfied customers, vigilant employees, new legislation or competitors’ ads. Yet a MIT Sloan Management Review article called problem identification the most underrated skill in management, and 85 percent of executives surveyed in Harvard Business Review said their people were poor at diagnosing problems. The perfectly rational decision maker would be a great problem identifier; bounded rationality causes four difficulties:

  • Perceptual defence. The perceptual system (Chapter 3) can defend you against unpleasant perceptions. Ottawa bureaucrats may have been unconsciously motivated not to “see” signals that the proposed Phoenix system was inadequate.
  • Problem defined in terms of functional specialty. Selective perception makes people view a problem as belonging to their specialty. Marketing people fixate on a marketing fix for poor sales even when the real problem is bad design.
  • Problem defined in terms of solution. Jumping to conclusions, aptly called the plunging-in bias, short-circuits the model. Restaurateurs who decide the menu needs a “new look” replace beloved dishes and irritate loyal customers; Google Glass seems to have started from “we can put smartphone technology in glasses” rather than from a careful look at consumer needs.
  • Problem diagnosed in terms of symptoms. “We have a morale problem” may be true, but fixing surface symptoms gives few clues to a solution. Low morale from poor pay calls for a different fix than low morale from boring work.
Memory Aid — The Four S’s of Problem Identification Shield (perceptual defence) · Silo (functional specialty) · Solution (defined in terms of a solution) · Symptom (diagnosed in terms of symptoms). Sorting rule: ask what the person is doing wrong: not seeing the signal (Shield), seeing it only through their department (Silo), already holding the answer (Solution), or stopping at the surface (Symptom). Trap: “it’s a marketing problem” is Silo, but “we need more advertising” is Solution, even though both come from marketing people. Self-test: write one vignette sentence for each S without looking.

Stage 2: Information Search

The Economic Person has free, instant access to all information. Real decision makers get too little or too much.

Too little information arises from four biases:

  • Availability bias: the tendency to favour information that can be accessed quickly and easily, so we remember vivid recent events. During the pandemic, some emergency-room doctors reportedly misdiagnosed patients with “COVID brain,” reading symptoms as COVID when another problem was underneath.
  • Overconfidence: a well-documented tendency to be too sure of our decisions; it led experts to mispredict the election of Donald Trump and Brexit.
  • Confirmation bias: the tendency to seek out information that conforms to your own definition of, or solution to, a problem. One expert calls this ceremonial search “decision-based evidence making” rather than evidence-based decision making. (Lecture example: if you believe price is the problem, you read the price complaints and overlook long waits, poor service and inconvenient opening hours.)
  • Not-invented-here bias: the tendency to ignore, or hold negative attitudes toward, ideas from outside your own organization or project team. Nokia kept developing its own phone software despite a good open-source option; Sony’s failure to take ideas from rivals damaged its competitiveness; and in Phoenix, Ottawa failed to learn from Australia’s similar fiasco.

Complexity adds the “law of unintended consequences”: Phoenix’s modifications were so complex that managers could not envision the risk. A remedy for too little information is the randomized controlled experiment: change one factor under controlled conditions (give some online customers coupons, others none) and see what happens. Google runs about 10,000 experiments a year and Booking.com has about 1,500 employees able to run them. But results are only information, not decisions, and experiments can be used questionably (StubHub found late-disclosed fees were accepted more often), so transparency matters.

Too much information is just as damaging. Information overload is the reception of more information than is necessary to make effective decisions. It leads to errors, omissions, delays and cutting corners, and overloaded people often try to use all the information, getting confused and letting low-quality or irrelevant data sway them (think of the term paper stuffed with too many references). Amazon employees, for instance, get 50–60 page metric printouts before reviews where they are pop-quizzed on thousands of numbers. Why do people keep wanting more? More information raises confidence even when it does not improve the decision, and managers fear being “kept in the dark” and associate information with power. The research review says managers gather information with little decision relevance, use information gathered after a decision to justify it, request information they never use, request more regardless of what they have, and complain about too little while ignoring what they already have. Social media and big data magnify all this: overload can paradoxically create a deficit of useful information, and we herd toward what others do.

Stages 3 to 5: Alternative Development, Evaluation and Choice

For a perfectly rational decision maker this is easy: conceive of all alternatives, know the ultimate value of each and the probability that it will work, and maximize, meaning choose the alternative with the greatest expected value (value × probability).

Bounded decision makers may not know all the alternatives, nor their values and probabilities. The text calls people weak intuitive statisticians who violate standard principles in four ways:

  • Base rates ignored. Firms keep launching novelty food products (foods in tubes, celebrity foods) despite very high failure rates.
  • Sample size ignored. A couple of vivid focus groups can outweigh a large national survey; people lean on anecdotes when emotionally involved (severe threat, personal health).
  • Chains overestimated. A many-step scenario sounds sensible but is less likely with each added link; before 2008 it was assumed the economy would stay healthy, real estate would keep rising and people would find a way to pay escalating mortgages.
  • Poor revision of estimates. The anchoring effect is the inadequate adjustment of subsequent estimates from an initial estimate that serves as an (often irrelevant) anchor. Real estate agents let the asking price pull their professional valuation; acquirers are swayed by the premium paid in the last acquisition in their industry; Apple, choosing between a hard keyboard and a soft touchpad for the iPhone, would have been anchoring on the industry norm had it chosen the keyboard.
Memory Aid — “Bad Statisticians Choose Rashly” Base rates ignored · Sample size ignored · Chains overestimated · Revision poor (anchoring). Sorting rule: the first three are about probability errors; the fourth is about not updating. Trap: anchoring is about adjusting a numerical estimate from a starting point, not about the way a problem is described (that is framing). Self-test: name the one example the text uses for each.

The text’s remedy: make people accountable for their decisions (reasoned reports, formal presentations of how the decision was reached), but before the decision, because accountability after the fact tends to increase bias as people protect their identity as good decision makers. Bounded decision makers also weigh extra criteria, such as “will the boss like it?”, which have their own values and probabilities and add complexity.

Because of all this, the bounded decision maker frequently satisfices instead of maximizing. Risky Business: Framing and Risk

Choosing between alternatives usually involves risk. The text uses a scenario from decision researcher Max Bazerman. A Fortune 500 company faces a class-action suit it believes it is innocent of, but a court may disagree; it would lose $50 million if it loses in court, and its lawyer puts the chance of losing at 50 percent. It can settle for $25 million. Which wording do you prefer?

Options A and B are a choice between losses; C and D are a choice between gains. All four are equal in dollars (50% × $50 million = $25 million), yet about 80 percent of students pick the riskier B in the loss frame and the safer C in the gain frame. The rule: when people view a problem as a choice between losses, they tend to make risky decisions (rolling the dice in the face of a sure loss); when they view it as a choice between gains, they tend to make conservative decisions (protecting the sure win). Be aware of the reference point you are using. Framing a problem as a choice between losses is not necessarily wrong, but it can contribute to a foolish level of risk taking. Other factors that stimulate managerial risk taking: compensation tied to the stock price, poor relative organizational performance, abundant organizational resources, and various personality characteristics.

The Research Focus on CEO personality makes the last point concrete. More extraverted CEOs (assertive, decisive, socially engaged) made more and bigger acquisitions, partly through the network that sitting on many boards gave them, and shareholders reacted more positively to those deals. Highly narcissistic CEOs (inflated self-importance, craving affirmation) were insensitive to objective performance but tuned in to media praise, and after praise they engaged in riskier spending on R&D, capital expenditures and acquisitions and paid inflated premiums; narcissistic bank CEOs pursued riskier strategies, especially with stock options. WeWork’s Adam Neumann is cited as a widely regarded narcissist.

Memory Aid — “Losses → Leap, Gains → Guard” and the Four P’s of Risk Taking Frame it as losses and people leap into the gamble; frame it as gains and they guard the sure thing. Other managerial risk drivers, the four P’s: Pay tied to the stock price · Poor relative performance · Plenty of organizational resources · Personality. Solution Evaluation: Justification, Escalation of Commitment and Hindsight (11.4)

The perfectly rational decision maker evaluates a decision with calm, objective detachment. A bounded decision maker has ego, reputation and money riding on the choice, which produces three linked problems at the monitoring stage. (Week 5A slide 17 lists the first two as source #8 of bounded rationality; Dr. Kassaye’s recording stopped before this slide, so the textbook is the main source here.)

Justification and Sunk Costs

People tend to be overconfident about their decisions, so a faulty decision arouses substantial dissonance (the discomfort of “I am a good decision maker” clashing with “this failed”). One way to prevent it is to avoid careful tests of the decision, and many organizations are notoriously lax about evaluating expensive training programs or advertising campaigns. If bad news cannot be avoided, decision makers put their energy into justifying the decision.

Justification is best seen in the irrational treatment of sunk costs: permanent losses of resources incurred as the result of a decision. The key word is permanent. Because those resources are gone, they should not enter into future decisions. Psychologist Barry Staw showed that people nevertheless “throw good resources after bad” as if they could recoup what was sunk. That is escalation of commitment: the tendency to invest additional resources in an apparently failing course of action. Example: an executive buys machines that turn out to be unreliable, then orders more from the same manufacturer to “prove” he was right, hoping to recoup the sunk costs.

Why People Escalate

Dissonance reduction is only one cause. The text (and slide 17) gives five:

  1. Dissonance reduction. Escalating “proves” the original decision was right.
  2. A social norm favouring consistency. Changing your mind and reversing a decision can look like weakness.
  3. Motivation not to appear wasteful. Even a new decision maker who did not make the original choice may press on: “otherwise the taxpayers will think we squandered their money.” British Columbia’s NDP government continued the Site C dam, begun by the previous Liberal government, despite delays, cost overruns and questionable need, and sunk costs were cited.
  4. The way the problem is framed. Once resources are sunk, the choice can be framed as a sure loss of x (stop) versus an uncertain loss of x + y (maybe the extra investment will succeed). From Section 5, people facing a choice between losses take the gamble, which here means escalating.
  5. Personality, moods and emotions. People high in neuroticism and negative affectivity (Chapter 2) are less likely to escalate, since they try to avoid stressful predicaments.
Memory Aid — “Don’t Say We Failed, Please” Dissonance reduction · Social norm for consistency · Wasteful-looking (not wanting to appear wasteful) · Framing (sure loss vs gamble) · Personality, mood and emotion. Sorting rule: D, S and W are about how you look to yourself and others; F is about how the choice is described; P is about who you are and how you feel. Trap: when the current decision maker did not make the original decision, dissonance and consistency drop out, and “not appearing wasteful” and framing explain the escalation. Also, the personality effect is counterintuitive: neurotic, negative-affect people escalate less. Self-test: for the Site C example, say which two of the five reasons apply best.

The text adds that escalation occurs in both competitive and non-competitive settings (Wall Street analysts overvaluing stocks before a crash is a non-competitive example). Competitive, auction-like situations are especially prone to it, because they combine time pressure, rivalry, interested audiences and the desire to be first mover, all of which raise emotional arousal. The Vietnam and Iraq wars are cited as examples of competitive escalation. (Dr. Kassaye’s slide 4 video, the Dollar Auction, demonstrates exactly this; see the resources box at the end of Section 7.)

Vocabulary Trap — Sunk Costs (the Cause) vs. Escalation of Commitment (the Behaviour) vs. Future Costs Sunk costs are the resources already spent and unrecoverable; escalation of commitment is the act of spending more because of them. Only future costs and benefits should drive the next decision. A manager who says “we have already spent $1 million, so we must continue” is escalating because of sunk costs; a manager who says “it will cost $400,000 more and return $150,000, so stop” is reasoning correctly.

Preventing Escalation

Logic and research suggest five steps:

  1. Be alert for excessive optimism or extremely positive media attention early in a project; good vibes blind decision makers to later signals of failure.
  2. Keep reframing the problem to avoid the trap of feeling that more must be invested; shift the frame to saving rather than spending.
  3. Set specific goals in advance that must be met before more resources are invested, so unclear early results do not justify escalation.
  4. Evaluate managers on how they made decisions more than on outcomes, which teaches them not to fear or hide failure.
  5. Separate initial and subsequent decision making so that people who started a course of action are assisted or replaced by others who decide whether to continue (banks do this with problem loans); leadership changes can break a spiral.
Memory Aid — “Owls Reframe Goals, Prize Process, Split roles” Optimism alert · Reframe as saving · Goals set in advance · Process over outcome in evaluating managers · Separate (split) initial and later decision makers. Sorting rule: O is about noticing, R and G are about thinking and planning, P and S are about organizational design. Trap: using a group to decide whether to continue does not help; the text says groups are more prone than individuals to escalate. Self-test: match each of the five steps to the escalation cause (D, S, W, F, P) it most directly counters.
The Flip Side: Using the Same Forces for Good Slide 17 closes with “prevention of escalation: what are your ideas?” and “how to use theory to increase commitment.” The psychology that traps people in bad decisions can also strengthen commitment to good ones: public commitment and consistency pull people to follow through on a sound plan; clear advance goals and agreed review points give “staying the course” a rational test; and framing a hard-but-right decision as protecting a gain rather than avoiding a loss makes people willing to keep going. The difference is whether the commitment is checked against future costs and benefits.

Hindsight

Careful evaluation is also inhibited by faulty hindsight: the tendency to review the decision-making process to find what was done right (in success) or wrong (in failure). Hindsight can be useful, but it often reflects a cognitive bias in two forms. The knew-it-all-along effect is the tendency to assume, after the fact, that we knew all along what the outcome would be: our faulty memory adjusts the probabilities we estimated beforehand to match what happened (the armchair quarterback who “knew” a risky pass was unnecessary because the team won; the money manager who made a consciously risky bet and now remembers it as a sure thing). The second form is self-serving responsibility: taking personal credit for successful outcomes while denying responsibility for unsuccessful ones (“we made a careful, logical decision” when it worked; “an unexpected external factor” when it did not).

Vocabulary Trap — “Hindsight” Is Reviewing the Process, and It Often Goes Wrong in Two Ways In the text, hindsight is not always a bias; it is reviewing the decision process to learn what went right or wrong. The bias shows up as (1) the knew-it-all-along effect and (2) taking credit for successes and blaming externals for failures. Do not confuse it with justification (defending a faulty decision to avoid dissonance) or with escalation.

How Emotions and Mood Affect Decision Making (11.5)

So far the chapter has been mostly cognitive. But the text points out that justification and hindsight already signal an emotional side: people do not like to be wrong and get emotionally attached to a failing course of action. (Week 5A slide 18 is source #9 of bounded rationality; the recording ends before it, so rely on the text.) The first point is that emotionless decision making would be poor decision making; the rational model is not meant to suggest otherwise. The graphic evidence: people with brain injuries that blunt emotion while leaving intellect intact make a series of poor life decisions because they cannot judge the impact of decisions on themselves and others.

Emotion: Help and Hindrance

Dr. Kassaye’s slide 18 and the textbook agree that emotion can do three things:

  • Correct ethical errors. Strong emotion often figures in the decision process that corrects ethical errors, and whistle-blowers often say emotion motivated them to protest decision errors. (The NASA Discovery mission example: some cautious decisions, such as delaying the landing, may have reflected mission control’s lingering emotions after the Columbia tragedy.)
  • Aid creative solutions. Strong positive emotion is implicated in creative decision making and proper use of intuition, which can short-circuit the rational model when speed is essential.
  • Distract and unsettle, leading to poor choices. People “blinded by emotion” are self-focused and distracted from the problem; stress and strong emotion are intertwined, and stress contributes to poor and risky decisions, partly through poor information search.

Evidence on emotion is mostly anecdotal because people are reluctant to take part in field research on emotional issues and it is unethical to induce strong emotions in a lab. Examples include excessive pride leading highly paid CEOs to overpay for acquisitions, angry CEOs losing their heads in competitive bidding (escalation of commitment again), and emotional conflict between business partners or family members provoking questionable business decisions. The video on slide 5, the trolley problem, is a classic demonstration of how strong feelings pull against calculation in an ethical choice; see the resources box at the end of this section.

Mood: Mild, Pervasive and Powerful

In contrast to strong emotions, there is a lot of research on mood. Recall from Chapter 4 that moods are relatively mild, unfocused states of positive or negative feeling. Two paradoxes: moods are low-key yet clearly affect what and how people think when deciding, and their biggest impact is not on routine problems but on uncertain, ambiguous ones, the kind most crucial to organizations. Many organizational decisions are made by groups rather than individuals, especially when problems are ill-structured. The text weighs the advantages against the problems.

Why Use Groups?

There are three reasons for employing groups to decide:

  • Decision quality. Experts argue groups can make higher-quality decisions because they are more vigilant (more people scanning the environment), can generate more ideas and can evaluate ideas better (checks and balances: an extreme position or incorrect notion held by one member is offset by the pooled judgments of the rest). Vigilance helps at problem identification and information search; idea generation helps at developing alternatives; evaluation helps at choosing.
  • Decision acceptance and commitment. The premise (from participative leadership, Chapter 9) is that people want to be involved in decisions that affect them, will better understand a decision they took part in, and will be more committed to a decision in which they invested personal time and energy. That helps with the implementation problem from Section 5.
  • Diffusion of responsibility (the less admirable reason). Using a group lets members share the burden of the negative consequences, so no one person is punished. The text notes that members then often “abandon ship” and show biased hindsight: “I knew all along the bid was too high, but they made me go along.”

Do Groups Actually Decide Better?

Yes, on balance. Reviews conclude that groups usually produce more and better solutions than individuals working alone, and that group performance is superior to that of the average individual in the group. Groups should do better than individuals when:

  • members differ in relevant skills and abilities, but not so much that conflict results;
  • some division of labour can occur;
  • memory for facts is an important issue; and
  • individual judgments can be combined by weighting them to reflect each member’s expertise.
Memory Aid — “Some Dogs Mostly Wag” Skills differ (but not too much) · Division of labour possible · Memory for facts matters · Weighting of judgments by expertise. Trap: equal weighting or simple averaging is inappropriate when some members have more expertise; and if several members share a view, it can prevail over a single expert, so extensive discussion (which lets accurate minority views be heard) matters. Groups are wiser when independent thinking is encouraged and those with more knowledge get more influence. Self-test: say the four conditions, then name one that a three-person student project team meets and one it may not.

Disadvantages of Group Decision Making

  • Time. Groups seldom work quickly or efficiently compared with individuals, because of process losses (Chapter 7); the problem grows with group size. Avoid groups when speed is the prime factor.
  • Conflict. Members may have personal axes to grind or resources to protect, so decision quality can take a back seat to political wrangling. Groups decide better when members feel psychologically safe.
  • Domination. The benefits are seldom realized if meetings are dominated by one person or a small coalition. Even if the dominant person is well informed, acceptance and commitment suffer; if misinformed, the decision is likely ineffective.
  • Groupthink (below).
Memory Aid — “Teams Cause Delays, Groupthink” Time · Conflict · Domination · Groupthink. Sorting rule: the first three are ordinary process problems; groupthink is the extreme conformity problem. Trap: diffusion of responsibility is a reason to use groups (a poor one), not a disadvantage in this list. Self-test: say each problem and the fix a manager could apply.

Groupthink

In Chapter 8 we saw conformity. The most extreme influence is groupthink: the capacity for group pressure to damage the mental efficiency, reality testing and moral judgment of decision-making groups, so unanimity is stressed over decision quality. Psychologist Irving Janis, who developed the concept, saw high cohesiveness as its root; it now appears other factors matter more: strong identification with the group, concern for its approval and isolation from outside sources of information. The strongest cause appears to be promotion of a particular decision by the group leader. Janis lists eight symptoms:

  1. Illusion of invulnerability. Overconfidence and willingness to take great risks; ignoring danger signals.
  2. Rationalization. Problems and counterarguments that cannot be ignored are “rationalized away” with seemingly logical but improbable excuses.
  3. Illusion of morality. The group’s decisions are seen as sensible and morally correct.
  4. Stereotypes of outsiders. Unfavourable stereotypes of those outside the group who are the targets of its decisions.
  5. Pressure for conformity. Members press each other to fall in line.
  6. Self-censorship. Members talk themselves out of voicing contrary opinions.
  7. Illusion of unanimity. Members perceive that everyone supports the chosen course.
  8. Mindguards. Members who “protect” the group from information that goes against its decisions.
Memory Aid — “In Review Meetings, Strangers Pressure Silent Unanimous Members” Invulnerability · Rationalization · Morality · Stereotypes of outsiders · Pressure for conformity · Self-censorship · Unanimity · Mindguards. Sorting rule: the first four are about how the group sees itself and outsiders (illusions and stereotypes); the last four are about how members behave toward each other (pressure, silence, false agreement, guarding). Preventing Groupthink and Managing Controversy

To prevent groupthink, leaders must avoid undue pressure for a particular outcome and concentrate on good decision processes, establish norms that encourage and even reward responsible dissent, and bring in outside experts from time to time to challenge the group’s views.

Full-blown conflict is not good for decisions either: people withhold information and personal or group goals take over. But a total lack of controversy is equally damaging, since relevant alternative views never surface; it is partly responsible for groupthink and contributes to escalation of commitment. One method of stimulating controversy is the devil’s advocate: a person appointed to identify and challenge the weaknesses of a proposed plan or strategy and say why it should not be adopted (a bank reviewing a new account might have someone “tear apart” the position paper before a decision). It improves decision quality, but only if the advocate’s views are presented objectively and unemotionally.

How Do Groups Handle Risk?

Do groups decide more or less riskily than individuals? Common sense gives contradictory predictions: there is “security in numbers” (diffusion of responsibility encourages bigger chances), yet members also check and balance each other into caution. MIT student James Stoner reported a risky shift: after discussing hypothetical cases (career choices, investments), groups advised riskier courses than the average risk their members initially favoured. Later studies also found conservative shifts: groups making less risky decisions than their members’ initial average.

Both occur, and what decides which is the initial position of group members (Exhibit 11.4, Figure 7): when members start somewhat conservative they shift toward more conservative; when they start somewhat risky they shift toward more risky. Discussion polarizes, or exaggerates, the group’s initial position. Boards of directors deciding how much to pay for an acquisition target show it: high individual judgments produce an even higher average offer after deliberation, and vice versa for low ones.

Two main reasons explain it: (1) group discussion generates ideas and arguments members had not considered, which naturally favour their initial leaning, so the leaning is exaggerated; and (2) members try to present themselves as similar to others but “even better,” so they one-up each other by taking a slightly more extreme version of the group’s initial stance. Managers should expect polarization: if it comes from a sensible exchange of information it may improve the decision, but if it comes from one-upmanship it can lower quality.

Vocabulary Trap — Groupthink vs. Risky/Conservative Shift vs. Diffusion of Responsibility Groupthink is a conformity failure (pressure, self-censorship, false unanimity). Risky/conservative shift is a polarization effect: discussion exaggerates where members already stood, and the group is not always riskier (the shift can go either way). Diffusion of responsibility is about sharing blame for a bad outcome. All three involve groups going wrong, but by different mechanisms; exams test the contrast directly. Also remember that groups are more prone to escalation of commitment than individuals.

Contemporary Approaches to Improving Decision Making (11.7)

Final Exam Only — Not on the Midterm Like Section 8, this is 11.7 material taught in Week 7 and examinable on the final exam only (and relevant to Group Assignment #2). There is no Week 7 lecture material on this page yet; everything below is from the textbook.

Good decisions are vital to organizational success, and they improve when decision makers adhere to the rational model (Exhibit 11.1), which helps preclude the biases and errors in Sections 4 to 7. Each contemporary approach shares that goal, and three themes run through them: managers must be more mindful of the quality of the evidence they use, be open to new and improved sources of evidence, and may have to involve new people in the decision.

Evidence-Based Management

Evidence-based management means “making decisions through the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of the best available evidence from multiple sources.” Wishful thinking and intuition are no substitute. It has its roots in evidence-based medicine, which summarizes thousands of medical studies to guide clinical practice. Much of what passes for decision making in organizations fails the test: guessing what the boss favours, indiscriminately copying competitors (defended as “benchmarking”), or buying canned consulting packages.

What is good evidence?

  • Scientific, peer-reviewed research is the gold standard, especially when many independent studies are summarized in a systematic review using meta-analysis. Social science research is underused by managers but can be a source of competitive advantage in HR, strategy and marketing decisions.
  • Data from the organization: professionally constructed customer and employee surveys, financial data and HR data.
  • New data sources: the views of the masses (crowdsourcing) and mining archived internet traffic.
  • Expert and professional judgment from people with deep experience in a domain.

Roughly: harder evidence (scientific research and company data) is most useful at the information search and solution evaluation stages of the model; expert and professional judgment is most useful in identifying the problem and mid-process, when developing, choosing and implementing solutions. Even the best evidence does not speak for itself, and context matters. Build an evidence-based mindset by always asking: What precisely is the evidence being brought to bear on this decision? How high is its quality? Can we obtain higher-quality evidence?

Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing is “outsourcing” aspects of a decision process to a large collection of people. It aims to capture the merits of group decision making (Section 8) on a large scale. In terms of the rational model it can be used to develop alternatives, to choose the best solution, or both. The crowd is usually outside the organization, though a large firm can open the process to all its employees (internal crowdsourcing). It is not contracting with outside advisors such as a formal consulting relationship; it is a more open, organic process seeking expertise among the masses, from everyday people to experts. Wikipedia, whose entries are written, edited and reviewed by volunteers, is the familiar example.

Applications range from the light-hearted to the profound: Doritos’ ten-year “Crash the Super Bowl” campaign crowdsourced consumer-made TV ads; Harvard Medical School crowdsourced new treatments for Type 1 diabetes (winners included a retired dentist, an undergraduate chemistry student, a geophysicist and a famous geneticist); and Vancouver-based Goldcorp published its geological data and ran the Goldcorp Challenge, a $575,000 prize for finding gold in Ontario, drawing over 1,000 participants from 50 nations and identifying new gold-rich sources that transformed the company.

Two ideas underpin it: (1) the information needed for the decision is not available in house (a team of 50-year-old male apparel executives will not know next summer’s colour preferences of preteen girls); and (2) a large, diverse collection of people is likely to contain either a single superior solution or, on average, the correct answer. Single superior solutions suit technical problems (finding gold, perfecting an app); averages of many opinions suit style and taste problems, which is why fashion relies on crowdsourcing; Chicago’s Threadless has its community vote on designs, and uses the votes for final production decisions. Crowdsourcing works best when in-house expertise is lacking, the problem can be clearly stated, and a large, diverse crowd is both knowledgeable and motivated. Motivation may be volunteer enthusiasm or prize contests, and then firms must cultivate trust that good ideas will not be taken without compensation; platforms such as InnoCentive act as intermediaries.

The text’s You Be the Manager case is LEGO. After losses beginning in 1998 the company, which had a product-focused, in-house design culture (and a policy of not pursuing unsolicited ideas), turned to its many adult fans. It tried various ways of soliciting ideas, then adopted a crowdsourcing platform (now LEGO Ideas) where fans submit designs, other users evaluate them, and a submission needs 10,000 votes before LEGO considers it; successful submitters receive 1 percent of net revenue. The vote requirement encouraged submitters to promote their entries on social media, giving LEGO word-of-mouth marketing. The text notes that toy design is largely a matter of style and taste, and that some products need expertise beyond what is in house.

Analytics and Big Data

Analytics is finding meaningful patterns in large datasets using conventional statistics, mathematical modelling and techniques for representing data visually. Sometimes the revealed patterns show that problems are more well-structured than was thought. The famous example is Moneyball: Oakland Athletics’ general manager used new performance statistics to evaluate players and set tactics, showing that many time-honoured rules (such as telling players to steal a base) were statistically wrong, and fielded competitive teams at modest salaries. Sports establishments are often hostile to analytics, but business less so: Netflix uses an algorithm from a $1-million contest to recommend movies; Amazon, HBO and Spotify use similar analytics; General Electric has built a data-driven analytics culture.

Big data is copious amounts of information, often collected in real time, from a wide variety of sources, particularly digital. It is not exactly a technical term. Remember its qualities as the three Vs: volume, velocity, variety. Sources include social-media messages and images, blog posts, cellphone and GPS data, online shopping records, news articles and sensor data from cars and machinery; such data are fairly unstructured and do not fit standard databases easily. Google and Facebook use analytics on big data for targeted advertising decisions; Amazon knows what books you considered, which reviews you read and what you bought, and refines recommendations in real time. Concerns: privacy, unintended consequences (for example racial discrimination by facial recognition technology) and overfitting (building overly complex models that do not generalize to new data).

Problem 1 — Well-Structured or Ill-Structured?

Classify each problem as well-structured or ill-structured, and say whether it calls for a programmed or non-programmed decision. (a) A payroll clerk applies the company’s fixed overtime formula to this week’s timesheets. (b) A hospital board must decide whether to build a new wing when future demand, funding and staffing are all uncertain, and board members disagree about the goals. (c) A courier chooses among three familiar routes to a regular customer.

Show answer
(a) Well-structured, programmed. The existing state, desired state and method are all clear, and a formula (a program) takes the clerk straight from problem to solution. (b) Ill-structured, non-programmed. The desired state is contested, the path is unclear, the problem is unique and uncertain, and it arouses controversy, so the board must gather more information and analyze self-consciously. (c) Well-structured, programmed: repetitive and familiar (the text’s own courier example). Watch the trap: the specific problem decides the classification, even inside a big messy project (as with Phoenix, where paying salaries is structured but building the system was not).
Problem 2 — Which Problem-Identification Error?

Name the error (Shield, Silo, Solution or Symptom) in each case. (a) A hospital administrator sees rising nurse turnover and immediately announces a retention bonus. (b) The IT director insists slow order processing is “a network problem,” although the real cause is a badly designed order form. (c) Managers dismiss early reports that their pet scheduling system is mis-assigning shifts. (d) A CEO says, “We have a morale problem.”

Show answer
(a) Solution (problem defined in terms of a solution; the plunging-in bias): the bonus is chosen before anyone diagnoses why nurses leave. (b) Silo (problem defined in terms of functional specialty): IT sees an IT problem. (c) Shield (perceptual defence): they are unconsciously motivated not to “see” unpleasant signals, like the officials who missed Phoenix’s warning signs. (d) Symptom (problem diagnosed in terms of symptoms): low morale from poor pay needs a different fix than low morale from boring work, so the real question is the cause of the morale problem.
Problem 3 — Which Bias?

Name the bias (anchoring, availability, confirmation, not-invented-here, overconfidence or hindsight). (a) A competitor’s CEO mentions a 10 percent price increase, so a manager sets a 9 percent increase even though her own costs rose only 3 percent. (b) After a widely covered data breach at another company, a manager over-spends on cybersecurity relative to likelier risks. (c) A product manager who thinks the app needs a redesign reads only the reviews that mention design. (d) A team rejects a proven open-source tool because “we didn’t build it.” (e) After a vendor fails, an executive says, “I knew all along they would.”

Show answer
(a) Anchoring effect: the first number sets the estimate and she did not adjust enough. (b) Availability bias: a vivid, recent event is easy to recall and crowds out base rates. (c) Confirmation bias: seeking information that conforms to one’s own definition of the problem. (d) Not-invented-here bias: ignoring or disliking ideas from outside the team (like Nokia and its own phone software). (e) Hindsight (the knew-it-all-along effect): memory adjusts the probabilities he estimated before to fit what happened.
Problem 4 — Framing and Risk

A division manager is shown two versions of the same choice. Version 1: Option A, a sure loss of $30,000, or Option B, a 50 percent chance of losing $60,000. Version 2: Option C, saving $30,000 for sure, or Option D, a 50 percent chance of saving $60,000. (i) Which option will most people pick in each version? (ii) Are the options really different? (iii) What should the manager do? (iv) Using Dr. Kassaye’s labels, what is a risk-averse decision maker?

Show answer
(i) In Version 1 (a choice between losses) most people pick the gamble, B; in Version 2 (a choice between gains) most pick the sure thing, C (Losses → Leap, Gains → Guard). (ii) No: every option has an expected value of $30,000 (50% × $60,000), so the two versions differ only in framing. (iii) Recognize the frame, state the choice both ways, identify the reference point being used, and decide on the underlying values and probabilities rather than the wording; the text warns that framing as losses can contribute to foolish risk taking. (iv) A risk-averse person (lecture term) prefers the certain outcome over a gamble with the same expected value; that preference alone is not irrational.
Problem 5 — Application: A Project That Won’t Die

Maple Ridge Manufacturing approved a $4 million project to build a new order-management system. Eighteen months in, it is $2 million over budget and three months late, and in pilot tests it still mis-routes about 1 in 10 orders. The project sponsor, the VP of Operations, was praised in the business press for the “bold plan.” He now asks the board for another $500,000, saying, “We’ve come too far to stop.” The steering committee, all from his department, has reviewed only the pilot sites that reported success. When an outside consultant warned that the design was unsuited to Maple Ridge’s product range, a committee member replied, “That approach wouldn’t work here.” Using Chapter 11, diagnose what is going wrong and recommend what the board should do. Answer in the order Name → Define → Apply → Recommend.

Show answer
Name: escalation of commitment driven by sunk costs, reinforced by confirmation bias and the not-invented-here bias (with elements of framing and perceptual defence).

Define: Escalation of commitment is the tendency to invest additional resources in an apparently failing course of action; sunk costs are permanent losses of resources incurred as the result of a decision and should not enter future decisions. Confirmation bias is seeking information that conforms to your own definition of a problem or solution. Not-invented-here bias is ignoring or disliking ideas from outside your own organization or project team. Framing is the aspects of the presentation of a problem assumed by decision makers; choices framed as losses encourage risk taking.

Apply: The roughly $6 million already spent (the $4 million budget plus the $2 million overrun) is sunk, yet the VP argues from them (“we’ve come too far”). Of the five reasons for escalation: dissonance reduction and consistency (he championed the plan and the press praised it, so quitting would look like admitting error), not appearing wasteful, and framing (“sure loss of everything spent” versus “maybe $500,000 more will save it” is a choice between losses, which invites the gamble). The early media praise is exactly the “good vibes” the text warns blinds people to later signals of failure. Confirmation bias appears in reviewing only the successful pilot sites; the not-invented-here bias in dismissing the consultant (“wouldn’t work here”); and ignoring a 10 percent error rate is a form of perceptual defence. The same people who started the project are also judging whether to continue.

Recommend: (1) Evaluate the request only on future cost ($500,000) against future benefit, treating the $6 million as gone. (2) Set specific, measurable goals (for example, error rate below 1 percent by a fixed date) that must be met before more money is released. (3) Reframe the question as “how do we save the most from here?” rather than “how do we avoid losing what we spent.” (4) Separate the initial and subsequent decision makers: add independent reviewers or replace the sponsor on the continue/stop decision (a group drawn from his own department would not help; groups escalate more than individuals). (5) Seek information that could disconfirm the plan, including the outside consultant’s view and the unsuccessful pilot sites. (6) Evaluate the VP on how the decision was made, not only whether the project launched. Remember the mnemonics: Don’t Say We Failed, Please for the causes and Owls Reframe Goals, Prize Process, Split roles for the cures.
