Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every managerial judgement you will ever make — who to hire, whose performance rating deserves a bump, whether a colleague is trustworthy, whether a policy is fair — passes through a single narrow gate first: perception. Two managers can watch the identical presentation, read the identical performance data, or sit in on the identical interview and walk away with entirely different conclusions, not because the facts differed, but because each perceiver filtered those facts through their own experience, needs, and emotions. Organizational behaviour treats this as a foundational fact rather than a curiosity: people act on their interpretation of reality, not on reality itself, so understanding how that interpretation gets built — and where it goes wrong — is a prerequisite for understanding almost everything else in this course.

Attribution is the specific perceptual process of assigning a cause to someone's behaviour, and it explains why the same missed deadline gets read by one observer as "he's unreliable" and by the person who missed it as "the client changed the brief twice." That mismatch is not random noise; it follows predictable patterns that this chapter will teach you to name and anticipate. Diversity, the chapter's third strand, is where perceptual errors stop being private misjudgements and become organizational liabilities: stereotypes that distort a single hiring decision, repeated at scale across an organization's recruitment, appraisal, and promotion systems, produce measurable disparities in who advances and who does not.

This chapter is unusually dense with named frameworks — five person-perception biases, three attribution cues, three attribution biases, five rater errors — and McMaster's midterm draws more "which bias is this vignette describing?" multiple-choice questions from this chapter than from any other chapter on the exam. The frameworks are not difficult individually, but they are easy to blur together under exam pressure, so the callouts throughout this chapter deliberately isolate the pairs and triples that get confused most often.

Section 2

What Is Perception? Perceiver, Target, Situation

Perception is the process of interpreting the messages of our senses to provide order and meaning to the environment. The definition is doing real work: perception is not passive recording, it is active interpretation, and the textbook's central claim is that people act on that interpretation rather than on whatever "objectively" happened. Three factors combine to shape any given perception, and exam questions frequently ask you to identify which one is operating in a scenario.

The perceiver brings their own experience, which builds expectations about what they are likely to see; their needs and motives, which unconsciously bias what they notice (a manager anxious about turnover may notice disengagement cues that a relaxed manager would miss); and their emotions, which colour interpretation. A specific perceiver-side mechanism worth knowing by name is perceptual defence: the tendency for the perceptual system to defend the perceiver against unpleasant or threatening information, effectively screening out or downplaying cues that would be uncomfortable to acknowledge. The target being perceived matters too — ambiguous targets are especially open to interpretation, and, counterintuitively, more information about a target does not automatically improve the accuracy of a perception; it can simply give a biased perceiver more raw material to selectively confirm what they already believe. Finally, the situation means that the identical perceiver looking at the identical target can form a very different impression depending on context: the same "blunt" comment lands differently in a casual team stand-up than in a formal client meeting.

Bruner's model of the perceptual process describes, step by step, how perceivers form impressions of an unfamiliar target. It begins with openness to cues from the target and situation; as familiar cues are encountered, the target is crudely categorized; from that point on, the search for further cues becomes more selective, actively seeking information that confirms the initial categorization; and the categorization strengthens as contradictory cues are ignored or distorted to fit. The textbook illustrates this with an engineer who categorizes a new coworker as having "great potential" based on his Stanford degree, and then continues to ignore that same coworker's later declining performance because it does not fit the category already formed. This is best pictured as a narrowing funnel: broad openness to information narrows, step by step, into a fixed, self-confirming impression. The model reveals three properties of perception worth memorizing as a set: selectivity (not all cues are weighted equally — some get used, most get ignored), perceptual constancy (once formed, the impression of a target tends to persist over time even as the target changes), and perceptual consistency (cues are actively selected, ignored, or distorted so that they fit together into one internally homogeneous picture, rather than a messy, contradictory one).

Section 3

Social Identity Theory

Social identity theory holds that people form perceptions of themselves based on two distinct sources. Personal identity is built from your unique interests, abilities, and traits — the things that make you, specifically, different from everyone else. Social identity is built from your membership in social categories: gender, nationality, occupation, employer, program, team. Both operate simultaneously, and which one is more salient at a given moment depends on context — the same person is perceived, and perceives themselves, as "professor" while standing in front of a classroom and as simply "a neighbour" or "a Baby Boomer" when chatting over a fence. Social identities are therefore relational and comparative: they are meaningful only in relation to other categories (there is no "in-group" without an implied "out-group") and situationally dependent, shifting in prominence as the context shifts.

The mechanism that connects social identity to perception is self-categorization: we categorize both ourselves and others into groups in order to make sense of a complex social environment, and once a category is activated we tend to see its members — ourselves included — as embodying a prototype, the set of attributes most typical of that category. This is efficient (it lets us make fast sense of new people) but it is also the root of in-group favouritism: because our own social identity is partly built from group membership, we have a motivated interest in seeing our own groups, and their prototypes, in a positive light, which predisposes us to extend more trust and more charitable interpretations to in-group members than to otherwise-identical out-group members.

Basic Biases in Person Perception

Beyond the general perceiver/target/situation framework, five specific biases recur constantly in how people form impressions of others, and each has a precise definition worth separating from its neighbours. The primacy effect is reliance on early cues, or first impressions; because of perceptual constancy, an early impression tends to have a lasting impact even when later evidence contradicts it. The recency effect is the mirror image: reliance on the most recent cues, or last impressions, which can override an earlier, more moderate view. Both are, technically, order effects — they describe how the position of information in a sequence (first vs. last), not its content, shapes the resulting impression, and exam vignettes will test whether you can tell which one applies to a given scenario, not just whether you can define them.

Reliance on central traits refers to personal characteristics of special interest to a perceiver that end up organizing the rest of the impression around them; physical attractiveness is a commonly documented central trait, linked to what researchers call the "beauty premium" in hiring and pay outcomes. Implicit personality theories are personal theories people hold about which personality traits "go together" — for example, unconsciously assuming that someone who is hardworking must also be honest, even though the two traits are logically independent. Projection is the tendency to attribute your own thoughts and feelings to other people; it can occasionally be efficient (if you and the target are genuinely similar), but it frequently causes serious misjudgment, and it can function as a form of perceptual defence, letting the perceiver avoid confronting feelings they would rather not own directly.

Stereotyping is the tendency to generalize about people in a social category while ignoring the variation that exists within it, and it has a precise three-step structure worth memorizing exactly: first, distinguish a category of people; second, assume that individuals in that category share certain traits; third, assume that every individual in the category has those traits. Stereotypes persist for two reinforcing reasons: they are often self-fulfilling (treating someone according to a stereotype can subtly shape their behaviour to match it) and they are protected by selective attention (once a stereotype-confirming observation is made, contradicting observations are more likely to be dismissed as exceptions).

Central Traits
A trait of special interest to the perceiver (often physical attractiveness) organizes the entire rest of the impression around it — everything else gets interpreted through that one lens.
Implicit Personality Theory
An unconscious personal theory about which traits "travel together" (e.g., hardworking → honest) is applied to a target without any actual evidence for the linked trait.
Projection
The perceiver's own thoughts or feelings are attributed to the target — the impression says as much about the perceiver as it does about the person being perceived.

Exam Trap — Primacy vs. Recency Both are order effects, not content effects: primacy is about what came FIRST dominating, recency is about what came LAST dominating. A vignette describing "first impressions carrying weight" is primacy; one describing "the last thing said sticking in memory" is recency. Watch for scenario cues about sequence length: primacy tends to dominate in short, high-stakes, one-shot encounters like interviews, while recency can dominate in longer, ongoing relationships where the most recent update overwrites an older view.
Section 5

Attribution: Perceiving Causes and Motives

Attribution is the process of assigning causes or motives to explain people's behaviour. Every attribution sorts into one of two buckets: dispositional attributions explain behaviour by pointing to the person's personality or intellect — reading the behaviour as reflecting the "true person" underneath — while situational attributions explain behaviour by pointing to the external environment, implying the person had little control over what happened. Since we cannot read minds, we rely on external cues to decide which bucket applies, and the textbook identifies three specific cues that guide the judgement.

  1. Consistency cues — does the person engage in this behaviour regularly and consistently over time? High consistency pushes toward a dispositional attribution (we assume it reflects who they really are); low, inconsistent behaviour pushes us to look for a situational, often temporary, cause instead.
  2. Consensus cues — do most people behave this way, or is it unique to this person? Low-consensus (unusual) behaviour is more revealing of the actor's true motives and pushes toward a dispositional attribution; typical, high-consensus behaviour is uninformative about the individual and is read as situational.
  3. Distinctiveness cues — does the behaviour occur across many different situations, or is it distinctive to just one? Low distinctiveness (the behaviour "stands up" everywhere) pushes toward a dispositional attribution; high distinctiveness (it only happens in one specific situation) pushes toward a situational one.

The textbook's own example makes the combinations concrete. Roshani is absent a lot, her co-workers are rarely absent, and she was absent a lot in her previous job too: that is high consistency, low consensus, and low distinctiveness, and managers reliably read it as dispositional — Roshani is seen as "lazy" or "irresponsible." Mika is also absent a lot, but her co-workers are absent a lot too, and she was almost never absent in her previous job: high consistency, but high consensus and high distinctiveness, which reliably produces a situational attribution — something about this specific job (bad working conditions, a difficult manager) is the likely cause. This pair is the pattern worth memorizing exactly: high consistency + low consensus + low distinctiveness → dispositional; high consistency + high consensus + high distinctiveness → situational. Notice that consistency is high in both cases — it is consensus and distinctiveness that flip the attribution, which is exactly what an MC question will test. A third, mixed pattern is also worth knowing: Sam is seldom absent, like her co-workers, and was seldom absent previously too — low consistency, high consensus, low distinctiveness — which the textbook reads as pointing to a temporary situational factor (perhaps an occasional sick child), since the behaviour is too inconsistent to be dispositional but not distinctive enough to blame the job itself.

Even a logical, cue-based process is open to systematic bias, and three named biases in attribution recur throughout the chapter and the exam. The fundamental attribution error (FAE) is the tendency for observers to overemphasize dispositional explanations for other people's behaviour at the expense of situational ones; it happens partly because we discount how strongly social roles constrain behaviour (a banker "seems" conservative partly because their job requires it), and partly because we usually observe others only in constrained, constant settings like work or school, which hides how much their behaviour might vary elsewhere. The actor–observer effect is the mismatch between how the same actor explains their own behaviour (situationally) and how an observer explains that very same behaviour (dispositionally); it is most pronounced for negative events and can even reverse for positive ones. The self-serving bias is about a single person's explanation of their own outcomes: taking a dispositional credit for your own successes ("I closed the deal because I'm skilled") while offering a situational excuse for your own failures ("I lost the client because the market turned").

Section 6

Person Perception and Workforce Diversity

Workforce diversity refers to differences among employees or recruits in characteristics such as gender, race, age, religion, cultural background, physical ability, or sexual orientation. Everything covered in Sections 4 and 5 — stereotyping in particular — becomes an organizational liability the moment it operates at scale inside HR systems: a stereotype that biases one manager's read of one resumé is a private misjudgement, but the same stereotype embedded across an organization's hiring, appraisal, and assignment practices produces systematic, measurable disparities in who gets hired, promoted, and developed. The textbook documents this concretely with Canadian evidence of "resumé whitening," where job applicants with ethnic-sounding names have altered their resumés to appear more "mainstream" in response to real, demonstrated hiring bias — a direct, real-world instance of racial and ethnic stereotyping's effect on hiring outcomes, not a hypothetical.

A related but distinct concept is stereotype threat: it occurs when members of a social group feel they might be judged or treated according to a stereotype about their group, and fear that their own behaviour or performance will end up confirming it. Critically, this fear can itself depress performance — for example, on cognitive or math tests, simply making a stereotyped demographic identity salient beforehand has been shown to measurably lower scores among people from the stereotyped group. The important distinction to hold onto is that stereotyping is a bias held by the perceiver (the person doing the judging), while stereotype threat is a burden carried by the target (the person being judged, or fearing they will be) — they are two ends of the same phenomenon, not the same thing.

Diversity climate is the degree to which an organization advocates fair HR policies, promotes equal employment opportunity and inclusion, and socially integrates underrepresented employees; it is positively related to job satisfaction, commitment, engagement, and performance, and negatively related to turnover — meaning diversity climate is not a "nice to have," it is measurably tied to outcomes organizations already care about. A crucial distinction the chapter draws is between tolerating and valuing diversity. Tolerating diversity means having fair hiring practices in place and helping minority members "fit in" to what remains a narrow, unchanged mainstream culture — diversity is accommodated, but the organization itself doesn't change. Valuing diversity goes further: it treats diversity itself as a strategic and competitive advantage. Cox and Blake's competitive-advantage arguments capture this directly — diversity can lower costs (reduced turnover and absenteeism among a more genuinely included workforce), improve resource acquisition (a reputation as an employer of choice for the best candidates from every group), strengthen marketing (better insight into diverse customer segments), and improve creativity, problem-solving, and organizational flexibility.

Managing diversity in practice means moving deliberately past symbolic representation: recruiting enough minority members that they move past "token" status (a lone representative is disproportionately visible and disproportionately taken as representative of their whole group, which itself is a form of stereotyping), developing a formal employment equity plan, offering flexible work arrangements, and encouraging cross-group teamwork so that colleagues form the kind of direct, individuating contact that erodes stereotypes rather than reinforces them. One counterintuitive but well-supported finding: diversity-awareness training by itself — training that simply raises awareness of stereotypes and biases without pairing it with concrete skills — can backfire, activating and rehearsing the very biases it aims to reduce without giving people new behaviours to replace them with. Effective programs pair awareness with skills training, such as structured, bias-resistant interviewing.

Section 7

Perceptions of Trust, Support, and Fairness at Work

Trust is a psychological state involving a willingness to be vulnerable and take risks based on positive expectations of another party's intentions or behaviour — and, notably, it is built from perception, which is why it belongs in this chapter rather than a chapter on formal HR policy. The textbook identifies three distinct components that combine to produce trust in a manager or organization: ability (is this person or organization actually competent and skilled at what they do?), benevolence (does this person or organization genuinely care about my interests, not just their own?), and integrity (does this person or organization adhere to a set of acceptable values and principles, consistently, even when it's costly to do so?). All three are necessary; a highly competent manager who is perceived as caring nothing for their team, or a warm manager who cannot deliver, will both struggle to earn full trust.

Ability
Perceived competence and skill — can this person or organization actually do what it says it can do?
Benevolence
Perceived genuine care for the employee's own interests, beyond what self-interest alone would require.
Integrity
Perceived, consistent adherence to acceptable values and principles — especially when following them is inconvenient.

Perceived organizational support (POS) is an employee's general belief that their organization values their contributions and genuinely cares about their well-being. Organizational support theory (OST) explains why POS matters behaviourally: employees who perceive strong organizational support feel a corresponding felt obligation to care about the organization's welfare in return — the norm of reciprocity, the same basic social instinct that makes people want to return a favour. Because supervisors are the most immediate and visible representatives of the organization to most employees, perceived supervisor support (PSS) is one of the strongest and most direct antecedents of overall POS: how an employee is treated by their direct manager shapes, more than almost anything else, how supported by "the organization" as a whole they feel.

This chapter also sets up, but deliberately does not fully develop, organizational justice — the perceived fairness of outcomes (distributive justice), of the processes used to reach them (procedural justice), and of the interpersonal treatment received along the way (interactional justice). These three justice types return in full in Chapter 4, where they get their own complete treatment; here, it is enough to recognize the term and understand that trust and POS are related to, but conceptually distinct from, organizational justice — they are separate frameworks that happen to sit next to each other in the course, not synonyms.

Worked Example — Trusting a New Manager
An employee's new manager promised, in her first week, that the employee would be first in line for a promotion opportunity opening up next quarter. Two months later, the manager gives that opportunity to someone else without explanation. Exam Trap — Trust's Three Components "Which of the following is NOT one of trust's three components?" is a recurring MC format. The three are ability, benevolence, and integrity — nothing else (not "predictability," not "communication," not "likeability," though these may sound plausible as distractors). Keep this list separate from POS's antecedents and from the three types of organizational justice, which are distinct frameworks covered nearby but tested separately.
Section 8

Person Perception in Human Resources: Interviews and Appraisals

Everything covered so far in this chapter converges in the two HR processes that most directly determine an employee's career: the hiring interview and the performance appraisal. In interviews, several biases already introduced resurface in a specific, high-stakes form. Interviewers frequently compare applicants against an internal, sometimes inaccurate, stereotype of the "ideal applicant" for the role. Primacy effects mean that early information — even something as minor as a pre-interview resumé scan — carries outsized weight in forming the interviewer's overall impression, and negative information, once encountered, tends to be weighted more heavily than positive information. Contrast effects are a distinctly interview-specific bias: how a candidate is rated is skewed by the candidates interviewed immediately before them, so an average candidate looks noticeably worse following two outstanding candidates, and noticeably better following two weak ones — meaning interview order alone, unrelated to any candidate's actual qualifications, can change outcomes.

One useful HR-specific concept for understanding the candidate's side of the interview is signalling theory: because job applicants lack complete information about what an organization and role are really like, they interpret their recruitment and selection experience itself — the tone of the interviewer's questions, whether the process felt fair, how they were treated — as cues, or signals, about what working there would actually be like, which shapes whether they accept an offer if extended. The best-documented remedy for interview bias is the structured interview: standardized, numeric scoring rubrics; job-related behavioural and situational questions; a consistent question order across every candidate; and limited off-topic rapport-building, all of which reduce the room for primacy, contrast, and stereotype effects to operate.

Performance appraisal introduces its own family of rater errors, and this chapter's five need to be kept precisely distinct from one another. Leniency is rating everyone especially favourably, regardless of actual performance variation. Harshness is the mirror image: rating everyone especially unfavourably. Central tendency is rating almost everyone in the middle of the scale, avoiding both extremes even when performance genuinely varies. The halo effect is a rating on one trait colouring the ratings given on unrelated traits — a rater impressed by one dimension of performance lets that impression bleed into how they score every other, unrelated dimension. The similar-to-me effect is favouring ratees who share the rater's own background, attitudes, or interests, independent of actual performance.

The three countermeasures worth knowing by name each target a different part of the problem. Behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS) replace vague scale labels ("good," "average") with specific, concrete behavioural examples anchored to each performance level, giving raters a shared, observable standard rather than a subjective impression to work from. Frame-of-reference (FOR) training has raters practise applying that shared standard to sample cases and receive feedback on how accurate their ratings were, directly training out systematic errors like leniency or halo. 360-degree feedback collects ratings from multiple sources around an employee — supervisor, peers, subordinates, sometimes customers — which dilutes the influence of any single rater's idiosyncratic bias (including their own personal case of similar-to-me or halo) by averaging across many independent perspectives.

Worked Example — Reading an Appraisal Pattern
A manager's annual reviews for her eight direct reports show every single employee rated as "strong" on every one of six unrelated performance dimensions — communication, technical skill, punctuality, teamwork, initiative, and client relations — with almost no variation between employees or between dimensions for the same employee.
Two candidate errors, and how to tell them apart:

LENIENCY would explain uniformly HIGH ratings across
EMPLOYEES (everyone rated favourably) - consistent with
what's described here.

HALO would explain uniform ratings ACROSS DIMENSIONS for
the SAME employee (one trait's rating copied to the rest)
- also partly consistent, since each employee shows little
variation across their own six dimensions.

Here both patterns appear together: ratings are high AND
flat across employees (leniency) AND flat across each
employee's own dimensions (halo) - a realistic appraisal
can show more than one rater error simultaneously, just as
Section 4's hiring panel showed several perception biases
at once.

Fix: introduce a BARS scale with concrete behavioural
anchors for each of the six dimensions (removing the vague
"strong" judgment call) and put the manager through FRAME-

against real behavioural benchmarks rather than a single
generalized, generous impression.
Exam Trap — Halo vs. Similar-to-Me vs. Leniency Halo effect: ONE TRAIT colours ratings of OTHER, unrelated traits for the SAME person. Similar-to-me effect: bias based on RATER–RATEE SIMILARITY (background, attitudes), independent of any trait or scale position. Leniency: EVERYONE is rated favourably, with no reference to similarity or cross-trait spillover at all. A scenario naming a specific shared characteristic between rater and ratee (same university, same hobby, same background) is testing similar-to-me, not halo — a common substitution error.

Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Name the Bias

During a project debrief, a team lead recalls almost nothing about a teammate's strong first three weeks on the project, but forms her entire assessment around a rushed, sloppy deliverable the teammate submitted in the project's final two days. Which basic person-perception bias best explains this, and why is it not the opposite bias?

Show answer
This is the recency effect: the most recent cues (the final, rushed deliverable) dominate the impression, overriding three weeks of earlier, stronger performance. It is not the primacy effect because primacy describes early information dominating a later impression — here, it is the opposite temporal position (the very end of the project) that is doing the dominating. The giveaway is which end of the sequence the decisive cue comes from, not how strong or memorable the cue itself is.
Problem 2 — Which Is NOT

Which of the following is NOT one of the three cues used to form an attribution about someone's behaviour?
a) Consistency   b) Consensus   c) Confidence   d) Distinctiveness

Show answer
(c) Confidence is not one of the three attribution cues. The three are consistency (does the behaviour recur over time?), consensus (do most people behave this way?), and distinctiveness (does the behaviour occur across many situations, or only one?). "Confidence" is a plausible-sounding distractor because it is a real perception-adjacent concept from elsewhere in the chapter (e.g., central traits), but it plays no role in the attribution cue framework specifically.
Problem 3 — Compare Two Concepts

Explain the difference between stereotyping and stereotype threat, and give a one-line example of each that could not be swapped for the other.

Show answer
Stereotyping is a bias held by the perceiver: generalizing about people in a social category while ignoring individual variation (e.g., "assuming an older job candidate won't be comfortable with new software before even reviewing their actual technical background"). Stereotype threat is a burden carried by the target: the fear of confirming a stereotype about one's own group, which can itself depress performance (e.g., "a candidate's math-test performance drops after being asked to note their gender beforehand, because the stereotype about their group becomes salient right before the test"). The key distinction: stereotyping is about who is doing the judging; stereotype threat is about who is affected by the fear of being judged.
Problem 4 — Theorist / Concept Attribution

Match each concept to the framework it belongs to: (a) Bruner (b) Cox and Blake    Frameworks: (i) competitive-advantage arguments for valuing diversity (ii) the perceptual process / narrowing funnel model

Show answer
(a) Bruner → (ii) the perceptual process, describing how an unfamiliar target is categorized and then confirmed through increasingly selective cue search (selectivity, constancy, consistency). (b) Cox and Blake → (i) the competitive-advantage arguments (cost, resource acquisition, marketing, creativity, problem-solving, system flexibility) for why organizations should value, not merely tolerate, diversity.
Problem 5 — Application: "Great Interview, Poor Hire"

A mid-size firm has noticed a troubling pattern over the past two years: candidates who interview exceptionally well are frequently underperforming within six months of being hired, while some strong performers report they "almost didn't get the job" after a mediocre interview. The firm's current process is unstructured — interviewers ask whatever questions come to mind, in whatever order, and candidates are typically interviewed in batches of four to six in a single day. Using concepts from this chapter, diagnose what is most likely going wrong in the firm's hiring process, and recommend specific fixes.

Show answer
Diagnosis: An unstructured, same-day, batch interview format is close to a textbook setup for several compounding perception biases. Contrast effects are likely distorting ratings within each batch of four to six candidates — a candidate interviewed right after two strong performers will look worse than their actual qualifications warrant, and vice versa, which would explain strong performers who "almost didn't get the job." Primacy effects mean unstructured interviews let early impressions (including a strong opening answer or even just a confident handshake) dominate the interviewer's overall rating, rewarding interview performance specifically rather than job-relevant competence — which would explain candidates who interview well but underperform on the actual job, since strong interview skills and strong job skills are only loosely correlated. Interviewers relying on an unstated internal stereotype of the "ideal candidate" compound this further, since with no standardized rubric, each interviewer's idiosyncratic implicit personality theories ("confident speakers must also be strong executors") go unchecked.

Recommendations: Move to a structured interview format: a standardized, numeric scoring rubric tied to job-related behavioural and situational questions, asked in the same order for every candidate, with interviewers scoring immediately after each interview rather than comparing candidates from memory at the end of the day. Spread interviews across multiple days or vary batch order to break up the specific sequences that produce contrast effects. Where possible, incorporate structured work-sample or job-simulation exercises, which test job-relevant skills more directly than a conversational interview and are less susceptible to primacy and central-trait biases built around verbal fluency and confidence. Together, these changes target the actual mechanism — a scoring process vulnerable to order and impression effects — rather than simply telling interviewers to "be more careful," which research on unstructured judgement suggests rarely works on its own.
