The chapter opens with Tabatha Bull, president and CEO of the Canadian Council for Aboriginal Business (CCAB; now the Canadian Council for Indigenous Business), a national non-profit that supports Indigenous businesses and entrepreneurs and bridges Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples, businesses and communities. What Is Leadership? (9.1)

Leadership
Leadership occurs when particular individuals exert influence on the goal achievement of others in an organizational context. Effective leadership uses that influence to achieve organizational goals by enhancing the productivity, innovation, satisfaction and commitment of the workforce. It is about motivating people and gaining their commitment about what needs to be done and how to get there. Effective leaders can change the way people think, feel and behave, with positive effects on individuals, groups, units and even whole organizations. Leadership is also positively related to workplace safety (fewer injuries, accidents and fatalities), so it matters for employees’ well-being as well as for effectiveness.

The text then separates three kinds of leadership that you must be able to tell apart.

Strategic leadership
A leader’s ability to anticipate, envision, maintain flexibility, think strategically, and work with others to initiate changes that will create a viable future for the organization. Strategic leaders can give an organization a sustainable competitive advantage by helping it compete in turbulent, unpredictable environments and exploit growth opportunities. They are open and honest with stakeholders and focus on the future. Research on CEOs finds that their strategic actions influence organizational performance. (Tabatha Bull’s selection as CCAB leader is the text’s example of leadership affecting an organization’s strategy, success and survival.)
Formal (assigned) leadership
Leadership that comes with a formal role. People with titles such as manager, executive, supervisor and department head occupy formal leadership roles: they are expected to influence others and are given specific authority to direct employees. But a formal role is no guarantee of leadership: some managers and supervisors fail to influence anyone, and they are usually judged to be ineffective leaders. Leadership involves going beyond formal role requirements to influence others. This chapter concentrates on formal leadership.
Emergent (informal) leadership
The degree to which an individual with no formal status or authority is perceived by one or more team members as exhibiting leaderlike influence. In other words, it is the extent to which others see you as influential and a leader. It often happens in groups with no formal or assigned leader. Because informal leaders have no formal authority, they must rely on being well liked or being perceived as highly skilled to exert influence.

A related idea is shared leadership: an emergent and dynamic team phenomenon in which leadership roles and influence are distributed among team members, with different members taking the lead at different times (multiple team members emerge as informal leaders). Research finds it is positively related to team performance and team creativity: the more leadership is shared in a team, the better it performs.

Vocabulary Trap — Formal vs. Emergent (and Slide 3’s “Informal”) The textbook’s terms are formal (or assigned) leadership and emergent leadership. The text uses “informal leadership roles” as another way of describing emergent leadership, and slide 3 uses the labels formal and informal. So informal = emergent: the same idea, two labels. Do not confuse emergent leadership with shared leadership: emergent is about one person without a title being seen as a leader; shared is about influence being spread across several members over time. And do not confuse either with strategic leadership, which is not about the formal/informal question at all but about steering the organization’s future (it is usually exercised from the top).

Are Leaders Born or Made? Trait Theory (9.2)

Looking at Tabatha Bull’s track record, you might call her a born leader; given her years of experience you might as easily say she learned. Historically, social observers analyzed figures such as Henry Ford, Martin Luther King, Jr., Barbara Jordan, Ralph Nader and Jack Welch to find what set them apart. The assumption behind that search is that leaders have a special set of traits that distinguish them from followers. That is the trait theory of leadership.

Trait theory of leadership
Leadership depends on the personal qualities, or traits, of the leader. Traits are personal characteristics of individuals, such as physical characteristics, intellectual ability and personality. Research has mostly looked at traits tied to demographics (gender, age, education), task competence (intelligence) and interpersonal attributes (extraversion). Serious scientific attention began in the 1900s: the U.S. military searched for officer traits during the First World War, and the search widened through the Second World War to school children and business executives.

Research on Leadership Traits

The results are mixed. Many, many traits are not associated with becoming a leader or being effective, but some are. Exhibit 9.1 lists the traits most often found to be related to leadership. Leaders (or more successful leaders) tend to be higher than average on them, although the connections are not very strong. Together they describe a high-energy person who really wants to have an impact on others, but who is smart and stable enough not to abuse power.

Memory Aid — I Enjoy Strong Direction; My Early Heroes Need Sleep Exhibit 9.1’s nine traits, in order: Intelligence, Energy and drive, Self-confidence, Dominance, Motivation to lead, Emotional stability, Honesty and integrity, Need for achievement, Sociability. For exam questions of the form “which is NOT a trait associated with leadership?”, remember what was left off: the list does not include things like age, gender, height or education (those are the demographic traits the text says are mostly unrelated), and it does not include narcissism, which is linked to leadership but as a risk, not as an effectiveness trait.

Big Five. All five Big Five personality dimensions are related to leadership emergence and success, but extraversion and conscientiousness are the most consistent predictors of leadership effectiveness. One study found that CEO and top-management-team conscientiousness were directly related to organizational performance, and a recent review concluded that top executives’ personalities can influence firm performance. (The Big Five were taught in Chapter 2.)

Narcissism. Narcissism is an inflated self-view and a general lack of regard for others. Narcissistic CEOs engage in questionable behaviours such as bullying, fraud and distorting financial information, and narcissism is associated with extreme variations in leader behaviour that produce greater performance volatility. The text notes narcissistic leaders tend to be enthusiastic politicians and returns to it in Chapter 12.

Affective and relational outcomes. The relationship between traits and leadership effectiveness is stronger for affective and relational measures (such as satisfaction with the leader) than for performance-related measures. Many organizations nonetheless use trait-based assessments in hiring and promotion decisions.

Research Focus: Motivation to Lead

Motivation to lead (MTL) is the desire to attain leadership roles and to expend effort to fulfil leader role requirements. It helps explain who is attracted to leadership roles and whether they will be effective. Badura and colleagues examined 100 studies and found three separate types of MTL (positively related to each other, but only modestly):

A quick cue for the three types: love it (affective-identity), owe it (social-normative), don’t count the cost (noncalculative).

Limitations of the Trait Approach

Even though some traits relate to leadership emergence and effectiveness, the text gives four reasons the trait approach is not the best way to understand and improve leadership.

  1. Cause and effect are unclear. Do traits make the leader, or does the opportunity for leadership produce the traits? If dominant people become leaders, we might seek out dominant people; if employees become dominant after becoming leaders, that strategy fails. There is evidence that traits can change after taking a leadership role: one study found employees’ conscientiousness increased after they became leaders, because of the job’s greater demands.
  2. It does not say what leaders should do. Knowing that dominance, intelligence or tallness goes with effective leadership gives few clues about how to train and develop leaders or diagnose leadership failures.
  3. It invites bias and discrimination. Believing that people with certain traits make better leaders can distort how leaders are evaluated and promoted. The text says this is one reason for the racial and gender gap in leadership positions (see leadership categorization theory below).
  4. It ignores the situation (the text calls this the most crucial problem). Top executives and first-level supervisors may need different traits, and physical capability may help direct a logging crew but be irrelevant to managing a team of scientists.
Leadership categorization theory
People hold a prototype of what a leader is like, and they are more likely to see someone as a leader, and as an effective one, when they match the prototype. The text reports that leaders (but not employees) are more often assumed to be white by both white and non-white participants, and that white and non-white participants rated white leaders as more effective and as having more leadership potential than non-white leaders when the leader was credited with the organization’s success. The lesson: it is not a good idea to focus exclusively on traits when judging leadership potential, because of this “white-leader effect.”
Vocabulary Trap — Trait Theory vs. Leadership Categorization Theory Trait theory says leaders have certain traits (the claim is about the leader). Leadership categorization theory says perceivers hold a prototype of a leader and judge people against it (the claim is about the people doing the evaluating, and it is a criticism of relying on traits because it explains bias). If a stem is about whom raters assume to be a leader, or about prototypes, the answer is categorization; if it is about personal qualities such as intelligence or extraversion, it is trait theory.

Bottom line. Traits are not sufficient for successful leadership; at most they are a precondition that makes certain actions possible and more likely. There is evidence that part of the reason traits relate to effectiveness is their association with leader behaviours, and that behaviours have a greater impact on effectiveness than traits and, unlike traits, can be learned and developed. That points the way to Section 4.

The Behaviour of Leaders (9.3)

The limits of the trait approach shifted attention from what leaders bring to a group to what they do. The questions: which behaviours cause people to become leaders, how do an assigned leader’s behaviours affect employee performance and satisfaction, and is there one style that is better than the others? The most systematic study was done at Ohio State University in the 1940s. Researchers had employees describe their superiors along many behavioural dimensions, and statistical analysis showed the descriptions boiled down to two basic kinds of behaviour.

Consideration
The extent to which a leader is approachable and shows personal concern and respect for employees. The considerate leader is seen as friendly and egalitarian, expresses appreciation and support, and is protective of group welfare.
Initiating structure
The degree to which a leader concentrates on group goal attainment. The structuring leader clearly defines and organizes their own role and followers’ roles, stresses standard procedures, schedules the work to be done, and assigns employees to particular tasks.

The two are not incompatible and not opposites: a leader can be high, low or average on one or both dimensions.

Two More Behaviours: Reward and Punishment

Leader reward behaviour
Providing employees with compliments, tangible benefits and deserved special treatment. When rewards are made contingent on meeting performance goals and expectations (contingent reward behaviour), employees should perform at a high level and be satisfied: they have a clear picture of what is expected and know positive outcomes follow if they deliver.
Leader punishment behaviour
The use of reprimands or unfavourable task assignments and the active withholding of raises, promotions and other rewards. As Chapter 2 showed, punishment is extremely difficult to use effectively, and when it is seen as random and not contingent on behaviour, employees react with great dissatisfaction.
Memory Aid — Care, Control, Carrot, Cane The text’s four leader behaviours: Care = consideration (approachable, respectful, protective of the group); Control = initiating structure (roles, procedures, schedules, goal focus); Carrot = leader reward behaviour (compliments, benefits, special treatment); Cane = leader punishment behaviour (reprimands, unfavourable assignments, withheld rewards). The shared exam point for the last two: they only work well when contingent on behaviour. A carrot or a cane handed out at random is just unfairness.

The Consequences

Consideration contributes positively to motivation, satisfaction and leader effectiveness and is more strongly related to follower satisfaction (with the leader and the job), motivation and effectiveness. Initiating structure contributes too and is slightly more strongly related to leader job performance and group performance. Contingent reward is positively related to trust in the supervisor, satisfaction, commitment, effort, performance and OCB; contingent punishment goes with more favourable outcomes, non-contingent punishment with unfavourable ones.

For reward and punishment, the relationships are much stronger when they are contingent on employee behaviour, so how a leader administers them is a critical determinant of effectiveness. One reason they work is that they lead to more positive perceptions of justice and lower role ambiguity. The relative importance of consideration and structure also varies with the task, the employee and the setting, which is the doorway to the situational theories in Section 5.

Vocabulary Trap — Three Kinds of “Consideration” (1) Consideration (this section, Ohio State): a leader behaviour, being approachable and showing concern and respect for the whole group. (2) “Initiating consideration”: the phrase on lecture slides 10 and 25. The textbook’s term is simply consideration; the slide has mixed up its own two labels (see the supplement below). (3) Individualized consideration (Section 7): one of the four transformational dimensions, which means treating employees as distinct individuals, caring about their personal development and mentoring or coaching them one-on-one. Ohio State consideration is a style toward the group; individualized consideration is a one-on-one developmental practice. Also: path–goal’s supportive behaviour is “essentially identical” to Ohio State consideration, and directive to initiating structure (Section 5).
Vocabulary Trap — Leader Reward Behaviour vs. Contingent Reward Behaviour Leader reward behaviour is the broad category: compliments, tangible benefits and deserved special treatment. Contingent reward behaviour is reward behaviour made dependent on meeting performance goals and expectations, and it is the form that produces the strong results. It is also exactly the first of the two behaviours that define transactional leadership in Section 7, where the text refers back to this section (“as discussed earlier in the chapter”). So “contingent reward” in Section 7 is not a new idea: it is leader reward behaviour with the contingency attached. The same logic applies to punishment, where contingent versus non-contingent decides whether it helps or hurts.

Section 5

Situational Theories: Fiedler and Path–Goal (9.4)

The situation is the setting in which influence attempts occur: the characteristics of the employees, the nature of the task, and the characteristics of the organization. The basic premise of situational (contingency) theories is that the effectiveness of a leadership style is contingent on the setting, so leadership style must be tailored to the demands of the task and the qualities of employees. The text covers the two best-known and most-studied examples.

Fiedler’s Contingency Theory

Fred Fiedler spent decades developing the first situational theory of leadership. The association between leadership orientation and group effectiveness is contingent on how favourable the situation is for exerting influence. Some situations are more favourable for leadership than others, and they call for different orientations. So the theory has two parts: measure the leader’s orientation, then measure the situation.

Part 1 — Leadership orientation: the LPC scale
Leaders describe their least preferred co-worker (LPC): a current or past co-worker with whom they had a difficult time getting the job done. A leader who describes the LPC relatively favourably (high LPC) is relationship oriented: despite the difficulty, they find positive qualities in the person. A leader who describes the LPC unfavourably (low LPC) is task oriented: they let the person’s low task competence colour their view of their personal qualities (“if they’re no good at the job, then they’re not good, period”). Fiedler argues LPC reveals a personality trait reflecting the leader’s motivational structure: high-LPC leaders are motivated to maintain interpersonal relations; low-LPC leaders are motivated to accomplish the task.
Vocabulary Trap — High LPC Means Relationship, Low LPC Means Task This is the classic reversed definition. High LPC = relationship oriented (you rate the hard-to-work-with person kindly); Low LPC = task oriented (you rate them harshly because they were poor at the job). A high score sounds like “high task focus” but means the opposite. Also: the text says that despite the apparent similarity, the LPC score is not a measure of consideration or initiating structure: those are observed behaviours, whereas LPC is an attitude of the leader toward work relationships. Cue: kind to the difficult colleague = Kind-hearted = relationship.
Part 2 — Situational favourableness
How favourable the situation is for exerting influence. It depends on three factors. Leader–member relations: is the relationship between leader and group good or poor? Task structure: is the task highly structured or unstructured? Position power: has the organization granted the leader formal authority to tell others what to do (strong) or not (weak)? A situation is most favourable when relations are good, the task is structured and the leader has strong position power; it is least favourable when relations are poor, the task is unstructured and position power is weak.

House’s Path–Goal Theory

Robert House, building on the work of Martin Evans, proposed path–goal theory, concerned with the situations under which various leader behaviours are most effective. The most important thing leaders do is clarify the paths to goals that matter to employees (a promotion, a sense of accomplishment, a pleasant work climate), which should promote job satisfaction, leader acceptance and effort. Leader behaviour employees see as unnecessary or unhelpful is resented, and to promote effort leaders must make rewards depend on performance and show how to earn them, which may take direction, guidance and coaching.

Section 6

Participative Leadership and the Vroom–Jago Model (9.5)

Participative leadership
Involving employees in making work-related decisions. “Involving” is intentionally broad: participation is a relative concept, not a fixed property. At the minimum it means getting employee opinions before the leader decides; at the maximum it lets employees make their own decisions within agreed-on limits. As the employees’ “area of freedom” grows, the leader is behaving in a more participative way. There is an upper limit: participation must not be confused with abdication of leadership, which is almost always ineffective.

Exhibit 9.4 (the Tannenbaum and Schmidt continuum) shows the range, from boss-centred leadership (high use of authority by the manager, little employee freedom) to employee-centred leadership (low use of authority, large area of freedom).

Its seven points run from (1) the manager makes the decision and announces it, (2) “sells” it, (3) presents ideas and invites questions, (4) presents a tentative decision subject to change, (5) presents the problem, gets suggestions, then decides, (6) defines limits and asks the group to decide, to (7) permits employees to function within limits defined by the superior.

Potential Advantages and Problems

  • Advantage: motivation and performance. Participation lets employees help set goals, adds task variety and autonomy (Chapter 6), and goes with more satisfaction, task performance and OCB, partly through empowerment and trust in the supervisor.
  • Advantage: quality. “Two heads are better than one,” especially when employees hold superior technical knowledge, and participation empowers them to solve problems directly.
  • Advantage: acceptance. Even when motivation and quality are unaffected, participation raises acceptance of decisions, especially where fairness is at issue (vacation schedules, phone coverage).
  • Problem: time and energy. It uses time, so it is the wrong strategy when a quick decision is needed (“the hospital emergency room is not the place”).
  • Problem: loss of power. Insecure leaders fear it and hand over trivial decisions (“what colour shall we paint the lounge?”), which changes nothing; flatter hierarchies make power-sharing inevitable.
  • Problem: lack of receptivity or knowledge. Employees may resent “doing management’s work,” or lack knowledge, often because they are unaware of external constraints.

Vroom and Jago’s Situational Model of Participation

Victor Vroom and Arthur Jago (building on the original Vroom–Yetton model) try to specify, in a practical way, when leaders should use participation and how much. For issues involving the whole work group they define five strategies. A stands for autocratic, C for consultative, G for group; I means an individual and II means a group is involved.

StrategyWhat the leader doesEmployees’ role
AISolves the problem or decides alone, using information available at the timeNone
AIIGets the necessary information from employees, then decides alone (may or may not tell them the problem)Information providers only, not generating or evaluating alternatives
CIShares the problem with relevant employees individually, gets ideas and suggestions, then decides (may or may not reflect their influence)Advisers, one at a time
CIIShares the problem with employees as a group, gets collective ideas, then decides (may or may not reflect their influence)Advisers, as a group

Which is best depends on the situation. The leader’s goal is high-quality decisions to which employees are adequately committed, without undue delay. To get there the leader works through the diagnostic questions of the decision tree (Exhibit 9.5). All of them aim at preserving either decision quality or commitment.

CodeQuestion the leader asks

By tracing a problem through the tree, the leader arrives at the prescribed degree of participation. In every case the tree gives the fastest (most autocratic) approach that still maintains decision quality and commitment. If the leader is willing to sacrifice some speed, a more participative approach can also stimulate employee development, as long as quality and commitment are not threatened. Following the model makes successful decisions more likely, and it is widely used in management development seminars. You do not need to memorize the whole tree, but you should be able to run a few simple cases through it:

  • Quality unimportant and commitment unimportant (QR low, CR low): AI. Just decide.
  • Quality unimportant but commitment matters (QR low, CR high): ask CP. If employees would accept your solo decision anyway, AI; if they would not, GII. (Think vacation schedules: any fair schedule is fine, but people must accept it.)
  • Quality matters, commitment does not, and you have the information (QR high, CR low, LI yes): AI.

These three paths come from the exhibit’s description and show the logic: the tree escalates participation only when quality or commitment is at risk, and pushes toward the group (GII) when commitment is essential and would not follow automatically.

Memory Aid — Who Decides? A, A, C, C, G Autocratic strategies (AI, AII): the leader decides alone; AII just adds information from employees first. Consultative strategies (CI, CII): the leader still decides but gets ideas first, from employees one by one (CI) or as a group (CII). Group strategy (GII): the group decides and the leader chairs. The sequence is a ladder of rising employee influence: none → information → individual advice → group advice → group decision. For the tree’s eight questions think “two requirements, two leader-side checks, four follower-side checks”: QR, CR (what the decision needs); LI, ST (what the leader and problem offer); CP, GC, CO, SI (what the employees would bring: commitment, shared goals, conflict, information).

Section 7

Relationship-Based Theories: LMX, Transactional and Transformational (9.6)

The earlier sections looked at leader traits and behaviours. This section covers three theories about the nature or type of relationship between leader and employee: leader–member exchange (LMX), transactional leadership and transformational leadership.

Leader–Member Exchange (LMX) Theory

LMX theory
Focuses on the relationship that develops between a leader and each individual employee. It is a social exchange, relationship-based approach. Over time, through their interactions, different relationships develop, so each leader–employee relationship is unique and differs in quality. Effective leadership results when leaders and employees develop and maintain high-quality social exchange relationships. Not every relationship in a workgroup will be high quality; LMX differentiation (LMXD) is the variability in the quality of LMX relationships among members of the same workgroup: leaders treat employees differently.

The basis is social exchange theory and the norm of reciprocity: people treated favourably feel obliged to respond positively and return the favour. Employees in a high-quality relationship reciprocate with extra effort, commitment and performance.

High LMXLow LMX
RelationshipHigh mutual influence and obligation; trust, loyalty, open communication and respectLow trust, respect, obligation and mutual support
What the leader providesChallenging tasks and opportunities, greater latitude and discretion, task-related resources, recognitionLess attention and latitude
What employees doPerform tasks beyond their job descriptionsDo only what their job descriptions and formal roles demand

Research evidence. Higher-quality LMX goes with higher satisfaction, commitment, OCB, role clarity, job performance, creativity and career outcomes, and lower role conflict, counterproductive performance and turnover intentions. The link to performance and OCB is mainly due to trust, and the benefits generalize across cultures. LMXD can raise team performance (by enhancing role clarity) or lower it (through team relationship conflict); it is especially harmful when based on the leader’s personal liking and positively related to effectiveness when based on members’ performance.

Transactional Leadership

Transactional leadership
Leadership based on a straightforward exchange relationship between leader and followers. Leaders set goals and provide direction and support, employees perform well, and the leader rewards them. As in path–goal theory, the leader clarifies expectations and provides the rewards for meeting them. It is the most common style of leadership in organizations, and the text describes the earlier theories in this chapter as traditional, transactional approaches.

It involves two behaviours:

  • Contingent reward behaviour: rewarding followers for meeting performance goals and expectations (introduced in Section 4).
  • Management by exception: the degree to which leaders take corrective action on the basis of the results of leader–follower transactions. They monitor behaviour, anticipate problems and correct them before they become serious. It has two forms: MBE-active (MBEA), routinely monitoring and actively searching for and correcting deviations as they happen; and MBE-passive (MBEP), correcting mistakes only after they have occurred.

Research finds transactional leadership positively related to employee task performance and contextual performance (OCB), and this is mostly due to contingent reward behaviour rather than management by exception.

Vocabulary Trap — MBE-Active vs. MBE-Passive Both are “management by exception” (act when something deviates). Active = watching for deviations and fixing them as they happen. Passive = waiting and fixing mistakes only after they occurred. Do not confuse MBE-passive with laissez-faire leadership (Section 9), which is avoiding or being absent from leadership altogether; the passive manager still steps in once something goes wrong. And do not treat contingent reward as the same thing as MBE: reward is a positive exchange for meeting goals; MBE is corrective action on deviations.

Transformational Leadership

Transformational leadership
Leadership that gives followers a new vision that instils true commitment to a project, department or organization. The leader changes followers’ beliefs and attitudes to match the new vision and motivates them to achieve performance beyond expectations. Examples in the text include Herb Kelleher (Southwest Airlines), Oprah Winfrey, Steve Jobs and Jeff Bezos, but it can also occur in less visible settings, such as a new coach revitalizing a poor soccer team.

The late Bernard Bass studied transformational leaders extensively. He found they are usually good at the transactional parts (clarifying paths to goals, rewarding good performance), plus four key dimensions:

1. Intellectual stimulation
Getting people to think about problems, issues and strategies in new ways. Part of the “new vision” aspect. The leader challenges assumptions, takes risks and solicits followers’ ideas; creativity and novelty are often at work. (Jobs convinced technical staff of the importance of making the Mac extremely user-friendly by raising their consciousness about what it feels like to be a new computer user.)
2. Individualized consideration
Treating employees as distinct individuals, showing concern for their needs and personal development, and serving as a mentor or coach when appropriate. The emphasis is on one-on-one attempts to meet each person’s concerns within the overall mission. Bass notes it is especially striking in military leaders, since military culture generally stresses impersonality and “equal” treatment.
3. Inspirational motivation
Communicating visions that are appealing and inspiring to followers. These leaders have a strong future vision based on values and ideals. They stimulate enthusiasm, challenge followers with high standards, communicate optimism about reaching goals, provide meaning for the task, and inspire through symbolic actions and persuasion.
4. Charisma (idealized influence)
The fourth and by far the most important aspect of transformational leadership. From a Greek word meaning “favoured” or “gifted.” Charismatic individuals command strong loyalty and devotion, which inspires enthusiastic dedication to the leader’s mission. Followers come to trust and identify with the leader and to internalize the leader’s values and goals (Chapter 8’s concepts). Charisma provides the emotional aspect of transformational leadership. It is a complex blend of traits (self-confidence, dominance, strong conviction), behaviours and being in the right place at the right time. Charismatic leaders create an impression of personal success, hold high expectations while expressing confidence in followers (which builds follower self-esteem), set goals with a moral or ideological flavour, and often emerge to articulate followers’ feelings in times of stress or discord.
Memory Aid — The Four H’s of Transformational Leadership: Head, Hand, Horizon, Heart Head = intellectual stimulation (new ways of thinking); Hand = individualized consideration (one-on-one coaching, a hand up); Horizon = inspirational motivation (an appealing vision of the future); Heart = charisma (loyalty, devotion, the emotional bond; the most important). On the exam, scenario questions describe a behaviour and ask which dimension it is: “challenges assumptions” = Head; “coaches each manager” = Hand; “paints an inspiring picture of the future” = Horizon; “followers trust, identify with and are devoted to the leader” = Heart. For transactional leadership remember one carrot and two ways to catch: contingent reward (carrot) plus management by exception, active (alert) and passive (after the fact).
Vocabulary Trap — “Charisma” vs. “Transformational” and Other Overlaps Charisma is one of four dimensions of transformational leadership (also called idealized influence), not a synonym for it, even though many authors simply talk about “charismatic leadership.” Also note that individualized consideration (a transformational dimension: one-on-one coaching) is different from Ohio State consideration (Section 4), and that contingent reward (transactional) is the same behaviour as Section 4’s contingent reward behaviour. Finally, the text calls transformational leadership an example of positive leadership in Section 8, so it is both a “relationship-based” theory here and part of the positive-leadership family there.

Research evidence. Transformational leadership is strongly related to follower motivation, engagement, satisfaction, commitment, leader effectiveness and individual, group and organizational performance; charismatic leadership to follower satisfaction and effectiveness. Compared with contingent reward, it is more strongly related to satisfaction with the leader and leader effectiveness, while contingent reward is more strongly related to job satisfaction and leader job performance. Against other behaviours (initiating structure, consideration, contingent reward) it is the most consistent predictor of effective leadership. CEO transformational leadership predicts organizational performance especially under environmental uncertainty and in small- to medium-sized organizations, and it is especially effective in times of change (employees are more committed to a large-scale change and resist it less). Overall, the best leaders are both transformational and transactional.

Theories of Positive Leadership (9.7)

Over recent decades leadership research has turned to styles that stress ethical and moral leader behaviour (ethical, moral, values-based leadership). Like transformational leadership, these are examples of positive leadership.

Positive leadership
Leadership that focuses on leader behaviours and interpersonal dynamics that increase followers’ confidence and result in positive outcomes, beyond task compliance. Such outcomes include motivating followers to go beyond expectations, positive self-development and prosocial behaviours. The text describes four theories and asks you to notice how each differs from the traditional styles earlier in the chapter.

Empowering Leadership

Traditional theories grew up when organizations were hierarchical and bureaucratic and stressed initiating structure and directive behaviour. Today many organizations are flatter, more flexible and less bureaucratic, and employees have more autonomy, which calls for leadership that lets employees decide and act without direct supervision.

Empowering leadership
Implementing conditions that enable power to be shared with employees. Empowering leaders highlight the significance of employees’ work, provide participation and autonomy in decision making, express confidence in employees’ capabilities, and remove bureaucratic constraints on performance. Employees then feel psychological empowerment: meaning (the work is personally important), competence (belief they can do the tasks), self-determination (freedom to choose how to start and carry out tasks) and impact (belief their behaviour makes a difference).

Evidence: empowering leadership raises satisfaction, commitment, self-efficacy, creativity, OCB, and job and team performance, through psychological empowerment and trust in the leader (and helped employees with less product knowledge most). Chapter 12 returns to empowerment.

Memory Aid — Psychological Empowerment: Mean, Can, Choose, Matter Mean = meaning (“my work is important to me”); Can = competence (“I can do this”); Choose = self-determination (“I choose how”); Matter = impact (“what I do makes a difference”). The leader’s four moves that produce it: highlight significance, give participation and autonomy, express confidence, remove bureaucratic obstacles.

Ethical Leadership

More North American CEOs are being fired for ethical lapses (a 36 percent rise in such turnover over four years in a survey of 2,500 large companies), and Volkswagen’s 2015 emissions scandal shows why ethical leadership matters. Unethical leadership can promote unethical behaviour and harm the organization and its stakeholders.

Ethical leadership
The demonstration of normatively appropriate conduct (e.g., openness, fairness and honesty) through personal actions and interpersonal relationships, and the promotion of such conduct to followers through two-way communication, reinforcement and decision making. Ethical leaders model honesty, trustworthiness, fairness and care; make ethics salient with explicit communications and standards; reward ethical behaviour and discipline or punish unethical behaviour (note the use of contingent reward and punishment); and make principled, fair decisions with ethical consequences in mind.

Research. Ethical leadership goes with more trust and favourable evaluations of the leader, better attitudes, performance and OCB, and less stress, turnover intention and counterproductive behaviour. The link is due to trust in the leader.

  • Promotion-focused ethical leadership (encouraging normatively appropriate behaviour) is positively related to trust and outcomes. Prevention-focused ethical leadership (discouraging inappropriate behaviour) is negatively related to trust and outcomes.
  • The positive effects of promotion-focused leadership are strongest when the leader is perceived to be a moral person: acting ethically is not enough; leaders need strong moral character.
  • Ethical leaders shape the ethical organizational climate (shared perceptions about ethical policies, practices and procedures; Chapter 3), reduce unethical behaviour and relationship conflict in work units, and the immediate supervisor probably has the greatest effect. (Ethics is treated in Chapter 12.)
Applying It: IKEA France (You Be the Manager and The Manager’s Notebook) In 2021 a French court fined IKEA France 1 million euros for spying on union representatives, employees and customers, including paying to access police files. The text’s analysis: this is unethical leadership, a failure of honesty, trustworthiness, fairness and care, likely to have damaged trust, attitudes and the ethical climate. IKEA fired four executives and changed its policy; the recommended fix is to build an ethical organizational climate, hire and develop ethical leaders, train them and hold them accountable. Reuse this pattern (name the style, tie behaviours to the case, recommend) for a Group Assignment ethics case.

Authentic Leadership

Authentic leadership
A positive form of leadership that involves being true to oneself. Authentic leaders know and act upon their true values, beliefs and strengths, and help others do the same; their conduct is guided by internal values, so there is consistency between values, beliefs and actions. They earn followers’ respect and trust and are seen as high in moral perspective and open and fair in decision making.

The text’s example is Maple Leaf Foods’ Michael McCain, who after the 2008 listeriosis outbreak (linked to 23 deaths) apologized, accepted full responsibility and was open about it, saying it flowed from the company’s ingrained values.

Authentic leadership: four behaviours
Self-awareness: an accurate understanding of one’s strengths and weaknesses and of one’s impact on others. Relational transparency: presenting one’s true self and openly sharing information and true thoughts and feelings. Balanced processing: objectively analyzing all relevant information before deciding, including views that challenge one’s own position. Internalized moral perspective: internal moral standards that guide behaviour and decisions, resisting social pressures.
Memory Aid — Know, Show, Weigh, Walk Authentic leadership’s four behaviours: Know yourself (self-awareness), Show yourself (relational transparency), Weigh everything fairly (balanced processing), Walk your values (internalized moral perspective). Keep the order: awareness first, action on values last.

Research. Followers of authentic leaders show more trust, commitment, satisfaction, OCB, engagement, and ethical and prosocial behaviour. In a bank, authentic leadership related to group psychological capital (Chapter 1) and trust, leading to better group citizenship and performance; in military teams, leader authenticity related to member authenticity and better teamwork.

Servant Leadership

Servant leadership
Going beyond one’s own self-interests, with a genuine concern to serve others and a motivation to lead. The focus is on followers’ needs and well-being, and on their growth and development as well as the needs of communities inside and outside the organization. Robert Greenleaf coined the phrase: the servant leader wants to serve first and lead second, rather than lead first.

Gender, Culture and Global Leadership (9.8–9.10)

Gender and Leadership (9.8)

Popular books claim women lead more intuitively, less hierarchically and more collaboratively. Two logics compete: different socialization could teach men and women different ways of influencing others, or both could equally gravitate to whatever style fits the setting, giving no general difference. Reviews find some differences in organizational settings. Eagly and Johnson concluded women tend to be more participative or democratic than men. Two possible reasons: women may have better social skills for managing the give and take participation requires, or women avoid autocratic styles because those violate gender stereotypes and trigger negative reactions.

Gender Bias in Leadership

Despite this, women hold few senior leadership positions in Canada. The text lists exceptions (Tabatha Bull, Catherine Tait of CBC/Radio-Canada, Lisa Lisson of FedEx Express Canada, Paulette Senior of the Canadian Women’s Foundation, Jay-Ann Gilfoy of Meridian Credit Union) and reports that senior-management shares have barely moved in two decades (about 35 percent of Canadian senior management roles, about 5 percent of TSX-listed CEOs), that many organizations have no Black or Indigenous women in the pipeline, and that women executives earn markedly less.

Glass ceiling vs. labyrinth
For decades the explanation was the glass ceiling: an invisible barrier that stops women reaching senior leadership. Eagly and Carli argue a better metaphor is a labyrinth: women face many twists, turns, detours and dead ends along the way up, so the shortage of women leaders is the sum of all barriers, not one barrier at the top.
Role congruity theory (RCT)
Prejudice against female leaders results from an incongruity between the perceived characteristics of women and the perceived requirements of leadership roles. Leaders are perceived as similar to men, as more agentic than communal, and as more masculine than feminine. Men are perceived as having agentic traits (assertion and control) and women as having communal traits (compassionate concern for others). The result is the stereotype “think manager, think male” and “think follower, think female.”

Exhibit 9.6 pairs five agentic traits (dedicated, charismatic, intelligent, determined, aggressive) with five communal traits (caring, sensitive, honest, understanding, compassionate). Example: an agentic leader fights for resources and logs long hours; a communal leader listens during a personal conflict and extends deadlines for family commitments.

Vocabulary Trap — Agentic vs. Communal, and RCT vs. Leadership Categorization Theory Agentic = assertion, control, drive (Act); communal = caring, compassion for others (Community). The bias is about stereotypes of traits, not an actual effectiveness gap: the evidence shows no overall difference in perceived effectiveness. Do not confuse role congruity theory (a mismatch between gender stereotypes and the leader role) with leadership categorization theory (Section 3: people judge leadership by a general prototype, with the text’s evidence about whiteness), though both explain biased evaluation. And keep glass ceiling (one barrier near the top) separate from labyrinth (barriers throughout a career).

Universal vs. non-universal mindsets. People with a universal mindset believe most people have high leadership potential; those with a non-universal mindset believe only some do. A universal mindset goes with less bias against women in leader evaluation and selection, because it blocks gender stereotypes of leadership.

What organizations can do (Exhibit 9.7). Legislation alone is not enough when inequality is embedded in culture and structure. Eagly and Carli recommend: raise awareness of the drivers of prejudice, change the long-hours norm, reduce subjectivity in performance evaluation, use open recruitment instead of informal referrals, ensure a critical mass of women in executive roles (no token or sole female members), build social capital, prepare women for line management, adopt family-friendly HR practices, give parents more time to prove themselves, welcome women back, and encourage male participation in family-friendly benefits. The Diversity Focus example, Apotex’s Women in Leadership Initiative (since 2017), took a grassroots, cultural approach rather than a one-off program, involved men as champions, and used three pillars (developing and networking, empowerment, appointing women to important roles).

Culture and Leadership: The GLOBE Project (9.9)

Are leadership styles equally effective across cultures? The GLOBE (Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness) project is the most extensive study ever of culture and leadership: 170 researchers over 10 years, data from over 17,000 managers in 62 societal cultures. It identified nine cultural dimensions (some similar to Hofstede’s from Chapter 4, many developed by GLOBE) and used them to group the 62 cultures into 10 culture clusters.

Six global leadership dimensions: Charismatic/value-based, Team-oriented, Participative, Humane-oriented, Autonomous, Self-protective: “Charismatic Teams Plan Humane Actions Safely.” Do not merge GLOBE’s nine with Hofstede’s dimensions from Chapter 4: they overlap in places but are separate lists.

GLOBE found that citizens of each nation hold implicit assumptions about what makes a good leader. Implicit leadership theory: individuals hold beliefs about the attributes, personality characteristics, skills and behaviours that contribute to or impede outstanding leadership, which affect whether they accept and respond to others as leaders. When these beliefs are shared across a culture, it is culturally endorsed implicit leadership theory (CLT). GLOBE identified 21 primary and 6 global leadership dimensions:

  • Charismatic/value-based: inspiring, motivating and expecting high performance based on firmly held core beliefs.
  • Team-oriented: effective team building and a common purpose.
  • Participative: involving others in making and implementing decisions.
  • Humane-oriented: supportive and considerate, with compassion and generosity.
  • Autonomous: independent and individualistic leadership.
  • Self-protective: ensuring the safety and security of the individual.

Cultures and clusters differ significantly on all six. For example, Canada and the United States score high on charismatic/value-based, participative and humane-oriented leadership, low on self-protective, and medium on team-oriented and autonomous.

Global Leadership (9.10)

Global leadership
Having leadership capabilities to function effectively in different cultures and being able to cross language, social, economic and political borders. Its essence is the ability to influence people who are not like the leader and who come from different cultures. Global leaders need a global mindset, tolerance for high ambiguity, and cultural adaptability and flexibility. The text’s example is Linda Hasenfratz of Linamar (61 plants in 17 countries; CEO since 2002; 2018 Canada’s Outstanding CEO of the Year).

Gregersen, Morrison and Black identify four characteristics of global leaders:

Developing global leaders. Potential leaders have lived or worked abroad, speak more than one language and have aptitude for global business, but need extensive training. The most powerful strategy is work experience: transfers and international assignments, especially long-term ones (GE, Citigroup, Shell, Siemens, Nokia). Moore and Mintzberg found that trade-dependent middle-economy countries (including Canada, Australia, Sweden and Switzerland) produce more than their share of good global leaders, and that a multicultural setting like Canada is excellent preparation.

Capstone: What Style of Leadership Is Best?

The chapter closes with its answer: “It depends.” Three reasons:

  1. A good leader must be able to use many styles. Consideration and initiating structure are not incompatible, and the best leaders are both transformational and transactional.
  2. The situation determines what works. From the situational theories, the situation consists of the nature of the task, employee characteristics, characteristics of the organization and national culture.
  3. Leaders work through mechanisms. Leadership influences followers’ cognitions (for example, self-efficacy and justice perceptions), affect (attitudes and emotions) and behaviour (actions and motivation).

Problem 1 — Fiedler: LPC and the Octants

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: A team lead describes their “least preferred co-worker” in surprisingly positive terms, even though that person was genuinely difficult to work with. According to Fiedler, this leader is: (A) low-LPC and task-oriented, (B) high-LPC and relationship-oriented, (C) high-LPC and task-oriented, (D) low-LPC and relationship-oriented. (b) Lena has just been made lead of a project team. Staff resent the way she was chosen, the project goals are vague, and she has no authority to assign pay or promotion. Which octant is this, how favourable is it, and which orientation does Fiedler predict will work best? (c) A veteran manager has an excellent relationship with her team and strong formal authority, but the task is new and unstructured. Same three questions. (d) True or false, and why: “A high LPC score shows that a leader scores high on consideration.” (e) Which octant’s prediction does the evidence contradict?

Show answer
(a) (B). Describing the difficult co-worker favourably gives a high LPC score, which Fiedler classes as relationship-oriented. (A) and (D) mismatch score and orientation (low LPC = task-oriented); (C) pairs a high score with the wrong orientation. (b) Octant VIII (poor relations, unstructured task, weak power): the least favourable situation, so Fiedler predicts a task orientation (low LPC), needed to get anything done. (c) Octant III (good relations, unstructured task, strong power): very favourable, so again task (low LPC), since employees are “ready” to be influenced. (With weak power it would be octant IV, medium favourability, favouring relationship.) (d) False. LPC is an attitude (revealing a personality trait), while consideration is an observed behaviour; and high LPC means relationship-oriented. (e) Octant II: the evidence contradicts its task-leadership prescription. Memory check: Relations, Task, Power; task at the tails, relationship in the middle.
Problem 2 — Behaviours and Path–Goal: Which Fits?

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: A manager oversees a routine, highly structured assembly job where employees already know exactly what to do. According to path–goal theory, adding more directive behaviour would most likely: (A) increase satisfaction because directive leaders clarify goals, (B) be seen as redundant and unnecessary, reducing satisfaction and acceptance of the leader, (C) have no effect, since path–goal applies only to ambiguous tasks, (D) work the same as achievement-oriented leadership. (b) Name the best-fitting path–goal behaviour for each: (i) call-centre agents in a tedious, frustrating job; (ii) new analysts who feel low in ability, working on a challenging, ambiguous project; (iii) a sales team of high need achievers; (iv) experienced technicians doing clear, routine work. (c) True or false, and why: “A leader high on consideration must be low on initiating structure.” (d) Which Ohio State behaviour is more strongly related to follower satisfaction, and which to group performance?

Show answer
(a) (B). For clear, routine tasks directive leadership is a redundant imposition that reduces satisfaction and leader acceptance. (A) is the opposite of the prediction; (C) is wrong because the theory predicts different behaviours for different situations; (D) is wrong because achievement-oriented behaviour (challenging goals, expressed confidence) is distinct. (b) (i) Supportive (compensates for a disliked job, though not for effort). (ii) Directive with coaching, and participative too, since challenging but ambiguous tasks welcome both. (iii) Achievement-oriented. (iv) Neither directive nor participative: capable employees on clear tasks find directive leadership irritating and have little to participate in. Memory check: Do, Soothe, Poll, Aim high. (c) False. The two are not incompatible; a leader can be high, low or average on either or both. (d) Consideration relates more to follower satisfaction; initiating structure slightly more to leader and group performance.
Problem 3 — Which Style Is It? Transactional, Transformational, LMX and Positive

(a) Exam-style multiple choice: A new VP announces a major restructuring, communicates a compelling long-term vision for why it matters, coaches each manager individually through the transition, and challenges the team to rethink old assumptions. This is the clearest example of: (A) transactional leadership, (B) transformational leadership, (C) laissez-faire leadership, (D) management by exception–passive. (b) Name the specific concept: (i) a manager checks the dashboard every morning and fixes slips as they happen; (ii) a manager steps in only after a client complains; (iii) a CEO publicly accepts responsibility for a product failure and says it follows from the company’s values; (iv) a manager sets honesty standards, rewards people who report problems and disciplines those who do not; (v) a manager removes red tape, gives staff decision authority and says she is confident in them. (c) A manager gives two favourites the best assignments because she likes them, while everyone else does only the formal job. Which theory applies, and what does the research say about the effect on the team?

Show answer
(a) (B). The scenario shows inspirational motivation (vision), individualized consideration (coaching each manager) and intellectual stimulation (challenging assumptions). (A) is an exchange of rewards for performance; (C) is absence of leadership; (D) fixes problems only after the fact and is not described. Memory check: Head, Hand, Horizon, Heart. (b) (i) Management by exception–active. (ii) Management by exception–passive. (iii) Authentic leadership (acting on true values, with accountability: the Michael McCain example). (iv) Ethical leadership (modelling and promoting conduct with contingent reward and discipline). (v) Empowering leadership (autonomy, confidence, removing bureaucratic constraints). (c) LMX theory with LMX differentiation: favourites (high LMX) get latitude and trust and go beyond their job descriptions; the rest (low LMX) do only formal duties. Differentiation based on personal liking is especially likely to harm the team (relationship conflict); based on performance it is positively related to effectiveness.

(a) A student club’s outgoing president made every decision solo, even trivial ones like meeting snacks, and members grew disengaged. Show answer
(a)(i) Snacks: quality requirement low and commitment requirement low, so the tree gives AI, the fastest strategy that protects quality and commitment; consulting everyone wastes time and energy. (A) and (C) are not what the theory says; (D) is backwards, since women lead more transformationally. (c) Individualistic and risk-taking are culturally contingent; honest and team builder are universal facilitators. He should not assume the first two will be endorsed abroad.
Problem 5 — Application: Harbourline Logistics’ Reliable but Uninspiring Manager

You interned at Harbourline Logistics. Your manager set clear weekly targets, gave specific feedback on your work, paid a bonus fairly whenever you hit your targets, and checked the dashboard daily to correct errors quickly. She never explained why the team’s work mattered or where the company was heading, and turnover on the team was high. You also noticed two long-serving staff got the interesting accounts and decision latitude, while everyone else did only their assigned tasks. Write a Group Assignment-style answer that (1) names and defines the relevant theories, (2) applies them to the manager, and (3) recommends what she should change.

Show answer
Name & define: (1) Transactional leadership: an exchange in which the leader sets goals and rewards performance, made up of contingent reward behaviour and management by exception (active = correcting deviations as they happen). (2) Transformational leadership: a new vision that instils true commitment, through intellectual stimulation, individualized consideration, inspirational motivation and charisma. (3) LMX theory: the quality of each leader–employee relationship (high LMX = trust, latitude, challenging tasks; low = formal duties only); LMX differentiation is variability in quality across a workgroup.

Apply: She practises solid transactional leadership: fair bonuses for hitting targets are contingent reward, the daily dashboard check is MBE-active, and clear targets set expectations. Missing are the transformational dimensions, above all inspirational motivation (she never explains why the work matters), plus individualized consideration and intellectual stimulation. The high turnover is consistent with that gap (an inference, not proven by the text). The two favoured staff show LMX differentiation: a high-LMX pair with latitude and interesting work, and a low-LMX majority doing only the formal job. We are not told whether it rests on liking or performance; the research says it harms the team when based on liking and helps when based on performance.

Recommend: (a) Keep the transactional base, since the best leaders are both. (b) Regularly communicate the team’s purpose and direction (inspirational motivation). (c) Hold one-on-one development conversations with everyone (individualized consideration) and invite ideas on improving the process (intellectual stimulation). (d) Make differences in assignments and latitude performance-based and transparent, and widen high-quality relationships. Expect better commitment and lower turnover, at the cost of more manager time.
