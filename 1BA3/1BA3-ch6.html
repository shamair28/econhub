Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Chapter 5 armed you with seven named theories of what makes people try harder — Maslow’s hierarchy, Alderfer’s ERG theory, McClelland’s theory of needs, self-determination theory, expectancy theory, equity theory, and goal-setting theory. None of that, on its own, tells an actual manager what to do on a Monday morning. Chapter 6 closes that gap. It surveys the concrete organizational practices — how pay is structured, how a job is designed, how objectives are set and reviewed, how a workweek is scheduled — that translate Chapter 5’s abstract mechanisms into policies you could implement, evaluate, or critique.

The throughline connecting every technique in this chapter back to Chapter 5 is worth holding onto as you read: pay-based techniques (Sections 2–5) work because of expectancy theory (pay must be a credible, visible consequence of performance) and equity theory (comparisons with others determine whether a given pay level feels fair); job-design techniques (Sections 6–7) work because of the need theories’ higher-order needs and self-determination theory’s need for competence and autonomy; Management by Objectives (Section 8) is goal-setting theory scaled up into an organization-wide program; and flexible work arrangements (also Section 8) respond to a reality visible throughout Chapter 5’s need theories — a single workforce contains people with genuinely different needs, so no single motivational lever fits everyone. This chapter is also the single most “listy” chapter in the Ch 1–6 midterm range: five wage-incentive problems, three merit-pay problems, four teamwork pay plans, five JCM characteristics with three psychological states and three moderators, six job-enrichment principles, and five flexible work arrangements. Budget your study time accordingly and lean hard on the memory aids below.

Section 2

Money as a Motivator

Employees and managers alike routinely underestimate how much pay actually motivates — when researchers ask managers to rank what motivates their employees, pay usually finishes lower than the evidence justifies, and yet pay is one of the strongest motivators an organization has available. The need theories from Chapter 5 explain part of why: money is unusually flexible as a reward, because it can be exchanged for goods that satisfy needs at every level of Maslow’s hierarchy or Alderfer’s ERG categories — survival and safety most directly, but also the prestige, esteem, and even self-actualization signals that come from a higher salary, a bigger bonus, or a promotion’s pay bump. Expectancy theory explains the mechanism more precisely: pay motivates most when the connection between performing well and being paid well is clear, credible, and immediate — high instrumentality, in Chapter 5’s vocabulary — and pay is typically rated as a highly valent outcome, meaning most people want more of it. When that link is weak, ambiguous, or absent, pay’s motivational power collapses even though the money itself is still attractive.

The umbrella term for any pay tied to a measured performance outcome is variable pay, or pay for performance (PFP). Financial incentives are generally associated with better performance and lower turnover — organizations that link pay to performance tend to out-produce and out-retain those that do not. There is a caution worth knowing, though: some evidence links performance-based pay to elevated mental health risk, particularly for lower performers and older workers, for whom a system that makes pay uncertain and comparison-driven can itself become a source of chronic stress.

The textbook opens the chapter with Costco as a real-world illustration of pay and job design working together as a coherent strategy rather than a set of disconnected perks. Costco made its pandemic-era pay increases permanent, pays above the market rate for retail, and promotes heavily from within — and reports roughly 7 percent annual turnover against a 60–70 percent industry norm for retail. None of that is charity: it is a deliberate motivational strategy, and it is the kind of case this chapter wants you to be able to analyze using the theories from Chapter 5, not just admire.

Which Chapter 5 Theory Explains Which Practice? Keep this mapping straight for the exam: when a question describes pay being contingent on measured performance, that is an expectancy theory mechanism (instrumentality specifically — performance leads to the reward). When a question describes employees comparing their pay to a coworker’s, a promised raise, or an industry benchmark, that is equity theory. Costco’s above-market wage is best read through equity theory (it sets a favourable outside comparison); a piece-rate plan is best read through expectancy theory (it strengthens the performance→pay link directly).
Section 3

Linking Pay to Performance on Production Jobs

The prototype pay-for-performance plan on a production job is the piece-rate: workers are paid a set amount per unit produced, either as a pure piece-rate (all pay is per unit) or as a base hourly wage plus a piece-rate differential for output above a threshold. The broader family of schemes built on this logic is called wage incentive plans, and when individual output genuinely cannot be isolated (an assembly line, a shared machine), organizations use group-level versions instead, dividing the incentive pool among the team.

The evidence on wage incentives is genuinely strong on the headline number: introducing a wage incentive plan is associated with a median productivity improvement of roughly 30 percent, a large effect by the standards of organizational interventions. But the textbook is equally insistent that wage incentives are not a free lunch — they introduce five distinct, well-documented problems, and a large share of this chapter’s exam value sits in being able to name and distinguish all five (Figure 1).

Lowered quality is the most intuitive risk: when a plan rewards quantity, quality can suffer, and it is especially hard to control in “people-processing” work — a phone interviewer paid per completed call has an incentive to rush respondents in a way a factory quality-control station can catch on a physical product but a phone survey often cannot. Differential opportunity is a fairness problem hiding inside a productivity problem: if workers do not have equal access to the raw materials, equipment, or assignments needed to hit high output, the incentive becomes unfair through no fault of the lower-output worker — read through expectancy theory, it is really an expectancy problem, because the same effort does not produce the same performance across workers with unequal opportunity. Reduced cooperation shows up when an individual incentive makes workers protective of anything that helps them personally hit their number — hoarding tools, guarding shortcuts, or neglecting shared, uncredited tasks like cleanup and equipment maintenance that do not count toward anyone’s individual total.

Incompatible job design is what happens when the way work is actually organized makes it hard to attribute output to one person in the first place — classically, an assembly line, where one person’s pace is constrained by everyone else’s. The same logic explains why team incentives lose power as team size grows: the larger the team, the weaker the link between any one member’s individual effort and their share of the payout, which erodes the incentive’s motivational force even though the plan is nominally still “pay for performance.” Restriction of productivity is the most purely social of the five, and the trap to avoid is treating it as a design flaw like incompatible job design — it is a group-norm problem. Workers on an incentive plan sometimes informally agree not to exceed a self-imposed output ceiling, historically out of fear that management will respond to high output by “cutting the rate” — demanding more units for the same piece-rate pay. Where trust between labour and management is high, this fear, and the restriction behaviour it produces, is less likely.

Section 4

Linking Pay to Performance on White-Collar Jobs

White-collar performance is harder to reduce to a countable unit than a warehouse pick or a stitched shirt, so organizations use merit pay plans instead of wage incentives: a manager’s periodic — usually annual — rating of an employee’s performance, translated into a pay increase that is folded permanently into base salary. Merit pay is far more common in practice than wage incentive plans, precisely because it can be applied to jobs where “output” is not a physical count. Despite that popularity, survey evidence the textbook cites is blunt: most merit pay systems are only “somewhat successful or not working at all” — and the reasons cluster into three specific, memorizable problems.

Low discrimination is a rater problem, not a formula problem: without a well-designed rating system, and often to avoid the discomfort of a difficult conversation, managers rate most employees as similar performers. The effect is a system that over-rewards weak performers relative to their actual contribution and under-rewards strong ones — which, from an equity theory standpoint, is exactly the condition that erodes a top performer’s sense that effort is fairly exchanged for outcomes.

Small increases is a budget problem: raises too small to be motivationally meaningful — most likely during the inflationary or economically difficult periods when motivation matters most, because merit budgets shrink at exactly the wrong time. One documented fix is the lump-sum bonus: a one-time payment, more visible than a permanent 1–2 percent base-salary bump precisely because it is not diluted across twenty-six pay periods. But a lump-sum bonus carries its own risk — a poorly designed bonus can incentivize employees to take unethical shortcuts to hit the metric it is tied to, so “more visible” is not automatically “better designed.”

Vocabulary Trap — Merit Pay vs. Lump-Sum Bonus A merit raise is added to base pay permanently — it compounds into every future raise calculated as a percentage of salary. A bonus is a one-time payment that does not become part of base pay and does not compound. A question that describes a payment that “recurs every year automatically” is describing a merit raise; one that describes a payment “paid out once, separately from salary” is a bonus.

Pay secrecy is the subtlest of the three and, on the evidence, arguably the most damaging: most organizations keep salaries confidential, which means even a well-designed, fairly administered merit system can still fail to motivate, because employees have no way to verify that the pay-performance link the plan claims to offer is actually operating. In the absence of real information, people do not simply withhold judgment — they invent salary estimates, and the textbook’s Exhibit 6.2 finding is a specific, testable detail worth memorizing: employees systematically overestimate what their peers and subordinates earn and underestimate what their boss earns. Both distortions damage motivation simultaneously — overestimating peers’ pay makes your own raise look comparatively smaller (an equity theory problem), while an unverifiable pay-performance link damages instrumentality (an expectancy theory problem). A small number of organizations, Whole Foods among them, use open-book pay policies instead, publishing salary information so the pay-performance link becomes checkable rather than assumed.

Memory Aid — “Merit pay fails when it’s Low, Small, and Secret” Low discrimination · Small increases · Secret (pay secrecy). Self-test: match each to the Chapter 5 theory it damages — low discrimination and small increases both undercut instrumentality/valence (the performance→pay→reward chain); secrecy undercuts equity perceptions specifically, since it is what forces employees to invent comparison estimates in the first place.

Section 5

Using Pay to Motivate Teamwork

Pay can also be used to motivate a team or an entire organization rather than a single individual, and the textbook groups four such plans together for comparison (Exhibit 6.3, Figure 2). What differentiates them is not just what gets rewarded but how directly an individual employee can see their own effort reflected in the payout — a property researchers call line of sight. The four plans sit on a rough spectrum from weakest to strongest line of sight.

Profit sharing returns a share of overall company profit to employees, in cash or deferred form. Its advantage is that it fosters a real sense of ownership and aligns everyone’s goals with the organization’s bottom line. Its disadvantage is line of sight: company profit is affected by an enormous number of factors completely outside any individual employee’s control — the broader economy chief among them — and in a large organization, the connection between any one person’s daily work and the profit figure is nearly invisible. Profit sharing therefore works best in smaller, reliably profitable firms, where the line from effort to profit is short enough to actually see.

Employee stock ownership plans (ESOPs) let employees buy company shares, sometimes with an employer match, creating a form of legal as well as psychological ownership. The advantage is similar to profit sharing’s — real alignment of employee and company goals — but share value, like profit, is driven by far more than employee effort, and an ESOP’s motivational power fades in a weak economy when share prices fall for reasons no employee caused.

Gainsharing narrows the target: rather than tying a bonus to company-wide profit, it ties the bonus to measurable, controllable productivity or cost gains within a specific unit — reduced scrap, lower energy use, fewer defects — and is typically designed with heavy workforce participation in identifying what to measure and how to share the gains. The classic version is the Scanlon Plan, developed by Joe Scanlon in the 1930s, which pairs participatory problem-solving with a formula for sharing the resulting gains. Gainsharing’s advantage is that it aligns organizational and employee goals while genuinely rewarding cooperative, problem-solving behaviour; its disadvantage is that a bonus might be paid out even in a year the company as a whole is not profitable, and objectives that fall outside the formula can be neglected as everyone’s attention narrows to what the formula measures.

Skill-based pay (also called pay-for-knowledge) ties pay increases to the number of job-relevant skills an employee has acquired, rather than to output or profit at all — useful in self-managed teams and flexible manufacturing environments where cross-trained flexibility itself has value. Its advantage is a genuinely more flexible workforce with a broader view of the whole work process; its disadvantage is cost: training costs rise, and as employees accumulate more skills over time, so does the underlying wage bill.

Memory Aid — “PEGS” Profit sharing · ESOPs · Gainsharing · Skill-based pay. The order runs from broadest and weakest line of sight (company profit) to most individual (your own skills). Trap: gainsharing rewards cost savings/productivity gains in a unit, not company profit — don’t swap it with profit sharing.
Vocabulary Trap — Profit Sharing vs. Gainsharing Profit sharing distributes a share of overall company profit — a number driven by the economy, pricing, and competition, none of which any one employee controls. Gainsharing distributes a share of measurable, unit-level cost or productivity gains (scrap reduction, energy savings) that the workforce can actually see themselves influence. A scenario mentioning “reduced waste” or “cost savings the team helped identify” is gainsharing; one mentioning “the company’s overall profit” or “the fiscal year’s earnings” is profit sharing.

Section 6

Job Design: Traditional Views and the Job Characteristics Model

Job design is the structure, content, and configuration of a person’s work tasks and roles — in effect, the blueprint for what a job actually asks someone to do and how much say they have in doing it. The traditional view of job design, rooted in Frederick Winslow Taylor’s scientific management, pursued efficiency through job simplification and specialization: break work into its smallest repeatable components, assign each worker one narrow component, and motivate performance through close supervision and piece-rate pay. For an unskilled, needs-driven historical workforce, this was a reasonably good fit — simple, controllable, and aligned with workers whose most pressing needs (in Maslow’s terms) were lower-order. For a better-educated modern workforce with different needs, the same design logic tends to produce the opposite of motivation: boredom, disengagement, and turnover.

One useful way to describe how far a job has moved from the traditional model is job scope, defined as breadth (the number of different activities a job involves) multiplied by depth (how much discretion and control the worker has over how those activities get done). Figure 3 plots this as a 2×2 grid. A job high on both breadth and depth — a professor, a manager — is a high-scope job; a job low on both — the classic assembly-line position — is low-scope. The two off-diagonal quadrants matter too and are a common exam distractor: a job can be broad but shallow (an assembly-line utility worker who rotates across many stations but controls none of them) or narrow but deep (a quality-control inspector who does one thing, but decides how). Both of these are still considered relatively low-scope for motivational purposes, because true motivating potential needs both dimensions together, not just one. Two concrete ways to raise scope without a full redesign are stretch assignments — giving someone a larger, more challenging project than their current role would normally include — and job rotation, moving employees through different tasks or departments, which raises breadth and doubles as a skill-development tool.

The chapter’s central framework for connecting job design to intrinsic motivation is Hackman and Oldham’s Job Characteristics Model (JCM), and it rewards being memorized component by component rather than just gestured at (Figure 4). Five core job characteristics feed into three critical psychological states, which in turn produce a specific set of outcomes, with several moderating variables gating how strongly the whole chain operates for a given person.

The five core characteristics: skill variety (the range of skills and activities the job actually uses — closely related to job breadth), task identity (whether the job involves doing a whole, identifiable piece of work from start to finish, rather than one fragment of it), task significance (how much the job’s output affects other people’s lives), autonomy (freedom to schedule one’s own work and choose how tasks get done — closely related to job depth), and feedback (information about how well one is performing, coming directly from the job itself rather than from a supervisor’s periodic review). These map onto three critical psychological states in a specific 3-1-1 pattern the model insists on: skill variety, task identity, and task significance jointly produce experienced meaningfulness of the work; autonomy on its own produces experienced responsibility for outcomes; and feedback on its own produces knowledge of results, the actual results of one’s work activities. The psychological states in turn produce the model’s outcomes: high internal work motivation, high “growth” satisfaction, high general job satisfaction, and high work effectiveness.

The model’s summary statistic is the Motivating Potential Score (MPS), and it is worth memorizing in exactly this form: MPS = [(Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance) / 3] × Autonomy × Feedback. Because Hackman and Oldham’s Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) rates each of the five characteristics on a 7-point scale, MPS can theoretically range from 1 to 343. The textbook reports real examples to anchor this range: a routine keypunching job scored about 20, while a lower-level managerial job in the same research scored 159; averaged across 6,930 employees in 876 different jobs, the mean MPS was 128. The formula’s structure is itself a frequent exam target: skill variety, task identity, and task significance are averaged together, so they are compensatory among themselves — a job can be a little low on one and make it up with the others. Autonomy and feedback, by contrast, are multiplied into the score, not averaged in — which means a job that scores zero on either autonomy or feedback collapses its entire MPS to zero, regardless of how rich the other characteristics are. (If this multiplicative structure looks familiar, it should: it is the same logic as Chapter 5’s expectancy-theory Force formula, where a zero expectancy collapses total force to zero no matter how valent and instrumental the reward is.)

The JCM does not claim that a high-MPS job automatically produces the outcomes column for everyone. Three moderating variables gate the relationship: knowledge and skill (a worker who lacks the relevant competence can be overwhelmed rather than motivated by a highly enriched job), growth need strength (how strongly a given person desires higher-order need satisfaction from their work — those high in growth need strength respond best to challenging, enriched jobs), and context satisfactions (satisfaction with surrounding factors like pay, supervision, and company policy — dissatisfaction here dampens how responsive someone is to an otherwise well-designed job). Research support across these components is uneven: experienced meaningfulness is the most consistently supported psychological state, while support for growth need strength and context satisfaction as moderators is comparatively weak.

Memory Aid — “a VISA Fee” Skill Variety · Task Identity · Task Significance · Autonomy · Feedback. Then the 3-1-1 mapping: V+I+S → experienced meaningfulness; A → experienced responsibility; F → knowledge of results. The MPS formula has the same shape: average the first three, multiply by A, multiply by F. Trap: task identity (doing a whole, identifiable piece of work) vs. task significance (impact on other people) — see the callout below.
Vocabulary Trap — Task Identity vs. Task Significance Task identity is about completeness: does the job produce a whole, identifiable outcome from start to finish (a cabinet maker who designs, builds, and finishes a piece) rather than one repeated fragment (a lathe operator who only makes table legs)? Task significance is about impact on others: does the job’s output matter to other people’s lives, inside or outside the organization? A scenario about a worker seeing a job “through to completion” is task identity; a scenario about a worker’s output “affecting patients, customers, or coworkers” is task significance.
Worked Example — Computing MPS for Two Part-Time Jobs
You are weighing two part-time jobs using the Job Diagnostic Survey’s 1–7 scale. Job A, a call-centre customer service role: skill variety 6, task identity 2, task significance 3, autonomy 2, feedback 5. Job B, a research assistantship: skill variety 4, task identity 5, task significance 5, autonomy 6, feedback 6. Which job has more motivating potential — and does the job with the higher skill variety rating win?
JOB A (call centre):
  (Skill variety + Task identity + Task significance)/3
    = (6 + 2 + 3) / 3 = 11 / 3 = 3.67
  MPS = 3.67 x Autonomy(2) x Feedback(5)
      = 3.67 x 2 x 5 = 36.7

JOB B (research assistantship):
  (Skill variety + Task identity + Task significance)/3
    = (4 + 5 + 5) / 3 = 14 / 3 = 4.67
  MPS = 4.67 x Autonomy(6) x Feedback(6)
      = 4.67 x 6 x 6 = 168.0

CONCLUDE: Job B has almost 5x the motivating potential
  of Job A (168 vs. 36.7), even though Job A scores
  HIGHER on skill variety alone (6 vs. 4). The reason
  is the formula's multiplicative structure: Job A's
  weak autonomy (2) and weak task identity (2) drag
  the whole score down, and because autonomy
  multiplies rather than averages in, a low autonomy
  score punishes the total far more than one strong
  characteristic can rescue it. Skill variety alone

  one-third of one of the three averaged, then
  multiplied, terms.

Section 7

Job Enrichment, Work Design, and Relational Job Design

Job enrichment is the deliberate redesign of a job to raise its core characteristics — and with them, intrinsic motivation, quality of working life, and job involvement, a psychological state of identifying with one’s job as central to one’s self-image. There are no fixed rules for how to enrich a specific job; good enrichment depends on a careful diagnosis of the work, the available technology, and the organizational context. But most enrichment schemes draw on the same six recurring principles (Figure 5), each aimed at raising one or more of the JCM’s core characteristics.

  • Combining tasks — assigning what used to be several separate jobs to one person; in a furniture factory, a lathe operator, assembler, sander, and stainer can become four “chair makers,” each doing all four tasks. Raises skill variety and, as each worker follows a unified job from start to finish, task identity.
  • Establishing external client relationships — putting employees directly in touch with people outside the organization who depend on their output, such as forwarding customer letters straight to the line workers involved. Raises task significance, sometimes skill variety (new interpersonal skills), and feedback.
  • Establishing internal client relationships — the same idea applied inside the organization, permanently pairing support staff (billers, expediters) with specific internal colleagues rather than routing work anonymously. Same advantages as the external version.
  • Reducing supervision or reliance on others — increasing autonomy and control over one’s own work, e.g., letting clerical staff check their own work for errors, or authorizing purchases up to a dollar limit without approval.
  • Forming work teams — replacing a sequence of small individual jobs with a stable team that completes an entire product or service together, rather than passing a client or product from person to person. Builds skill variety and task identity.
  • Making feedback more direct — routing performance information straight to the person who did the work, e.g., an assembler’s name and contact information travelling with the product so customers reach them directly rather than a generic service line.

Despite its theoretical appeal, job enrichment runs into five recurring problems. Poor diagnosis covers two opposite failure modes: half-hearted changes that do not really raise motivating potential (most often, simply giving someone more same-level tasks — job enlargement, the chapter’s classic exam trap, discussed below), and the reverse error of over-enriching an already-rich job, sometimes called job engorgement, which produces role overload rather than motivation, particularly in downsized organizations where survivors are handed too many extra responsibilities. Lack of desire or skill acknowledges that not everyone wants a more demanding job, and even workers who welcome the idea in principle may lack the skills — sometimes requiring costly training, and some skills (social skills, for instance) are simply hard to train. Demand for rewards is an equity-flavoured problem: workers whose jobs are enriched sometimes ask for greater extrinsic rewards to match their new responsibilities, as happened at a General Foods dog food plant in Topeka, Kansas, where employees with radically enriched jobs sought a bonus tied to the redesign’s success. Union resistance reflects North American unions’ historical preference for negotiating over easily quantified extrinsic motivators (pay) and for narrow job classifications seen as protecting members’ jobs — though global competition has pushed many unions and employers toward dismantling these restrictive provisions. Supervisory resistance is the most structurally interesting: because enrichment increases the autonomy of the employees whose jobs are redesigned, it can simultaneously “dis-enrich” their supervisor’s job — some organizations have responded by removing direct supervision of enriched roles entirely, or repositioning the supervisor as a trainer and developer instead.

Vocabulary Trap — Job Enrichment vs. Job Enlargement Enrichment adds depth — genuine control, responsibility, and richer core characteristics. Enlargement adds breadth only — more tasks at the same level, with no change to autonomy, identity, significance, or feedback. A part-time retail job where “more responsibility” just means more repetitive tasks at the same skill level is enlargement, not enrichment, and will not boost intrinsic motivation. This is the single most tested reversed-definition trap in the chapter.
Memory Aid — The Five C’s of Enrichment (Six Principles) Combine tasks · Connect to clients (external and internal — two separate principles) · Cut supervision · Create work teams · Close the feedback loop. Self-test: map each principle onto the JCM characteristic it raises — combine → variety & identity; clients → significance & feedback; cut supervision → autonomy; teams → variety/autonomy; feedback → feedback.

Work design (Morgeson and Humphrey) broadens the JCM into a wider model they deliberately call work design rather than job design, because it acknowledges the job itself and its surrounding work environment together. Their work design characteristics (Exhibit 6.7) fall into three categories. Motivational characteristics include task characteristics essentially equivalent to the JCM’s five core characteristics, plus knowledge characteristics — job complexity, information processing demands, problem solving, skill variety, and specialization. Social characteristics cover the interpersonal side of work: social support, interdependence, interaction outside the organization, and feedback from others (as opposed to feedback from the job itself). Work context characteristics cover the physical and environmental side: ergonomics, physical demands, work conditions, and equipment use. Morgeson and Humphrey’s Work Design Questionnaire (WDQ) measures all of this and is currently the most comprehensive available tool for the purpose. One finding worth remembering specifically: social characteristics are even more strongly related to some outcomes — turnover intentions and organizational commitment in particular — than the JCM’s motivational characteristics are, a reminder that who you work with and how supported you feel can matter as much as the design of the task itself.

Adam Grant’s relational job design takes a different angle again, focusing on a job’s relational architecture — the structural properties of work that determine how much opportunity an employee has to connect with the people affected by their work. The goal is to build prosocial motivation, the desire to expend effort specifically to benefit other people. Jobs vary naturally in their built-in potential for this kind of impact — a surgeon or firefighter has obvious, frequent opportunities to see the difference they make; a cashier or janitor typically does not — but Grant’s research shows that any job can be relationally redesigned to make that impact visible. The most-cited evidence is a series of studies on university call-centre fundraisers: callers’ persistence and the money they raised rose sharply after a brief, direct exposure to a scholarship recipient whose education their calls had funded, and in a related study callers more than doubled their weekly pledges after simply reading stories about students their predecessors had helped.

All of the approaches above are organization-driven — someone else redesigns the job. Job crafting flips that: it is employee-initiated, self-directed change to one’s own job demands and resources to better fit personal abilities and needs, and it takes four forms (Exhibit 6.8): increasing social job resources (asking for feedback, advice, or support), increasing structural job resources (seeking more autonomy or skill variety), increasing challenging job demands (volunteering for more responsibility or a harder project), and decreasing hindering job demands (reducing an unsustainable workload or a source of conflict). Both personality (agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, openness, proactive personality, and general self-efficacy) and job characteristics (autonomy, workload) predict how much someone job-crafts, and there is some evidence women engage in more job-crafting behaviour than men. The payoff is real: job crafting is positively related to job satisfaction, engagement, and performance, and negatively related to strain and burnout, with increasing structural job resources standing out as the single most consistently important type for predicting these outcomes. A brief, related idea worth knowing by name: work gamification, applying game features (points, levels, badges, leaderboards) to work tasks to increase intrinsic motivation through real-time feedback and a sense of accomplishment — used at organizations like Google and Zappos, and worth recognizing if it appears as a distractor alongside job crafting or enrichment.

Worked Example — Redesigning a Call-Centre Agent’s Job
A telecom company’s call-centre agents handle inbound billing questions, following a rigid script, with calls randomly assigned by a queue system and no visibility into whether a customer’s issue was actually resolved after the call ends. Section 8

Management by Objectives and Flexible Work Arrangements

Management by Objectives (MBO), developed by management theorist Peter Drucker, is what goal-setting theory looks like when it is scaled up into a formal, organization-wide program rather than left to an individual manager’s discretion. In a well-designed MBO program, objectives for the organization as a whole are set by top management and cascade downward, translated at each level into specific, behavioural objectives for individual employees — “objectives” is simply MBO’s term for goals.

The recurring interaction between a manager and an individual employee follows the same cycle regardless of the specific organization: first, the manager and employee jointly develop objectives for the coming period — made as specific and, where feasible, quantified as possible, with an explicit time frame and, often, a priority ranking, all put in writing; second, periodic progress-monitoring meetings check in on how things are going, with objectives revised if circumstances change; third, an appraisal meeting evaluates how much of the agreed-upon objectives were actually achieved, treated explicitly as a learning exercise rather than a verdict; and the cycle then repeats. Hewlett-Packard ran MBO as the cornerstone of its management philosophy for nearly six decades, and at Toronto-based Janssen Inc., employee bonuses of up to 20 percent are tied directly to a cascading list of corporate objectives.

The research evidence on MBO comes with one large, specific caveat worth memorizing exactly: a review of MBO programs found a 56 percent average productivity gain among programs with high top-management commitment, against just 6 percent for programs with low commitment. Without genuine commitment from the top, managers further down simply go through the motions, objectives get set haphazardly, and the whole program degenerates into what employees describe — accurately, at that point — as “just a bunch of paperwork.” Other documented failure modes: an overemphasis on easily quantified objectives (production numbers) at the expense of equally important but harder-to-measure ones (employee development), excessive short-term orientation, and appraisal meetings that curdle into punitive browbeating rather than the developmental conversation MBO is supposed to produce.

Exam Trap MBO is not a separate theory of motivation — it is goal-setting theory applied as an organization-wide program. A question asking “which theory underlies MBO” wants goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham), not expectancy theory or anything else. MBO’s objectives are specific, time-framed, and set with employee participation for commitment, with progress feedback built into the review cycle — the exact ingredients goal-setting theory says a motivating goal needs.

Where pay and job design aim primarily to raise effort and performance, flexible work arrangements have a different primary purpose: not to motivate harder work, but to meet the needs of a genuinely diverse workforce, support work–life balance, and thereby aid recruitment and retention while reducing absenteeism and turnover. This distinction is itself a frequent exam target — a question may test whether you understand that not every motivational practice in this chapter is trying to raise output. Five arrangements matter (Figure 6).

Flex time keeps the total required hours fixed but lets employees choose when, within limits, they arrive and leave, as long as they are present for defined core hours. A typical structure (Exhibit 6.9, Figure 7) allows arrival any time from 7:00 a.m., with core hours from 9:15 a.m. to noon and 2:00 to 4:15 p.m., a flexible midday band for lunch (with a minimum half-hour required), and departure any time up to 6:00 p.m., as long as the total daily hours are met. Flex time is a poor fit for tightly interdependent work — an assembly line, a hospital surgical team — because it can create supervisory-coverage gaps and breaks coordination, which is why it is concentrated almost entirely in office environments (banks, insurers, and government offices are the textbook’s examples). The research evidence is consistently positive: employees strongly prefer it to fixed hours, work attitudes improve, abuse of the system is minimal, and reviews find a positive effect on productivity, job satisfaction, and schedule satisfaction, along with lower absenteeism and turnover.

The compressed workweek packs the same total weekly hours into fewer days — most commonly the 4–40 pattern, four 10-hour days instead of five 8-hour days. It reduces commuting time and cost by roughly 20 percent and can appeal to dual-earner households who stagger their days off to cover childcare, but the longer individual days bring real fatigue, especially for physically demanding work. The evidence is more mixed than for flex time: workers like it and it raises job and schedule satisfaction, but a review found no reliable effect on absenteeism, job performance, productivity, or physical health — some of the fatigue-driven recovery days off can even offset attendance gains.

Job sharing and work sharing sound similar and are the pair most likely to be confused on an exam question, but they solve different problems. Job sharing is two part-time employees splitting one full-time job’s duties (and often its benefits) — useful for retaining skilled people who want reduced hours (a parent, someone caring for an elderly relative) but requiring real coordination between the two job-holders. Work sharing reduces everyone’s hours to avoid layoffs during a downturn; in Canada it is often paired with a federal Employment Insurance work-sharing program that supplements the reduced wages. Real Canadian examples the textbook cites include Rogers Communications offering staff a voluntary 20 percent hour-and-pay reduction to avoid broader layoffs, and Buhler Industries in Winnipeg moving to a three-day week for 200 employees rather than laying off 90 and closing its tractor plant for four months.

Telecommuting (also called telework or remote work) lets employees work from a remote location while staying connected through information and communication technology. The pandemic made it the default experience for millions of Canadians almost overnight, and it is now widely viewed as a permanent fixture, typically in hybrid form. The research evidence traces telecommuting’s benefits mainly to one mechanism: a review found telecommuting’s positive effects on job performance and organizational citizenship behaviour are mediated primarily through increased perceived autonomy — working remotely does not motivate people through some separate mechanism, it motivates them largely because it makes them feel more autonomous, tying this arrangement conceptually back to the JCM’s autonomy characteristic from Section 6. Downsides include reduced visibility for promotion consideration under very heavy telecommuting, workload spillover onto colleagues who remain in the office, and — especially salient from pandemic-era research — isolation, loneliness, and burnout risk with excessive remote time. The textbook’s nuance on career outcomes is specific: those who telecommute only occasionally, rather than extensively, get the best career results overall.

Memory Aid — Flexible Work by What It Flexes When (flex-time) · How many days (compressed workweek) · How much / by whom (job sharing, work sharing) · Where (telecommuting). Self-test: given an arrangement's description, name what it flexes before naming the arrangement itself.
Vocabulary Trap — Job Sharing vs. Work Sharing Job sharing = two people voluntarily split one full-time job on an ongoing basis. Work sharing = employees accept reduced hours and pay, usually firm-wide and temporarily, specifically to avoid layoffs during a downturn. A scenario about "saving jobs during a slowdown" is work sharing; a scenario about "two people permanently splitting one role" is job sharing.

No single motivational practice in this chapter is “correct” in the abstract. The right choice is a contingency decision (Exhibit 6.10) resting on four factors: employee needs, the nature of the job (individual versus group-based work), organizational characteristics (strategy, culture), and the outcome the organization actually wants — performance, retention, and satisfaction are not always served by the same lever. The most effective organizations typically combine several motivators rather than betting everything on one — performance-based pay alongside job enrichment, for instance — because each technique in this chapter targets a different piece of the motivational picture Chapter 5 laid out.

Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Name the Wage-Incentive Problem

A print shop moves press operators onto an individual piece-rate for finished t-shirts. Within a few weeks, operators start stashing the good ink cartridges in their own lockers instead of leaving them at the shared station, and the communal trimming table — nobody’s individual job, but essential to everyone’s workflow — keeps running out of stock because no one restocks it anymore. Which of the five wage-incentive problems is this, and why is it not one of the others?

Show answer
This is reduced cooperation: the individual incentive is causing operators to hoard a shared resource (ink cartridges) and neglect a shared task (restocking the trimming table) that does not count toward anyone’s personal output total. It is not differential opportunity, which describes unequal starting materials or equipment across workers rather than workers actively withholding resources from each other; it is not restriction of productivity, because there is no evidence operators are capping their own output out of fear of a rate cut — if anything, they are still producing; and it is not incompatible job design, since t-shirt pressing output is clearly attributable to each individual operator.
Problem 2 — Choose the Team Pay Plan

A 4,000-employee auto parts manufacturer wants a bonus plan for one specific stamping plant, tied to reductions in scrap metal and energy costs that the plant’s own workforce can directly influence, designed using extensive input from the floor. Which of the four team-oriented pay plans is the best fit — profit sharing, an ESOP, gainsharing, or skill-based pay — and what makes the other three a worse fit here?

Show answer
Gainsharing (in the Scanlon Plan tradition) is the correct fit: a bonus tied to measurable, controllable cost/productivity gains, built with heavy workforce participation, is exactly gainsharing’s definition. Profit sharing is a poor fit because company-wide profit across 4,000 employees would be far too diluted to reflect one plant’s scrap-reduction efforts. An ESOP is a poor fit because share price is driven by many factors unrelated to one plant’s cost performance. Skill-based pay is a poor fit because the organizational goal here is cost/waste reduction, not skill acquisition — skill-based pay would reward the wrong behaviour entirely.
Problem 3 — MPS Arithmetic

A job is rated on the Job Diagnostic Survey’s 1–7 scale as follows: skill variety = 5, task identity = 3, task significance = 7, autonomy = 4, feedback = 1. Calculate its Motivating Potential Score, showing your work, and explain in one sentence why the feedback rating matters as much as it does here.

Show answer
(Skill variety + Task identity + Task significance) / 3 = (5 + 3 + 7) / 3 = 15 / 3 = 5. MPS = 5 × Autonomy(4) × Feedback(1) = 5 × 4 × 1 = 20. Despite a strong task significance rating (7) and a respectable average across the first three characteristics, the score is dragged down to just 20 (out of a possible 343) because feedback is multiplied in at a bare 1 — since autonomy and feedback multiply rather than average into the formula, a near-zero rating on either one collapses the whole score almost regardless of how high the other four ratings are.
Problem 4 — Enrichment or Enlargement?

A retail chain announces that part-time sales associates will now also be responsible for restocking shelves and straightening clothing racks, in addition to their existing till duties — all at the same pay grade, with no change to their supervision, decision authority, or the type of customer contact they have. Management calls this “job enrichment.” Is it? Justify your answer using the Job Characteristics Model.

Show answer
No — this is job enlargement, not enrichment, and it is the textbook’s single most common reversed-definition trap. The change adds more tasks at the same level (till work, restocking, straightening are all similarly routine, low-discretion activities) without touching autonomy, task identity, task significance, or feedback — the core characteristics that would need to rise for this to count as genuine enrichment under the JCM. At best, skill variety ticks up marginally because associates now do a slightly wider range of low-skill tasks; nothing here increases the depth (control, responsibility) that defines real enrichment. Calling this “enrichment” is exactly the kind of poor-diagnosis failure mode the textbook warns about: a half-hearted change that does not meaningfully raise motivating potential.
Problem 5 — Application: A Disengaged White-Collar Workforce

A mid-sized insurance firm’s white-collar staff are visibly disengaged. Annual raises are 2 percent across the board regardless of performance rating; salaries are strictly confidential and employees are told not to discuss pay; office hours are a rigid 9-to-5 with no flexibility; and exit interviews repeatedly mention “feeling like a cog” and “no idea if hard work actually matters here.” Using merit-pay problems, the Job Characteristics Model, and flexible work arrangements, diagnose what’s going wrong and recommend a package of changes.

Show answer
Merit pay diagnosis: two of the three classic merit-pay problems are both present. Low discrimination — a flat 2 percent for everyone means the rating system, whatever it produces, has no actual bearing on pay, over-rewarding weak performers and under-rewarding strong ones. Pay secrecy — compounds the problem, since even if ratings quietly did drive some of that 2 percent, employees have no way to verify it, so (per Exhibit 6.2) they default to inventing salary estimates that further erode trust in the system. (Small increases is arguably present too, but low discrimination and secrecy are the dominant, more diagnostic signals here given the “no idea if hard work matters” comments.)

JCM diagnosis: “feeling like a cog” is a meaningfulness complaint — the language of low experienced meaningfulness, which the JCM traces back to weak skill variety, task identity, and/or task significance. Rigid, undifferentiated white-collar roles in an insurance back office are a plausible source of exactly this pattern, and the case gives no evidence of autonomy or direct feedback either, suggesting all five core characteristics may be under-realized.

Flexible work diagnosis: the rigid 9-to-5 with zero flexibility is worth naming directly — flexible work arrangements exist specifically to meet a diverse workforce’s needs and support work–life balance, and their absence here is a plausible independent contributor to disengagement and turnover risk, separate from the pay and job-design issues above.

Recommended package: (1) Widen the merit-raise band so top and average performers see a visibly different outcome, addressing low discrimination; (2) move toward a more open pay-information policy — at minimum, publish raise ranges by rating band — addressing secrecy; (3) redesign roles using enrichment principles (combine tasks so staff own a case file start to finish; establish direct client relationships; make feedback more direct through client-outcome updates), targeting task identity, significance, and feedback; (4) introduce flex-time with core hours, the lowest-friction flexible arrangement for an office environment, to address the rigid-hours complaint. No single change fixes all three diagnoses — this is a case for combining pay, job-design, and flexible-work levers together, exactly as the chapter’s “motivational practices in perspective” discussion recommends.
