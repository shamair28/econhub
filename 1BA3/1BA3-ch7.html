Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

The chapter opens at Whole Foods, the natural and organic grocery chain (now owned by Amazon) that grew from a single Austin, Texas, health food store in 1980 into a highly profitable exception in an industry known for thin margins. Most Whole Foods stores run about ten teams, organized around functions such as checkout and products such as prepared foods, meat, produce and seafood. Team leaders within a store form a team, store leaders in a region form a team, and so do regional presidents. These teams, not a hierarchy, are the core of the company, and peer pressure replaces bureaucracy as the way to ensure good performance.

The teams have unusual autonomy over ordering, spending, pricing and promotions, and they compete within and between stores. To make that autonomy work, detailed information on team and store sales, profits, salaries and bonuses is openly available across the company, information a conventional supermarket guards closely. Even hiring is team-based: every new hire, from cashier to head-office finance professional, goes through a rigorous 60-day screening that includes multiple interviews and a provisional placement on a team, and needs a two-thirds majority vote of that team to become permanent. Teams are strict about it partly because a gainsharing bonus ties their pay to team performance, and nobody wants a slacker. Teams sometimes reject the provisional placements leaders make, which reinforces ownership and fosters cohesion.

Almost every idea in this chapter is visible in that story: groups and how they develop (Sections 2–3), the structure that shapes them, from size to norms, roles and status (Sections 4–5), what makes a group attractive to its members (Section 6, cohesiveness), the temptation to let others carry the load (Section 7, social loafing, which is exactly what Whole Foods’ vote and gainsharing guard against), and the design of effective teams, including self-managed, cross-functional and virtual teams (Sections 8–11).

What Is a Group? (7.1)

For behavioural scientists, a group consists of two or more people interacting interdependently to achieve a common goal. Each part of the definition does work:

  • Interaction is the most basic aspect: it tells you who is in the group and who is not. It need not be face to face or even verbal. Telecommuters can belong to their office work group while communicating by email or text from kilometres away.
  • Interdependence means members rely to some degree on each other to accomplish goals.
  • A common goal: all groups have one or more, ranging from having fun to marketing a new product to achieving world peace.

Group memberships matter for two reasons. Groups exert tremendous influence on us: they are the social mechanisms through which we acquire many beliefs, values, attitudes and behaviours. And groups give us a context in which to exert influence on others.

Formal and Informal Groups

Formal work groups
Groups established by organizations to facilitate the achievement of organizational goals. They are intentionally designed to channel individual effort. The most common is a manager plus the employees who report to that manager (a production manager and six shift supervisors; each supervisor plus their own subordinates), so an organization’s hierarchy is a series of formal, interlocked work groups. Other formal groups: task forces and project teams (temporary groups that meet to achieve a particular goal or solve a particular problem, such as suggesting productivity improvements, or designing products through interdisciplinary project teams) and committees (usually permanent groups that handle recurrent assignments outside the usual work group structure, such as a standing committee on work–family balance).
Informal groups
Groups that emerge naturally in response to the common interests of organizational members. They are seldom sanctioned by the organization, their membership often cuts across formal groups, and they can either help or hurt the organization, depending on their norms for behaviour.

Dr. Kassaye’s slide 3 gives a pair that makes the contrast easy to remember: working on a project together (formal) versus playing on the company softball team (informal).

Vocabulary Trap — “Temporary” Does Not Mean “Informal” A task force or project team is temporary but still formal: management set it up to achieve an organizational goal. A committee is formal and usually permanent. What makes a group informal is that it emerges naturally from members’ common interests and is seldom sanctioned. Test question to ask yourself: who created the group, and why?

Group Development: Five Stages and Punctuated Equilibrium (7.1)

Even simple groups are complex social devices that need negotiation and trial and error before members function as a true group. Knowing each other beforehand does not replace the need for development. The text gives two models: a general stage model, and a different sequence that appears when a group works to a deadline.

Typical Stages of Group Development

Leaders and trainers have observed that many groups develop through a series of stages over time. Each stage presents members with challenges they must master to reach the next, and groups vary in how quickly they move through them (slide 4).

  1. Forming. Members orient themselves by “testing the waters.” What are we doing here? What are the others like? What is our purpose? The situation is often ambiguous, and members are aware of their dependency on each other.
  2. Storming. Conflict often emerges. Confrontation and criticism occur as members decide whether to go along with the way the group is developing. Sorting out roles and responsibilities is often at issue. Problems are more likely to happen earlier rather than later in development.
  3. Norming. Members resolve the issues that provoked the storming and develop social consensus; compromise is often necessary. Interdependence is recognized, norms are agreed to, the group becomes more cohesive, and information and opinions flow freely.
  4. Performing. With its social structure sorted out, the group devotes its energies to task accomplishment. Achievement, creativity and mutual assistance are prominent themes.
  5. Adjourning. Groups with a definite lifespan (task forces, design project teams), and groups broken up by layoffs and downsizing, disperse. Rites and rituals affirming the group’s success are common (ceremonies, parties), and members often show emotional support for each other.
Memory Aid — Form, Storm, Norm, Perform, Adjourn: Follow the Question The rhyme gives you the order; the trick is to attach the question the group is asking at each stage: Who are we? (forming) → Who’s in charge of what? (storming: roles and responsibilities) → What are our rules? (norming: norms and cohesion) → Let’s get it done (performing) → Goodbye (adjourning). Trap: exam stems describe behaviour, not stage names. “Members argue over who should lead” is storming, not forming; “members start helping each other and the work takes off” is performing, not norming. Self-test: write one sentence of dialogue for each stage from a group you have been in.

Practical Learnings from the Stage Model

The stage model is a good tool for monitoring and troubleshooting how groups are developing, and slide 6 adds that it is especially helpful for new groups trying to understand the process of developing together. But not every group goes through the stages:

  • The process applies mainly to new groups that have never met before.
  • Well-acquainted task forces and committees can short-circuit the stages when they have a new problem to work out.
  • Some settings are so highly structured that storming and norming are unnecessary even for strangers. Commercial airline cockpit crews perform effectively even though they can be strangers who meet just before takeoff.

The Punctuated Equilibrium Model

When a group has a specific deadline for a problem-solving task, researcher Connie Gersick found a very different sequence, which she called the punctuated equilibrium model: a model of group development that describes how groups with deadlines are affected by their first meetings and crucial midpoint transitions. Equilibrium means stability: the research showed long stretches of stability punctuated by a critical first meeting, a midpoint change in activity, and a rush to finish.

  • Phase 1 runs from the first meeting to the midpoint of the group’s existence. The first meeting is critical: the assumptions, approaches and precedents set there dominate the first half of the group’s life. The group gathers information and holds meetings, but makes little visible progress toward the goal.
  • The midpoint transition occurs at almost exactly the halfway point in time toward the deadline (a two-month deadline gives a transition at about one month). The need to move forward is apparent, and the group may seek outside advice. It may consolidate earlier information or mark a completely new approach, but it crystallizes the group’s activities for Phase 2. How the group manages it is critical for progress.
  • Phase 2: for better or worse, the decisions and approaches adopted at the midpoint are played out. It ends with a final meeting showing a burst of activity and concern for how outsiders will evaluate the product.

The model applies to groups with deadlines, and such groups may also show some of the stages above, with a new cycle of storming and norming after the midpoint transition. Advice the model gives for project teams, advertising groups or class project groups (the same five points appear on slide 8):

  1. Prepare carefully for the first meeting. What is decided there strongly determines the rest of Phase 1. A coach or adviser should stress motivation and excitement about the project.
  2. As long as people are working, do not look for radical progress during Phase 1.
  3. Manage the midpoint transition carefully. Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of Phase 1 ideas, clarify questions with whoever is commissioning the work, and recognize that a fundamental change in approach must occur here for progress; essential issues are unlikely to “work themselves out” in Phase 2. A coach should focus on the strategy for Phase 2.
  4. Be sure adequate resources are available to execute the Phase 2 plan.
  5. Resist deadline changes. They could damage the midpoint transition.
Memory Aid — Walk the Timeline: First Meeting, Patience, Midpoint, Resources, Deadline The five tips are in time order, so you can rebuild them by walking left to right along Figure 2: First meeting (prepare it) · Phase 1 (patience: no radical progress expected) · Midpoint (manage the transition, change approach) · Phase 2 (resources ready) · Deadline (don’t move it). Trap: punctuated equilibrium does not replace the five stages for every group; it describes groups with a deadline, and those groups may still storm and norm again after the midpoint. Self-test: for a 12-week course project, say in which week each tip applies.
Vocabulary Trap — Which Model Fits the Question? If the stem describes a group’s social development (getting to know each other, arguing over roles, agreeing on rules), think five stages. If it mentions a deadline, a first meeting that “set the tone,” months of little visible progress, or a sudden change of approach halfway through, think punctuated equilibrium (Gersick). The midpoint is about time (halfway to the deadline), not about how much work is done.

Group Size, Task Type and Diversity (7.2)

Group structure refers to the characteristics of the stable social organization of a group: the way a group is “put together.” The most basic structural characteristics are size and member diversity. The others are the expectations members have about each other’s behaviour (norms), agreements about “who does what” (roles), the rewards and prestige given to members (status) and how attractive the group is to its members (cohesiveness). This section covers size and diversity; Section 5 covers norms, roles and status, and Section 6 covers cohesiveness.

Group Size

The smallest group is two people (a manager and one employee). The upper limit is debatable, but given the definition of a group, a parliament of 300–400 members is somewhere near it. In practice, most work groups, task forces and committees have 3 to 20 members. Slide 9 asks the obvious question, “What is the ultimate group size?”, and the answer is that it depends on whether you care about satisfaction or performance, and on the task.

Size and satisfaction. Members of larger groups rather consistently report less satisfaction. The text gives five reasons: (1) as opportunities for friendship increase, the time and energy to develop them may not; (2) more members with different viewpoints can prompt conflict and dissension; (3) the time available for each member to speak shrinks; (4) many people are inhibited about participating in larger groups; and (5) members identify less easily with the group’s success (a member of a 4-person cancer research team can see their contribution to a breakthrough more easily than a member of a 20-person team).

Size and performance. Whether bigger is better depends on the exact task and how performance is defined. The text distinguishes three kinds of task:

AdditiveThe sum of the performance of individual membersBuilding a house (add up the carpenters’ efforts)Tug-of-war; your group assignment; charity fundraisingPotential performance rises

Notice the word potential. As groups get bigger they suffer from process losses: performance difficulties that stem from the problems of motivating and coordinating larger groups (slide 10 uses the same definition). Even with good intentions, communication and decision-making problems grow with size: imagine 50 carpenters trying to build one house. So:

Actual performance = Potential performance − Process losses

Exhibit 7.3 (Figure 4) shows the consequences for additive and disjunctive tasks: (a) potential performance and process losses both rise with size; (b) the net effect is that actual performance rises with size up to a point and then falls off; and (c) the average performance per member falls as size grows. Up to a point, larger groups may perform better as groups, but their individual members become less efficient. For purely conjunctive tasks, performance should fall as size increases, by any standard.

Memory Aid — Add It Up, “OR,” “AND” Additive = add everyone up. Disjunctive works like a logical OR: the group succeeds if A or B or C solves it, so one star is enough. Conjunctive works like a logical AND: A and B and C must all come through, so the chain is only as strong as its weakest link (conjunctive = chain). Size rule that follows: more members means more effort (additive) and a better chance of a star (disjunctive), but a better chance of a weak link (conjunctive). Self-test: classify a debate team where the best speaker answers the judges, a bucket brigade, and a food drive.
Vocabulary Trap — Disjunctive vs. Conjunctive, and Lecture vs. Textbook Examples The two words look alike but mean opposite things: disjunctive = the best member decides; conjunctive = the poorest member limits. Also note that slide 9 calls a relay race conjunctive (one slow runner sinks the team) and brainstorming disjunctive (one great idea is enough). The textbook’s examples are building a house, finding a single program error, and an assembly line or team teaching. On a textbook-based multiple-choice question, reason from the definition rather than memorizing the examples. And remember: process losses are the reason that actual performance falls short of potential, not a fourth task type.

Diversity of Group Membership

Compare an 8-member product development task force made up entirely of 30-something white men of Western European heritage with one that mixes gender identities, eight different ethnic or racial backgrounds and ages from 20 to 60. The first is homogeneous, the second heterogeneous (diverse). What the research says:

  • More diverse groups have a harder time communicating effectively and becoming cohesive, so they may take longer to do their forming, storming and norming (slide 11 makes the same point).
  • Once they develop, more and less diverse groups can be equally cohesive and productive.
  • Diverse groups sometimes perform better on certain tasks: diversity in educational background and functional specialty (marketing versus product design, for example) enhances creativity and innovation because a wider variety of ideas is considered. Slide 11 puts it as performing better on cognitive, creativity-demanding and problem-solving tasks rather than routine work.
  • Negative effects of surface diversity (age, gender, race) are generally small or wear off over time: age diversity is unrelated to group performance, while racial and gender diversity have small negative effects. Deep diversity (in attitudes toward work or how to accomplish a goal) can badly damage cohesiveness.
  • When groups and organizations value and manage diversity (Chapter 3), they offset some of the initial process-loss costs and capitalize on the benefits of a positive diversity climate. One way is training on working in diverse settings, which works best when integrated with other efforts to foster diversity.
Vocabulary Trap — Surface vs. Deep Diversity Surface diversity is what you can see or record on a form (age, gender, race); its effects are small or fade. Deep diversity is about attitudes and approaches to the work, and it is the kind that “can badly damage cohesiveness.” An answer claiming that diverse groups are “always less productive” is wrong: the text says diverse groups develop more slowly but can end up equally cohesive and productive, and do better on creative work.

Norms, Roles and Status (7.2)

Norms

Social norms are collective expectations that members of social units have regarding the behaviour of each other. They are codes of conduct that specify what people ought and ought not to do, and standards against which we judge whether behaviour is appropriate. Changing norms is one way to change behaviour: Google’s major research effort, Project Aristotle, concluded that developing effective group norms was the key to improving teamwork in the company.

Much normative influence is unconscious. We notice it mainly in special circumstances: watching children struggle to master adult norms, watching international visitors spar with our culture’s norms, meeting norms that conflict (“Get ahead” but “Don’t step on others”), or entering a new situation (“Can I tell the boss he’s wrong?” “Can I personalize my workspace?” on the first day of a job).

How norms develop, in three questions:

  • Why? The most important function of norms is to provide regularity and predictability to behaviour, which gives psychological security and lets us carry out daily business with minimal disruption.
  • About what? Behaviours that are at least marginally important to their supporters. Managers are more likely to adopt norms about performance and attendance than about office decoration, and less deviation is accepted from norms about more important behaviours.
  • How? Through shared attitudes (Chapter 4): when members share related beliefs and values, they share the resulting attitudes, and those shared attitudes form the basis for norms. So there is no such thing as “my personal norm”: norms are collectively held and need two or more people to exist.

Why people comply. Much compliance happens because the norm matches privately held attitudes. Even trivial norms (when to shake hands) save time and prevent social confusion. Most interesting is compliance with norms that go against private attitudes: couples without religious convictions marrying in religious services, necktie-haters wearing ties to work. Groups have an extraordinary range of rewards and punishments to induce conformity.

Some Typical Norms

  • Dress norms. Social norms frequently dictate clothing at work: polished buttons and razor-sharp creases in military and quasi-military organizations; even with casual dress policies, people worry about what they wear at work or on a Zoom call.
  • Reward allocation norms. At least four norms can dictate how pay, promotions and informal favours are allocated: equity (reward according to inputs such as effort, performance or seniority), equality (reward everyone equally), reciprocity (reward people the way they reward you) and social responsibility (reward those who truly need it). Most Western organizations stress a combination of equity and equality: give employees what they deserve, and no favouritism.
  • Performance norms. Performance may be as much a function of social expectations as of ability, motivation or technology. New members watch for cues (“Is it all right to take a break now?”). Official norms usually favour high performance, but work groups often set their own informal norms, such as restricting productivity under a piece-rate pay system. Groups that set specific, challenging goals perform at a high level.

(Lecture) Slide 12 lists the same three classes and adds a fourth heading, social interaction norms (how members are expected to interact with each other), without further detail. It is not one of the textbook’s named classes.

Memory Aid — Reward Allocation: Deserve, Same, Swap, Need Equity = what you deserve (inputs) · Equality = everyone the same · Reciprocity = a swap (I reward you as you reward me) · Social responsibility = by need. Western default: deserve + same (equity and equality). Trap: equity here is the Chapter 5 idea of inputs and outcomes, not “equal shares”; a manager who gives the biggest bonus to the hardest worker is applying equity, and one who splits it evenly is applying equality. Self-test: label the norm behind “she covered my shift, so I’ll back her for the award.”

Roles

Roles are positions in a group that have a set of expected behaviours attached to them. They are “packages” of norms that apply to particular members: many norms make all members behave similarly, while roles signal that members may have to act differently from one another.

  • Designated or assigned roles are formally prescribed by the organization to divide labour and responsibility: they say “who does what” and “who can tell others what to do” (president, software engineer, analyst, sales manager).
  • Emergent roles develop naturally to meet members’ social-emotional needs or to help with formal job accomplishment: the class clown and the office gossip (social-emotional), an “old pro” who helps newcomers learn the job, informal leaders, and scapegoats who become targets of group hostility.

Slide 13 lists the key issues with roles as role ambiguity, role conflict and status effects, and asks about their consequences.

Role Ambiguity

Role ambiguity is a lack of clarity of job goals or methods: confusion about how performance is evaluated, how good performance can be achieved, or what the limits of one’s authority and responsibility are. The text’s model of the role assumption process (Exhibit 7.4) runs: organizational factors → role senders (such as managers) develop role expectations → they send the role → the focal person receives it → role behaviour. Ambiguity can enter at three points:

  • Organizational factors. Some roles are inherently ambiguous because of their function: middle management roles may lack the “big picture” of upper management and do not require the attention to supervision of lower management.
  • The role sender. Senders may have unclear expectations, or send clear ones badly: a weak orientation session, vague performance reviews, inconsistent feedback and discipline.
  • The focal person. Even clear, well-sent expectations may not be fully digested, especially by people new to the role. Ambiguity tends to decrease with time in the role.

Consequences: the most frequent are job stress, dissatisfaction, reduced organizational commitment, lowered performance and intentions to quit. Managers can reduce unnecessary ambiguity by providing clear performance expectations and performance feedback, especially for new employees and people in intrinsically ambiguous jobs.

Role Conflict

Role conflict is the condition of being faced with incompatible role expectations. It differs from ambiguity: the expectations may be crystal clear but mutually exclusive, impossible to meet at the same time, or unsuited to the person. There are four forms:

  • Intrasender role conflict: a single role sender gives incompatible expectations (a manager says “take it easy, don’t work so hard” while delivering another batch of reports needing immediate attention). Especially likely to provoke ambiguity too.
  • Intersender role conflict: two or more role senders differ in their expectations. Boundary spanners between the organization and its clients are especially prone, but it also arises inside: a first-level manager is pressured from above to get the work out and keep the troops in line, and encouraged from below to be considerate and friendly.
  • Interrole conflict: several roles held by one person involve incompatible expectations (a marketing expert who heads the market research group, reports to the VP of marketing and sits on a product development task force; or the clash between work and family roles). Competing demands for time are a frequent symptom.
  • Person–role conflict: role demands call for behaviour incompatible with the personality or skills of the role occupant. Many cases of whistle-blowing signal person–role conflict: the organization demands behaviour the occupant considers unethical.

As with ambiguity, the most consistent consequences of role conflict are job dissatisfaction, stress reactions, lowered organizational commitment and turnover intentions. Managers can help prevent it by avoiding self-contradictory messages, conferring with other role senders, being sensitive to multiple role demands, and fitting the right person to the right role: one prevention for each of the four forms, in the same order. Ambiguity and conflict are not always bad: a film-industry study found that assistant first directors, who report to both the producer and the director, used the ambiguity and conflict to craft their jobs toward their artistic goals.

Memory Aid — Role Conflict Counts: 1 Sender, 2 Senders, 2 Roles, You vs. the Role Intrasender = one sender contradicts themself · Intersender = two or more senders disagree · Interrole = two or more of your roles clash · Person–role = the role clashes with you (personality, skills, ethics). The four manager fixes come in the same order: don’t contradict yourself · talk to the other senders · respect multiple roles · fit the person to the role. Self-test: classify “my two bosses want opposite things” and “my job asks me to mislead customers.”
Vocabulary Trap — Ambiguity vs. Conflict; Intra vs. Inter Role ambiguity = expectations are unclear (“I don’t know what I’m supposed to do or how I’m judged”). Role conflict = expectations are clear but incompatible (“I know exactly what they want, and I can’t do both”). Within conflict, intra means within one sender; inter means between senders (intersender) or between roles (interrole). And a role (a position with expected behaviours) is not a norm (an expectation that usually applies to everyone): roles are packages of norms for particular members.

Status

Status is the rank or social position accorded to group members in terms of prominence, prestige and respect: the group’s evaluation of a member. When a status system works smoothly, the group has clear norms about who gets higher or lower status.

  • Formal status systems are management’s attempt to publicly identify who has higher status, using status symbols: tangible indicators such as titles, particular working relationships, pay packages, work schedules and the physical work environment. Criteria include seniority (longer-serving members may choose the day shift or a better office) and, more important, one’s assigned role (labourers, managers and executives do different jobs and acquire different statuses). Why bother? Status symbols are powerful magnets that induce people to aspire to higher positions, and status differentiation reinforces the authority hierarchy, since people pay attention to high-status individuals.
  • Informal status systems are less advertised but can operate just as effectively. Job performance is often a basis (the “power hitters” on a baseball team, the “cool heads” in an emergency unit, early-career “fast trackers”), but informal status is just as often linked to factors such as gender or race: the man who takes a day off for a sick child is praised as a model father, while the woman who does the same may have her commitment questioned. The text’s Research Focus describes a rare rapid status rise for non-physician healthcare workers early in the COVID-19 pandemic; most welcomed it at first, then insisted they were not heroes, and almost all expected it to be temporary.

Consequences of status differences. Status has a paradoxical effect on communication: most people prefer to communicate with others at their own status or higher, so communication should move up the hierarchy; but if status differences are large, people are inhibited from communicating upward, so much communication gets stalled. Status also affects the amount of communication and influence: higher-status members talk more and have more influence. Jury studies show jurors with higher social status (managers, professionals) participate more and have more effect on the verdict, and there is no guarantee the highest-status person is the most knowledgeable.

Reducing status barriers. Because status differences inhibit communication, many organizations downplay them by removing questionable status symbols, to foster teamwork across ranks: Silicon Valley’s egalitarian culture; old-line industries dropping reserved parking and fancy executive offices; Vancity, where all newcomers (tellers, middle managers or board members) attend the same week-long orientation. Some methods are phoney: a “casual Friday” with no other cultural change only underlines status differences the rest of the week. Email and texting have levelled status barriers by giving lower-status people direct access to VIPs, even in the rank-conscious military.

Vocabulary Trap — Role vs. Status A role is what you are expected to do (a position with expected behaviours). Status is how highly the group ranks you (prominence, prestige, respect). Your assigned role is the most important criterion for formal status, but they are different concepts. Status symbols are tools of the formal system; informal status can come from performance, or unfairly from gender or race.

Group Cohesiveness (7.3)

Group cohesiveness is the degree to which a group is especially attractive to its members (slide 14). Because the group is attractive, members especially want to stay in it and describe it in favourable terms. The stereotype is the September baseball team headed for the World Series: precision teamwork on the field, sweetness and joviality in the clubhouse. Cohesiveness is a relative property: there is no objective line between cohesive and non-cohesive groups, so “cohesive” means more attractive to its members than average.

Consequences of Cohesiveness

Whether more cohesiveness is desirable depends on its consequences and on who is judging. Slide 15 lists the same three as the text:

  • More participation in group activities. Members want to stay, so voluntary turnover is low; they like each other, so absence is lower; and there is a high degree of (often friendlier, more supportive) communication as members cooperate and help each other.
  • More conformity. Cohesive groups are well equipped to supply information, rewards and punishment, so they induce conformity. They react to deviants by increasing communication directed at them (information to help them “see the light,” plus veiled threats). If that fails, communication decreases: the group has isolated the deviant to keep the majority cohesive.
  • More success. Success contributes to cohesiveness, and cohesiveness contributes to success: research links cohesiveness to performance. The relationship is reciprocal. Cohesive groups achieve goals because participation, communication and conformity produce agreement on goals and methods, and coordinated effort pays off.

But cohesive groups are especially effective at accomplishing their own goals. If those match the organization’s, cohesion helps; if not, organizational effectiveness may be threatened. One large study of work groups concluded (Exhibit 7.6, Figure 7):

  • In highly cohesive groups, members’ productivity tends to be similar; in less cohesive groups it varies more (slide 15: “in more cohesive groups, individual performance is similar”).
  • Highly cohesive groups can be more or less productive than less cohesive groups, depending on other variables. If they accept the organization’s productivity norms they should be highly productive; if they reject them they are especially effective at limiting productivity.

Cohesiveness also pays off more when the task truly requires interdependence and cooperation (a football team versus a golf team). In summary: in a good labour relations climate, cohesiveness on interdependent tasks should contribute to high productivity; in a climate of tension and disagreement, cohesive groups may pursue goals that lower it.

Vocabulary Trap — “Cohesive” Does Not Mean “Productive” The safe exam statement is that cohesive groups are effective at achieving their own goals and make members’ productivity similar. Whether that productivity is high or low depends on whether the group accepts the organization’s norms. An option saying “increasing cohesiveness always raises productivity” is wrong. Cohesiveness reappears in Chapter 11: Janis saw high cohesiveness as the root of groupthink, though the text now says other factors matter more (Section 12).

Social Loafing (7.4)

Social loafing is the tendency to withhold physical or intellectual effort when performing a group task: people would work harder alone than as part of the group (slide 18: members exert less effort in teams than when working alone). Of the two sources of process loss, coordination and motivation, social loafing is a motivation problem. People in groups face a social dilemma: slacking off might benefit the individual, but if everyone does it, group performance is poor. Social loafers resolve the dilemma in a way that hurts organizational goals.

Free rider effect
People lower their effort to get a free ride at the expense of fellow group members.
Sucker effect
People lower their effort because they feel others are free riding; they are trying to restore equity in the group. Picture the free riders slacking first and the “suckers” following suit: group performance suffers badly.

How to counteract social loafing (the same five on slide 18):

  1. Make individual performance more visible. The simplest way, where appropriate, is to keep the group small so contributions are not hidden; posting performance levels and having people present their accomplishments also help.
  2. Make sure the work is interesting. If the work is involving, intrinsic motivation should counteract loafing.
  3. Increase feelings of indispensability. Members slack when they feel their inputs are unnecessary. Use training and the status system to give members unique inputs (one person masters the computer graphics programs).
  4. Increase performance feedback. Some loafing happens because groups or members do not know how they are doing; feedback from the boss, peers and customers encourages self-correction.
  5. Reward group performance. Members are more likely to monitor and maximize their own performance, and attend to colleagues’, when the group is rewarded for effectiveness.
Memory Aid — See It, Like It, Need Me, Know It, Share It See it: make individual performance visible · Like it: make the work interesting · Need me: increase indispensability · Know it: increase performance feedback · Share it: reward group performance. Read it from the loafer’s point of view: “people can see what I do, I like the work, they need me, I know how I’m doing, and we share the prize.” Trap: “make the group bigger” or “reward only individuals” are distractors; the text says keep groups small to raise visibility, and reward the group. Self-test: apply all five to your next group assignment.
Vocabulary Trap — Free Rider vs. Sucker; Loafing vs. Process Loss The free rider slacks to gain (a free ride on others’ work). The sucker slacks to avoid being exploited (restoring equity because they believe others are free riding), so the sucker effect is a reaction to the free rider effect. And process losses are the broader category (coordination and motivation problems of larger groups); social loafing is the motivational part.

What Makes a Work Group Effective?

A good model for an effective work group is a successful sports team: a small group of highly skilled people who meld their skills into a cohesive effort; an intrinsically motivating task with very direct feedback; status differences (if any) based on contribution to the team; an obsessive concern with getting the right personnel (tryouts, drafts); and a team that is coached, not supervised.

According to J. Richard Hackman, a work group is effective when:

  1. its output is acceptable to management and to users in other parts of the organization;
  2. group members’ needs are satisfied rather than frustrated by the group; and
  3. the group experience enables members to continue to work together.

Group effectiveness occurs when high effort is directed toward the task, great knowledge and skill are directed toward it, and the group adopts sensible strategies for accomplishing its goals. These are the “group processes” in Exhibit 7.7 (Figure 9, Section 9).

Memory Aid — Hackman’s Three Criteria: Good Output, Happy Members, Still a Team Output acceptable to management and users · members’ needs met · continuity: the group can keep working together. Trap: effectiveness is not just output. A team that hits its target but burns out its members, or can never work together again, fails two of Hackman’s three tests. Self-test: judge a group project that got an A but whose members now refuse to work together.

Basic Qualities of Effective Work Teams

Six qualities benefit all types of work teams. They both contribute to and result from team cohesion:

  • Psychological safety: a shared belief that it is safe to take social risks, such as questioning the status quo, suggesting a new idea or disagreeing with a colleague. It promotes trust, which is critical for functioning and performance, and members who feel safe learn from mistakes rather than becoming defensive. (You met this concept in Chapter 10 as the climate that makes voice possible.)
  • Team reflexivity: the extent to which teams deliberately discuss team processes and goals and adapt their behaviour accordingly. Conscious reflection improves coordination and avoids problems of unclear communication; simply assuming everyone agrees is not a recipe for effectiveness.
  • Shared mental models: team members share similar information about how they should interact and what their task is. They keep members “on the same page” and contribute greatly to performance, at least when the shared knowledge reflects reality.
  • Capacity to improvise: sticking to an agreed course is usually positive, but not everything goes as planned, so flexibility and adaptability are essential for long-run success.
  • Collective efficacy: shared beliefs that a team can successfully perform a given task (the team version of Chapter 2’s self-efficacy). Self-efficacy does not automatically translate into collective efficacy: five skilled musicians do not necessarily make a good band. The four qualities above contribute to it.
  • Team resilience: a team’s capacity to bounce back from setbacks or adversity. Sometimes that means persisting toward established goals; sometimes it means adapting the strategy or revising goals. Individual resilience does not automatically make a resilient team: team resilience emerges from positive interaction patterns, and the five qualities above are resources for it.
Memory Aid — Safe, Reflect, Share, Improvise → Believe → Bounce Back The text lists the qualities in build-up order: four foundations (safe to speak, reflect on how we work, share the same mental model, improvise when plans fail), which together let the team believe it can succeed (collective efficacy), which with the other four lets it bounce back (resilience). Trap: team reflexivity (deliberately discussing and adapting how the team works) is not shared mental models (having the same picture of the task and how to interact); and collective efficacy is a shared team belief, not the average of members’ self-efficacy. Self-test: give one behaviour you would see in a team for each of the six.

Self-Managed Work Teams (7.6)

Self-managed work teams are work groups that have the opportunity to do challenging work under reduced supervision. Other labels are autonomous, semi-autonomous and self-directed; slide 21 describes them as teams with full, reduced or no supervision. The general idea is that the group regulates much of its own members’ behaviour. Success depends on three things, which slide 22 lists with exactly the same sub-points as the text: the task, the composition of the group, and the support in place.

Tasks for Self-Managed Teams

Tasks should be complex and challenging and require high interdependence. They should have the qualities of enriched jobs (Chapter 6): the team should see the task as significant, do it from beginning to end, and use a variety of skills. A self-managed team needs something useful to self-manage. Putting olive stuffers on a food-processing line in distinctive jumpsuits, calling them the “Olive Squad” and telling them to self-manage will not work: the task is still boring, “a prime recipe for social loafing.”

Beyond complexity, the range of tasks is wide, in white-collar (complex service and design jobs) and blue-collar settings (General Mills uses self-managed work groups extensively). The text’s example is a new U.K. confectionery plant, where groups of 8 to 12 were all expected to do each of eight production jobs and were collectively responsible for allocating jobs, meeting production, quality and hygiene targets, solving local problems, recording data, organizing breaks, ordering materials, calling for engineering support, training new recruits and helping select new employees, with the key decisions made at weekly group meetings. The theme: the breakdown of traditional, specialized roles. Members adopt whatever roles make the group effective.

Composition of Self-Managed Teams: “Stable, Small and Smart”

  • Stability. Self-managed teams need a lot of interaction and high cohesiveness, which require understanding and trust, so membership must be fairly stable. Rotating members in and out prevents a true group identity, and teams with higher average tenure tend to perform better.
  • Size. As small as is feasible without the stress of understaffing, to keep coordination problems and social loafing to a minimum. These are harder for self-managed teams because there is no manager to coordinate or to search out loafers. Amazon’s rule: a two-pizza team, no larger than two pizzas can feed.
  • Expertise. Members should have high expertise about the task; not everyone needs to know everything, but the group as a whole must. All members need some social skills (talking things out, communicating, resolving conflict), and high cognitive ability helps.
  • Diversity. Members should be similar enough to work well together and diverse enough to bring a variety of perspectives and skills. Diverse goals threaten self-management, because no manager is present to align them.

One way to maintain composition is to let the team choose its own members, as at Whole Foods, or at Pret a Manger, where job seekers work in a shop for a day and the staff vote on whether they join. The risk is that a group uses irrelevant criteria such as race or gender to exclude people, so HR oversight and very clear selection criteria (behaviours, skills, credentials) are necessary. Selection is critical because some studies find elevated turnover in self-managed teams, and turnover reduces a team’s capacity to learn and adapt.

Supporting Self-Managed Teams

  • Training, almost always extensive: technical training (maths, computers, tasks a supervisor used to handle; cross-training in teammates’ specialties), social skills (assertiveness, problem solving, routine dispute resolution), language skills (for linguistically diverse teams), and business training (basic finance, accounting and production, so members see how their work fits the bigger picture).
  • Rewards. Tie rewards mostly to team accomplishment, while still giving members some individual performance feedback to counteract social loafing. Microsoft’s European product support group switched to team rewards when individual rewards discouraged engineers from taking difficult cases. Gainsharing, profit sharing and skill-based pay (Chapter 6) fit; skill-based pay is especially attractive because it rewards learning multiple skills.
  • Management. Self-management is poorly supported when managers feel threatened by it (less power, fewer promotions), and some traditional managers never adapt. Those who do can mediate relations between teams and handle union concerns about cross-functional job sharing. The most effective managers encourage groups to observe, evaluate and reinforce their own behaviour: coaching teams to be independent.

Research strongly supports many of these relationships and shows improvements in team productivity, quality, customer satisfaction and safety after self-managed work teams are introduced. On size, the text’s Research Focus adds a twist from 95 million papers, patents and software products over 60 years: smaller teams were more likely to produce creative, “disruptive” work, while larger teams were more likely to develop existing ideas. Both kinds of outcome are needed for progress.

Cross-Functional Teams (7.7)

Cross-functional teams are work groups that bring people with different functional specialties together to better invent, design or deliver a product or service. If the task is recurrent and not too complex, a cross-functional team might be self-managed and permanent. If it is complex and unique (designing a car or a mobile app), it needs formal leadership and usually lasts only as long as the project. Either way, “cross-functional” means the diverse specialties are necessary and cross-training is not feasible: people must be experts in their own area but able to cooperate. They are used in service industries (banking, hospitals) and are best known for product development: Rubbermaid’s household products; pharmaceutical teams of toxicologists, biologists, clinicians, chemists and marketers.

The goals are some combination of innovation, speed and quality from early coordination among specialties. Compare the traditional, sequential way of designing cars: stylists designed the look, then engineering produced specifications, then manufacturing worked out how to build it, and somewhere down the line marketing and accounting had their say. Each link misread the one before, or resisted its ideas because they were “not invented here,” producing slow, expensive development and early quality problems. The cross-functional approach gets all the specialties working together from day one, and auto companies cut years off new-model development. A complex project can run more than 30 cross-functional teams at once, forming multi-team systems, which place a premium on a strong overarching goal and on leaders who span the boundaries between teams.

Principles for Effectiveness

The text illustrates six principles (the same six on slide 23) with a past redesign of the Ford Mustang:

  1. Composition. All relevant specialties are present and nobody is overlooked. Auto companies put labour representatives on design teams to warn of assembly problems; the Mustang project included outside suppliers; some companies include clients.
  2. Superordinate goals. Attractive outcomes that can be achieved only by collaboration. They override detailed functional objectives that might conflict (finance versus design). Mustang’s was keeping the legendary name alive in the face of corporate cost cutting.
  3. Physical proximity. Members are located, or relocated, close together to make informal contact easy. The Mustang teams were housed in a former furniture warehouse in Allen Park, Michigan.
  4. Autonomy. Some autonomy from the larger organization, and authority for specialists to commit their function to project decisions, to prevent meddling or “micromanaging” by upper-level or functional managers.
  5. Rules and procedures. Petty rules are avoided, but basic decision procedures prevent anarchy. On the Mustang project a single manufacturing person had a veto over radical body changes.
  6. Leadership. Because of the potential for conflict, leaders need especially strong people skills as well as task expertise. The “tough engineer” who led the Mustang project succeeded by developing his people skills.

Several of these principles help members build shared mental models, which are a particular challenge in cross-functional teams because members’ backgrounds differ. The text’s “tough truck” example: told to design a tough truck, a designer reads tough as “powerful looking” (big grille, big tires), while an engineer reads it as durable and objects that big tires compromise power and hauling capacity; the designer then rejects the engineer’s smaller wheels as “cartoonish.” Same word, different mental models.

Vocabulary Trap — The Three Team Types Overlap The labels describe different things: self-managed is about supervision (reduced), cross-functional is about membership (different specialties), and virtual is about location and technology (dispersed members). One team can be all three: a cross-functional team doing a recurrent task can be self-managed, and the text notes virtual teams are often cross-functional. A key contrast: self-managed teams favour cross-training and broken-down roles, while cross-functional teams exist because cross-training is not feasible.

Virtual Teams (7.8)

Virtual teams are work groups that use technology to communicate and collaborate across time, space and organizational boundaries. Beyond technology, their primary feature is a lack of face-to-face contact due to geographic dispersion, often across countries and cultures, and they are often cross-functional. Their technologies can be asynchronous (email, voice mail: time to reflect before responding) or synchronous (phone, instant messaging, chat, electronic conferencing: dynamic, real-time). One study found monolingual teams preferred real-time media, while multilingual teams favoured email, which let members study messages and rehearse replies, and used redundancy (email, then a phone call to check understanding); both liked virtual chatrooms, which combine speed with a searchable written record. The lesson: virtual practices must be fine-tuned to the team’s context.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced countless employees to virtual work, and even with familiar colleagues many reported “Zoom fatigue” and miscommunication, so managers should not treat virtual teams as regular teams that happen to use technology. Paradoxically, their performance depends especially on also having a high volume of open communication, because openness builds the cooperation, cohesion and trust that virtuality makes hard. The key is to recognize how virtual teams differ without focusing solely on technology: many recommendations for any team still apply, and virtual teams must be genuine teams “if not by location, then in mind and spirit.”

The text’s You Be the Manager case is Orange, France Télécom’s mobile operator (57 million customers in 17 countries), whose global product-development virtual teams suffered low trust: British attempts at humour on conference calls left Paris colleagues feeling isolated. Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Which Stage, Which Model?

(a) Name the development stage shown by each snapshot of a new hospital process-improvement group: (i) “Members argue over whether the nurse or the pharmacist should lead the data collection”; (ii) “At the first meeting people ask what exactly the hospital wants and politely size each other up”; (iii) “The group agrees to meet every Tuesday, share notes in one folder and settle disagreements by vote”; (iv) “The group holds a lunch to celebrate after presenting its report.” (b) A different team has ten weeks to deliver a proposal. Weeks 1–5 produce little visible progress; in week 5 the team scraps its plan after a meeting with the client. Which model fits, and what should its leader have done in week 1 and week 5?

Show answer
(a) (i) Storming: conflict, sorting out roles and responsibilities. (ii) Forming: testing the waters, “what is our purpose? what are the others like?” (iii) Norming: norms agreed, consensus. (iv) Adjourning: rites and rituals affirming success as the group disperses. (b) Gersick’s punctuated equilibrium model, which describes groups with deadlines: Phase 1 (weeks 1–5) makes little visible progress under the assumptions set at the first meeting; the midpoint transition comes at almost exactly halfway (week 5 of 10), when the need to move on is apparent and the group may seek outside advice (here, the client); Phase 2 plays out the new approach. Week 1: prepare the first meeting carefully, since it sets Phase 1, and stress motivation and excitement. Week 5: manage the midpoint transition: evaluate Phase 1 ideas, clarify questions with the client, accept that a fundamental change of approach is needed, and make sure resources are ready for Phase 2 (and resist any deadline change).
Problem 2 — Task Type and Group Size

Classify each task as additive, disjunctive or conjunctive, and say whether adding members should raise or lower performance: (a) volunteers stuffing envelopes for a mail campaign; (b) a security team trying to spot the one flaw in a new app before launch; (c) a surgical team where every step must be done correctly in sequence; (d) a debate team that wins if any one member can rebut the judges’ final question. (e) For (a), explain why doubling the group from 10 to 20 might not double the output.

Show answer
(a) Additive: output is the sum of individual efforts; potential rises with size. (b) Disjunctive: success depends on the best member finding the flaw; potential rises with size because a superior performer is more likely to be included. (c) Conjunctive: performance is limited by the poorest member (one weak link ruins the sequence); adding members lowers potential and actual performance because a weak link becomes more likely. (d) Disjunctive (one member is enough, a logical OR). (e) Process losses: actual performance = potential performance − process losses. More members create coordination problems (crowding, waiting for supplies, communication) and motivation problems (social loafing, since individual contributions are harder to see). So total output rises with size only up to a point, and output per member falls (Exhibit 7.3).
Problem 3 — Diagnose the Role Problem

Label each as role ambiguity or one of the four kinds of role conflict, and give the matching managerial fix: (a) A new analyst does not know how her performance will be evaluated or how far her authority extends. (b) A store supervisor is told by the regional manager to cut staff hours and by her employees to protect their shifts. (c) A sales rep is told by his manager to “never oversell to customers,” then told to hit a quota that requires overselling. (d) A nurse who is also a union steward and a single parent cannot meet the time demands of all three. (e) An accountant is asked to backdate invoices, which he believes is unethical.

Show answer
(a) Role ambiguity (lack of clarity of job goals or methods), likely at the focal person stage, since she is new to the role; fix: clear performance expectations and performance feedback. (b) Intersender role conflict: two or more senders (from above and below) with incompatible expectations; fix: confer with the other role senders. (c) Intrasender role conflict: a single sender with incompatible expectations; fix: avoid self-contradictory messages. (d) Interrole conflict: several roles held by one person clash; fix: be sensitive to multiple role demands. (e) Person–role conflict: the role demands behaviour incompatible with his personality or values; the text notes many whistle-blowing cases signal it; fix: fit the right person to the right role (and do not demand unethical behaviour). Typical consequences of both ambiguity and conflict: dissatisfaction, stress, lower commitment and turnover intentions.
Problem 4 — The Tight-Knit Crew That Won’t Speed Up

A warehouse night crew has worked together for years, hazes new hires through a tough first month, and recently beat the day crew in a safety competition. Management raises the hourly picking target, but the crew quietly agrees to stay at the old pace; the one new member who picks faster is first lectured, then ignored. (a) Which factors explain the crew’s cohesiveness? (b) Use the consequences of cohesiveness and Exhibit 7.6 to explain the crew’s behaviour. (c) Is “break up the crew to reduce cohesion” the right fix? Explain.

Show answer
(a) Toughness of initiation (the hazing month), competition and success (beating the day crew), and probably stability and similarity (years together, so low member diversity); a small crew size would add to it. (b) Cohesive groups produce more conformity: they react to a deviant first by increasing communication (lecturing the fast picker) and, if that fails, by isolating him (ignoring him). In highly cohesive groups members’ productivity is similar, and whether it is high or low depends on whether the group accepts the organization’s norm; here it rejects the new target, so it is especially effective at limiting productivity (the low, narrow curve in Exhibit 7.6), like the informal norms that restrict output under piece rates. (c) Not necessarily. Cohesiveness helps when group and organizational goals align, and the text ties a good labour-relations climate to productive cohesive groups. A better fix is to win acceptance of the norm: explain and involve the crew in setting the target (specific, challenging goals the group accepts raise performance), reward group performance, and use the crew’s cohesion as an asset rather than destroying it.
Problem 5 — Application: The Jacket That Missed Its Launch

Northshore Outfitters forms a 14-person team to design a new winter jacket line in 12 weeks: designers in Toronto, engineers and a supplier representative in Vancouver, and marketing staff in Ho Chi Minh City, working mostly by email. The team’s first meeting was a 20-minute video call with no agenda. By week 6 there is still no agreed design, and the designers and engineers are fighting over what “technical” means: the designers want a sleek, high-fashion look; the engineers want heavy, waterproof materials. Two marketing members have stopped contributing (“the others will sort it out”), and a third says she will match their effort. Toronto staff complain they do not trust Vancouver’s cost estimates, and nobody is sure who has the authority to make the final call. Senior management keeps moving the launch date. In the style of a group-assignment answer (Name → Define → Apply → Recommend), diagnose the team’s problems and recommend a package of changes, using at least five concepts from this chapter.

Show answer
Name & define: (1) Cross-functional team: people with different functional specialties brought together to invent, design or deliver a product. (2) Virtual team: a work group using technology to collaborate across time, space and organizational boundaries. (3) Punctuated equilibrium model: groups with deadlines are shaped by their first meeting and a midpoint transition. (4) Shared mental models: members share similar information about how to interact and what the task is. (5) Social loafing: withholding effort on a group task, in its free rider and sucker forms. (6) Role ambiguity: lack of clarity of job goals or methods, including the limits of one’s authority. (7) Superordinate goal: an attractive outcome achievable only by collaboration.

Apply: The team is a cross-functional, virtual team that is breaking several of the text’s principles. Its 20-minute, agenda-free first meeting set weak assumptions that dominated Phase 1, and week 6 is the midpoint of a 12-week deadline: the moment to change approach, which management is undermining by moving the deadline (the text says to resist deadline changes). The “technical” fight is a missing shared mental model, exactly like the text’s “tough truck” example, worsened by having no superordinate goal to override conflicting functional objectives. Two marketers are free riding; the third shows the sucker effect. At 14 people the team is large, so process losses (coordination and motivation) and loafing are more likely. Distrust of Vancouver’s estimates is the classic virtual-team challenge of trust, alongside miscommunication by lean email. And no one knowing who decides is role ambiguity plus a missing rules and procedures principle.

Recommend: Timeline: treat week 6 as the midpoint transition: hold a well-prepared, longer synchronous session that evaluates Phase 1 ideas, clarifies the brief with management, adopts a new approach, and confirms resources for Phase 2; ask management to fix the launch date. Cross-functional principles: set a superordinate goal (for example, “the jacket that looks good enough for the city and performs well enough for the mountains”); agree on rules and procedures (who has final authority, such as one decision-maker per area with an agreed tie-break), which also cures the role ambiguity; give the team autonomy from micromanagement; choose a leader with strong people skills; and build a shared mental model by defining “technical” together, with samples. Loafing: make individual contributions visible, give each marketer an indispensable piece, add feedback and group rewards, and consider a smaller core team. Virtual lessons: personalization (a video kickoff, informal time), leadership that sets communication standards and gives progress updates, training in cross-cultural communication, and weekly peer feedback; follow Orange’s lesson of small teams, strong leadership and a common working framework, and focus on outputs to build trust.
