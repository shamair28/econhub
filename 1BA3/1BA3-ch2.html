Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Almost every concept you will meet later in this course — what motivates someone, why a team gels or falls apart, how a leader gets discretionary effort out of a reluctant employee — rests on an unspoken assumption: that you already have a working answer to two separate questions. First, who is this person? Are they naturally conscientious, or do they need external structure to stay organized? Do they thrive on unpredictability or wilt under it? Second, how do people change their behaviour over time? Why does a new hire who was praised constantly during training suddenly slack off once the praise stops? Why does punishing lateness sometimes backfire? Chapter 2 answers the first question with personality and the second with learning — two of the most heavily tested topics in this course precisely because they are the load-bearing walls under almost everything that comes later.

The two topics also work as a pair conceptually, not just administratively. Personality theory treats individual differences as relatively fixed — traits that predispose someone toward certain behaviours across many situations. Learning theory treats behaviour as fundamentally changeable — shaped, strengthened, or extinguished by its consequences. A manager who understands only personality risks concluding that a poor performer is permanently a poor performer; a manager who understands only learning risks assuming that the right reward schedule can turn anyone into anyone. Johns and Saks present both because effective organizational behaviour requires holding both truths at once: some things about people are stable, and some things about behaviour are trainable.

Section 2

Part A — Personality: Three Approaches

Personality is the relatively stable set of psychological characteristics that influences the way an individual interacts with their environment, and how they feel, think, and behave. Two things about that definition are easy to skim past but worth sitting with. First, “relatively stable” is not the same as “permanently fixed” — personality can shift somewhat over a lifetime, but it does not reset from one Tuesday to the next the way a mood does. Second, personality has a dual origin: it is shaped by genetic predisposition and by a person’s long-term learning history, which is exactly why the two organizing topics of this chapter — the fixed and the trainable — turn out to be intertwined rather than opposed.

Organizational behaviour has historically explained workplace conduct through three competing lenses. The dispositional approach holds that individuals possess stable traits and characteristics that predispose them to act in particular ways regardless of the setting — hire the right disposition, in other words, and the behaviour follows. The situational approach takes the opposite position: characteristics of the organizational setting itself — its reward systems, its punishments, the way a task is designed — are what actually drive people’s feelings, attitudes, and behaviour, largely independent of who occupies the role. Both views capture something real, and both are incomplete on their own.

The interactionist approach resolves the tension by treating organizational behaviour as a joint function of both the disposition and the situation — to predict how someone will act, you need to know something about their personality and something about the environment they are in. This is the most widely accepted view in the field today, and Johns and Saks build the rest of the personality material on top of it. The clearest way to see the interaction is through the idea of weak and strong situations. In a weak situation — loosely defined roles, few rules, weak reward and punishment contingencies, such as a newly formed volunteer committee — nothing in the environment tells people how to behave, so personality fills the gap and has maximum influence. In a strong situation — clearly defined roles, explicit rules, tight contingencies, such as a routine military operation — the environment itself dictates behaviour so thoroughly that personality differences barely show through. Trait activation theory sharpens this further: a trait produces behaviour only when the situation makes the need for that trait salient. An extraverted employee will not visibly “act extraverted” while silently filing solo paperwork, but the same trait will show up the moment the job calls for networking or public speaking.

Worked Example — One Salesperson, Two Situations
Priya is naturally outgoing, persuasive, and a little impulsive. In her first role, a tightly scripted call-centre position, she reads from an approved call script, every deviation is flagged by quality monitoring, and compensation is a flat hourly wage. In her second role, a field-sales territory with no set call script, discretion over her own daily schedule, and commission-based pay, she is left to develop her own approach to each client. In which role would you expect Priya’s personality to more strongly predict her performance and behaviour, and why?
Step 1 — Identify the situation type in each role
  Call-centre: scripted calls, monitored compliance, flat pay
  → clearly defined roles + rules + contingencies = STRONG situation

  Field sales: no script, schedule discretion, commission pay
  → loosely defined roles + few rules + weak contingencies = WEAK situation

Step 2 — Apply the interactionist principle
  Strong situations suppress personality's influence — Priya's
  outgoing, impulsive style will show up less because the script
  and monitoring constrain HOW she can behave.
  Weak situations let personality drive behaviour — with no script
  to follow, Priya's natural persuasiveness and initiative determine
  how she performs.

Step 3 — Conclude
  → Priya's personality will predict her behaviour and results far
    more strongly in the field-sales role than in the call-centre
    role, even though she brings the same traits to both jobs.
    This is trait activation theory in practice: her extraversion is
    "activated" only when the situation gives it room to matter.
Interactionism Is the Textbook’s Endorsed View When an exam question asks which approach organizational behaviour research “most widely accepts,” the answer is the interactionist approach — not dispositional, not situational alone. Dispositional and situational are the two historical positions the interactionist view was built to reconcile.
Section 3

Part A — The Big Five

The Five-Factor Model, better known as the Big Five, organizes personality into five relatively independent dimensions. “Independent” is the operative word: a person’s score on one dimension tells you nothing about their score on another, so someone can be highly conscientious and highly extraverted, highly conscientious and introverted, or any other combination. The textbook’s Exhibit 2.1 illustrates this by drawing each dimension as its own separate bipolar scale — for example, “Sociable, Talkative” at one end and “Quiet, Reserved” at the other for extraversion — rather than collapsing all five into a single composite score. That visual choice matters for how you should think about the model: there is no single “good” personality profile, only five separate sliders that combine differently in every person.

Each dimension has a clear low and high pole, and each has been linked to different work outcomes. Extraversion runs from shy and reserved to outgoing, sociable, and energetic, and it matters most in jobs built around interpersonal interaction — sales and management being the textbook examples. Emotional stability runs from anxious, hostile, and insecure at the low end to calm and confident at the high end; the low end of this same dimension is what the literature calls neuroticism — they are the same trait viewed from opposite poles, not two different traits. Agreeableness runs from cold and argumentative to friendly, warm, and cooperative, and it matters most in roles that depend on helping and teamwork. Conscientiousness runs from careless and impulsive to responsible, dependable, and achievement-oriented, and unlike the other four, it is the strongest and most consistent predictor of job performance across every occupation studied — not just the interpersonally demanding ones. Openness to experience runs from conventional thinking to flexible, curious, and receptive to new ideas, and it matters most in roles requiring ongoing learning or creativity.

Because conscientiousness predicts performance almost everywhere, and emotional stability underpins the resilience to handle stress and setbacks in almost any job, the research finding you should remember is that conscientiousness combined with emotional stability predicts job performance more broadly across occupations than any other pairing of traits — the other three dimensions add predictive power only in the specific job families where they are relevant. This is not just textbook theory: recruiters at Canadian companies like Kirmac Collision & Autoglass have been reported to informally weight personality — conscientiousness in particular — above technical experience when hiring, on the logic that a dependable, achievement-oriented employee can be trained on technical skill, but conscientiousness itself is much harder to train into someone who does not already have it.

Exam Trap — Neuroticism Is a Pole, Not a Sixth Trait Neuroticism is not a separate personality dimension from emotional stability — it is the low end of the emotional stability scale. A question describing someone as “high in neuroticism” is describing the same person a different question would call “low in emotional stability.” Watch for reversed-scoring questions that test whether you can flip between the two framings without getting confused.

Lecture Supplement — Personality Preferences: Jung and the Myers-Briggs

This material is from Dr. Kassaye’s Week 2 lecture slides (slides 12–13), not from the Johns & Saks textbook. Because it was taught in class, treat it as examinable.

Alongside the Big Five, the lecture introduces a second, older way of describing personality: personality preferences, drawn from the work of Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung. Jung’s two claims, as the slides state them, are that people’s behaviour is rarely random but reflects a stable pattern of personal preferences, and that large percentages of people in certain occupations tend to share similar preferences — which is why the framework is popular in career counselling and team-building workshops.

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) operationalizes Jung’s ideas as four two-sided preferences. A person is sorted to one side of each, and the four letters together give one of sixteen “types” (for example, ESTJ or INFP):

Extraversion (E) or Introversion (I)
Where you direct your energy and attention — outward to people and activity, or inward to ideas and reflection.
Sensing (S) or Intuition (N)
How you take in information — through concrete facts and present details, or through patterns, possibilities, and the big picture.
Thinking (T) or Feeling (F)
How you make decisions — by impersonal logic and consistency, or by values and the effect on people.
Judgment (J) or Perception (P)
How you deal with the outside world — preferring structure, plans, and closure, or preferring flexibility, spontaneity, and keeping options open.
Common Confusion — MBTI Preferences vs. Big Five Traits These are two different kinds of model, and a question can test whether you know which is which. The Big Five describes traits on continuous scales — you are more or less conscientious, and the research links each dimension to measurable work outcomes (Section 3 above). The MBTI sorts people into categorical preferences — you are either a T or an F — and is a Jungian typology, not a trait model. Only one letter overlaps in name: MBTI’s Extraversion/Introversion maps loosely onto the Big Five’s extraversion dimension; the other three MBTI pairs have no one-to-one Big Five equivalent. If a question asks which framework the textbook and most OB research treat as the best-supported predictor of job performance, the answer is the Big Five; if it asks which framework describes the S/N or J/P preference, that is the MBTI.

Section 4

Part A — Other Personality Variables That Matter at Work

Beyond the Big Five, several narrower personality variables show up repeatedly in OB research because they predict specific workplace behaviours especially well. Locus of control describes a person’s beliefs about where the factors that control their behaviour are located. High internals believe they control their own outcomes through their own effort, and this belief is associated with higher job satisfaction, stronger organizational commitment, better pay outcomes, more active career planning, and lower stress. High externals believe that luck, fate, or powerful other people control what happens to them, and they tend to show the opposite pattern on each of those outcomes.

A cluster of other traits round out the picture. Positive affectivity (PA) and negative affectivity (NA) are enduring, largely genetic emotional dispositions — and critically, the textbook treats them as relatively independent dimensions, not opposite ends of a single scale. A person is not simply “high PA” because they are “low NA”; someone can be high on both, low on both, or any mix. High-PA people experience positive emotions and see the world in a positive light, and PA turns out to be the strongest personality predictor of employee engagement discussed in the text; high-NA people experience negative emotions and view the world negatively, with links to more stress, more counterproductive behaviour, and more withdrawal. Proactive personality is a stable disposition to take the initiative to improve one’s circumstances or create new ones — the resulting proactive behaviour is linked to job satisfaction, performance, engagement, organizational citizenship behaviours, leadership effectiveness, and long-run career success.

Self-Monitoring
High self-monitors continuously observe and adjust how they present themselves to fit social cues — well suited to sales, law, public relations, and politics, and more likely to emerge as informal leaders, but they also report more role stress and lower organizational commitment. Low self-monitors behave consistently regardless of audience — “what you see is what you get” — with the opposite trade-offs.
Self-Esteem & Behavioural Plasticity
Self-esteem is the degree to which a person holds a positive self-evaluation. Behavioural plasticity theory predicts that people with low self-esteem are more “pliable” — more susceptible to external and social influence — than people with high self-esteem, and they also react more negatively to critical feedback.
Proactive Personality & General Self-Efficacy
General self-efficacy (GSE) is a broad, stable motivational trait: a person’s overall belief in their ability to succeed across a wide range of challenging situations, built up from a lifetime of accumulated successes and failures. Combined with a proactive disposition, high-GSE employees are more likely to take on stretch assignments without being asked.

Judge, Locke, and Durham combined four of these already-defined traits into a single broader concept called core self-evaluations (CSEs): self-esteem, general self-efficacy, locus of control, and neuroticism (emotional stability) — exactly those four and no others. CSEs are among the best dispositional predictors of both job satisfaction and job performance in the personality literature, precisely because bundling four related traits captures more of a person’s underlying self-regard than any single trait alone.

Worked Example — A New Hire’s Reaction to Critical Feedback
Two weeks into the job, Marcus receives blunt written feedback from his manager listing three things he did wrong on his first client deliverable, with no acknowledgement of what he did well. He goes quiet for the rest of the day, tells a coworker “I probably shouldn’t have taken this job,” and seems to expect to be let go by the end of the month. Analyze Marcus’s reaction using three personality concepts from this section.
Concept 1 — Locus of control
  Marcus jumps straight to "I'll probably be let go" rather than
  "here's what I'll fix" — consistent with a more EXTERNAL locus
  of control, where outcomes feel controlled by the manager's
  judgment rather than his own next actions.

Concept 2 — Self-esteem / behavioural plasticity theory
  His strong, immediate emotional reaction to one piece of negative
  feedback — generalizing it to "I shouldn't have taken this job" —
  fits behavioural plasticity theory's prediction that people with
  LOWER self-esteem react more negatively to critical feedback and
  are more easily swayed by a single social input.

Concept 3 — Negative affectivity (NA)
  If this pattern (going quiet, catastrophizing, expecting the
  worst) is how Marcus tends to react across many situations, not
  just this one, that consistency points to trait-level NA rather
  than a one-off bad day.

Conclusion for the manager: pair future feedback with concrete
next steps and at least one specific positive observation — this
softens the impact for a lower-self-esteem, higher-NA employee
without lowering the standard being communicated.
Exam Trap — GSE vs. Task-Specific Self-Efficacy General self-efficacy (GSE), defined here in Part A, is a broad, stable personality trait: a general belief in your ability to succeed across many different challenging situations. Later in this chapter (Part B), social cognitive theory introduces task-specific self-efficacy beliefs — a person’s confidence in their ability to perform one specific task. The two are easy to conflate, but they belong to different theories: a person can have high GSE overall while still having low self-efficacy for one narrow task they have never attempted, such as public speaking.
Exam Trap — The Four CSE Traits, Exactly Core self-evaluations is a bundle of precisely four named traits: self-esteem, general self-efficacy, locus of control, and neuroticism (emotional stability). Extraversion and conscientiousness are NOT among the four, even though they are well-known Big Five dimensions — a classic “which of the following is NOT” question substitutes one of these two as a plausible-looking wrong answer.

Part B — What Is Learning? Operant Learning Theory

Having spent three sections on the relatively stable side of the person–situation equation, Part B turns to the side that is deliberately changeable: learning. Formally, learning occurs when practice or experience produces a relatively permanent change in behaviour potential — a definition that deliberately excludes temporary changes caused by drugs, fatigue, or simple biological maturation. What employees learn on the job falls into four broad content categories: practical skills (job-specific, technical know-how), intrapersonal skills (problem solving, critical thinking, risk taking), interpersonal skills (communication, teamwork, conflict resolution), and cultural awareness (an organization’s norms, goals, and expectations). Almost every training program you will encounter in your career targets one or more of these four categories, even when it does not say so explicitly.

The dominant framework for how learning actually happens at work is B. F. Skinner’s operant learning theory: the idea that a person learns to operate on their environment to produce consequences, and that their future behaviour is controlled by the consequences that followed it in the past. The mechanism is reinforcement — the process by which stimuli strengthen behaviours — carried out by a reinforcer, any stimulus that follows a behaviour and increases or maintains the probability that the behaviour will happen again. There are exactly two ways to reinforce a behaviour, and the distinction between them is the single most heavily tested idea in this chapter. Positive reinforcement increases or maintains a behaviour by adding a stimulus — praise, a bonus, public recognition — immediately after the behaviour occurs. Negative reinforcement increases or maintains a behaviour by removing a stimulus — most often something unpleasant — immediately after the behaviour occurs. Both processes increase behaviour; they differ only in whether something is added or taken away.

Reinforcement fails in practice more often from managerial error than from the theory being wrong. Johns and Saks identify three recurring organizational errors. The first is confusing rewards with reinforcers: a reward like seniority-based overtime access is not contingent on any specific desired behaviour, so — however generous it is — it does not actually reinforce anything in particular. The second is neglecting diversity in reinforcer preferences: individual and generational differences mean the same “reward” (a plaque, a gift card, public praise in a meeting) can be strongly reinforcing to one employee and nearly meaningless to another. The third is neglecting important sources of reinforcement, especially performance feedback and social recognition — both are often far more powerful reinforcers than the manager assumes, and both are free.

Worked Example — Reinforcement in Three Workplace Scenes
(1) A supervisor stops emailing daily reminders to a technician once the technician starts submitting timesheets on time without being asked. (2) A manager begins publicly thanking a cashier by name every time she proactively offers a receipt-based promotion to customers, and the behaviour becomes routine. (3) An employee starts arriving fifteen minutes early each day after their manager stops assigning them the unpopular Monday inventory count whenever they do. For each, identify whether positive or negative reinforcement is at work.
(1) Daily reminder emails STOP once timesheets are on time
  → something unpleasant (nagging emails) is REMOVED
  → behaviour (on-time submission) is maintained
  = NEGATIVE REINFORCEMENT

(2) Public thanks is ADDED every time the behaviour occurs
  → something pleasant (social recognition) is ADDED
  → behaviour (offering the promotion) increases
  = POSITIVE REINFORCEMENT

(3) The unpopular inventory count is REMOVED when arriving early
  → something unpleasant (the unwanted task) is REMOVED
  → behaviour (early arrival) increases
  = NEGATIVE REINFORCEMENT

Shortcut: ask two questions only — (a) was something ADDED or
REMOVED? (b) did the target behaviour go UP? If the answer to (b)
is "up," you are looking at one of the two reinforcement types,
never punishment or extinction — those two only ever DECREASE
behaviour.
Exam Trap — Negative Reinforcement Is NOT Punishment This is the most commonly confused pairing in the entire course. Negative reinforcement increases a behaviour by removing something unpleasant — it is a form of reinforcement, not a form of discipline. Punishment, covered in the next section, decreases a behaviour by adding something unpleasant. If a question describes a consequence that makes a behaviour happen more often, the answer can never be “punishment,” no matter how unpleasant the stimulus being removed sounds.

Section 6

Part B — Reinforcement Strategies, Extinction, and Punishment

Not all reinforcement schedules produce the same kind of learning. Continuous, immediate reinforcement — rewarding every instance of a behaviour right after it happens — produces fast acquisition: the behaviour ramps up quickly. But it also produces low persistence: once the reinforcement stops, the behaviour drops off almost as quickly as it appeared. Partial, delayed reinforcement — rewarding only some instances, and not always right away — produces slower learning at first, but the resulting behaviour is far more persistent once reinforcement is reduced or withdrawn altogether. This is why behaviour learned under a partial or delayed schedule is genuinely harder to extinguish than behaviour learned under a continuous, immediate one.

Extinction is the gradual dissipation of a behaviour following the termination of the reinforcement that had been maintaining it — no new consequence is added, the old one is simply withdrawn. Extinction works best when it is paired with reinforcing a substitute behaviour, so the person has something else to do instead of the behaviour you are trying to eliminate. Punishment is a different mechanism entirely: it is the application of an aversive stimulus following a behaviour, intended to decrease its probability. Punishment and negative reinforcement are often confused because both involve something unpleasant, but they sit on opposite ends of the add/remove logic introduced in the previous section — punishment adds the unpleasant stimulus, negative reinforcement removes it.

Punishment has real practical problems that make it a poor first choice for changing behaviour. It only communicates what not to do, without teaching the person what they should do instead. It tends to produce emotional side effects — resentment, anxiety, avoidance of the punisher — that can outlast whatever behavioural gain was achieved. Timing matters enormously, and delayed punishment (as in a supervisor who criticizes an error weeks after it happened) loses most of its effectiveness. And because punishment is itself a form of behaviour, the person delivering it can inadvertently learn to rely on it too readily. None of this means punishment should never be used — only that it needs to be used carefully, following a specific set of guidelines.

  1. Make sure the chosen punishment is genuinely aversive to that specific person — what stings for one employee may not register for another.
  2. Punish immediately, as close to the behaviour as possible, so the connection between action and consequence stays clear.
  3. Do not reward the unwanted behaviour before or after punishing it — mixed signals undercut the message.
  4. Keep emotions under control, and avoid punishing an employee in front of observers.
  5. Provide an acceptable alternative behaviour, so the person knows what to do instead of what they were just told not to do.
  6. Watch for accidentally punishing desirable behaviour — for example, cutting next year’s budget for a department that came in under its capital budget teaches every department to spend right up to the limit.
Worked Example — Fixing Chronic Lateness Three Ways
A team lead has an employee who is late roughly twice a week. The team lead is considering three options: (A) say nothing and simply stop assigning any consequence at all when the employee is late, hoping it fades on its own; (B) formally write up the employee and dock a half-day’s pay the next time it happens; (C) publicly and immediately praise the employee’s teammates every day they personally arrive on time, while saying nothing about lateness itself. Evaluate each option.
Option A — "Do nothing" is not extinction
  Extinction requires withdrawing a reinforcement that was
  ACTUALLY maintaining the behaviour. If nothing was ever
  reinforcing the lateness in the first place, doing nothing
  differently is not extinction — it's just inaction, and the
  lateness has no reason to change.

Option B — Punishment
  Docking pay is a clear aversive stimulus applied after the
  behaviour = PUNISHMENT. Can work, but only if delivered
  immediately (not "the next time"), calibrated to be genuinely
  aversive to this employee, and paired with a clear alternative —
  otherwise it risks resentment without changing the habit.

Option C — Reinforcing the alternative
  Praising ON-TIME arrival (a positive stimulus added for the
  desired behaviour) is POSITIVE REINFORCEMENT of the alternative
  the team lead actually wants — showing up on time — rather than
  a consequence attached to lateness at all.

Best strategy: combine B and C rather than relying on either
alone — immediate, calibrated consequences for lateness PLUS
consistent positive reinforcement the moment the employee arrives
on time, giving the employee both a reason to stop the old
behaviour and a reason to keep doing the new one.
Exam Trap — Do Not Reverse the Reinforcement Schedules It is tempting to assume that MORE reinforcement (continuous, immediate) always produces the BEST result, but “best” depends on what you are optimizing for. Continuous, immediate reinforcement wins on speed of acquisition; partial, delayed reinforcement wins on persistence after reinforcement stops. Multiple-choice questions frequently swap this pairing — read carefully for whether the question asks about how fast a behaviour is learned or how long it lasts.

Part B — Social Cognitive Theory

Social cognitive theory (SCT), developed by Albert Bandura, complements operant learning theory rather than replacing it — it adds the role of cognitive processes to the picture of how people learn and regulate their own behaviour. Where operant theory explains behaviour purely through external consequences, SCT explains it through triadic reciprocal causation: personal factors, the environment, and behaviour itself all interact and influence one another, rather than causation running in a single direction from environment to behaviour.

SCT has three components. Observational learning is learning by watching and imitating a model rather than only through direct personal experience — it involves a form of self-reinforcement, in which the observer imagines that the model’s positive outcomes will come their way too if they copy the behaviour. Not every model is equally influential: effective models tend to be attractive, credible, competent, high-status, and memorable, which is exactly why organizations are careful about who they select to demonstrate a new process in a training video or a town hall.

The second component, self-efficacy beliefs, is where the chapter’s sharpest exam trap lives. Self-efficacy beliefs are task-specific: a person’s belief in their ability to successfully perform one particular task, as opposed to the broad, stable personality trait of general self-efficacy from Part A. The same person can have high confidence for one task and low confidence for another entirely different one. Bandura identified exactly four sources of self-efficacy beliefs, and this list is worth memorizing verbatim: performance mastery (your own past success at the task), observation of others (watching someone similar to you succeed at it), verbal persuasion and social influence (someone credible telling you that you can do it), and physiological and emotional state (how calm, energized, or anxious your body feels heading into the task).

The third component, self-regulation, describes how people use learning principles to manage their own behaviour, reducing how much external control is needed at all. It works through self-observation, self-evaluation against a standard, and self-reinforcement, and it produces two distinct patterns: discrepancy reduction, working to close the gap between a goal and current performance, and discrepancy production, deliberately raising the bar and setting an even higher goal once the original one has been reached. Self-regulation is, in a real sense, SCT applied to yourself rather than to someone you are training.

Worked Example — Building Confidence in a Nervous Presenter
A new hire, Aaliyah, has excellent written work but is visibly anxious about an upcoming client presentation and tells her manager she does not think she can do it well. Design a short preparation plan that uses all four sources of self-efficacy from social cognitive theory.
Source 1 — Performance mastery
  Have Aaliyah present a LOW-STAKES internal version first (to her
  own team) so she banks a real, direct success before the client
  meeting — mastery from her own experience is the strongest source.

Source 2 — Observation of others
  Have her watch a recording of a colleague she sees as similar to
  herself (comparable seniority, comparable communication style)
  successfully delivering a client presentation.

Source 3 — Verbal persuasion / social influence
  Have her manager and a trusted senior colleague explicitly tell
  her, specifically and credibly, that they've seen her handle
  harder client conversations informally and believe she is ready.

Source 4 — Physiological / emotional state
  Schedule a short walk or breathing exercise immediately before
  the presentation, and avoid stacking it right after another
  stressful meeting, so her physical state going in reads as calm
  rather than keyed-up.

Result: all four sources targeted deliberately, rather than
leaving Aaliyah's self-efficacy for this one task to chance.
Exam Trap — Self-Reinforcement vs. External Reinforcement A question that asks “which theory does NOT rely on direct external reinforcement” is pointing at social cognitive theory. Observational learning works through self-reinforcement — imagining a model’s rewards will transfer to you — not through an external party directly reinforcing your own behaviour, which is the mechanism operant learning theory depends on. SCT complements operant theory; it does not require the same external consequence loop.

Section 8

Part B — Organizational Learning Practices

Organizational behaviour modification (O.B. Mod) is the systematic use of learning principles to influence behaviour across an organization, rather than leaving reinforcement to informal chance. It works: in one documented food-manufacturing plant, a program combining feedback charts with supervisor praise raised the rate of safe work practices from 74 percent to roughly 97 percent. The underlying finding generalizes well beyond that one plant — money, feedback, and social recognition are all effective positive reinforcers on their own, but they produce the strongest effect on task performance when used together rather than relying on any single one.

Employee recognition programs formalize positive reinforcement at an organizational level: to work, they need to specify how a person is recognized, what specific behaviour is being encouraged, how the acknowledgment happens publicly, and what token or icon the recipient actually receives. Peer recognition programs extend the same idea sideways, letting employees formally recognize each other rather than routing all recognition through a manager — Canadian examples include social recognition platforms used at organizations like RBC.

Finally, the chapter distinguishes training from development: training builds the knowledge and skill an employee needs for their current job, while development focuses on preparing them for future responsibilities. Behaviour modelling training (BMT) is the most direct organizational application of the observational learning component of social cognitive theory from the previous section: describe the target behaviours explicitly, provide a credible model demonstrating them, let trainees practise, deliver feedback and social reinforcement, and take deliberate steps to transfer what was learned back onto the actual job.

Group Assignment Angle If Group Assignment #1 asks your team to design an intervention rather than just diagnose a problem, a well-specified employee or peer recognition program is a strong, concrete answer: name the target behaviour precisely, name who does the recognizing and how publicly, and name the actual token or icon involved. Vague answers (“give more recognition”) score worse than specific, implementable ones.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Big Five Scenario

A junior consultant is described by her team as unfailingly organized, always meets deadlines early, and volunteers for extra client work even when it is not required. Which Big Five dimension does this describe most directly, and why is this dimension considered a strong predictor of job performance in almost any occupation?

Show answer
This describes high conscientiousness — organized, dependable, achievement-oriented behaviour is the defining pattern of this dimension. Conscientiousness is the strongest and most consistent predictor of job performance across virtually all occupations because dependability and follow-through matter in nearly every job, unlike traits such as extraversion or openness, whose predictive power is concentrated in specific job families (sales/management, or learning/creative roles, respectively).
Problem 2 — Which Statement Is FALSE?

Which of the following statements about reinforcement, punishment, and extinction is FALSE?
(a) Positive reinforcement adds a pleasant stimulus to increase a behaviour.
(b) Negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus to increase a behaviour.
(c) Punishment removes a pleasant stimulus to decrease a behaviour.
(d) Extinction withdraws the reinforcement that was maintaining a behaviour, with no new consequence added.

Show answer
(c) is FALSE. Punishment does not remove a pleasant stimulus — it ADDS an aversive (unpleasant) stimulus following a behaviour to decrease its probability. Statements (a), (b), and (d) are all accurate definitions straight from the chapter, and (c) is the classic trap answer that swaps punishment’s add/remove logic.
Problem 3 — Compare and Apply

Two employees receive the same negative performance review after missing a major deadline. Employee A immediately asks her manager what specifically she should change to hit the next deadline. Employee B tells coworkers “the deadline was always unrealistic — there was nothing I could have done.” Identify each employee’s likely locus of control, and explain how this belief will shape their career planning behaviour going forward.

Show answer
Employee A shows an internal locus of control — she treats the outcome as something within her own control and immediately looks for what she personally can change, a pattern linked to more active career planning and higher job satisfaction over time. Employee B shows an external locus of control — she attributes the outcome to factors outside her control, a pattern linked to more passive career planning, since she does not see her own actions as the lever that changes outcomes. Over multiple performance cycles, Employee A is more likely to actively seek out training or stretch assignments, while Employee B is more likely to wait for circumstances to change on their own.
Problem 4 — Name the Source

Before her first solo negotiation, Tia’s mentor tells her, “I’ve watched you handle three tough client calls this year — you’re ready for this one.” Which of Bandura’s four sources of self-efficacy does this represent, and name one of the other three sources that is NOT being used here.

Show answer
This is verbal persuasion and social influence — a credible person (her mentor) directly telling Tia she is capable, based on his own observation of her track record. It is not performance mastery (which would require Tia herself to have already succeeded at a solo negotiation specifically), not observation of others (which would require Tia to watch someone else negotiate), and not physiological/emotional state (which concerns how her body feels heading into the task, not what she is told). Any one of those three is an acceptable second answer.
Problem 5 — Application: A Call Centre’s New-Hire Turnover Problem

A mid-sized insurance call centre loses about 40% of new hires within their first 90 days. Exit interviews reveal two recurring themes: (1) many new hires say the highly scripted, tightly monitored calls felt “soul-crushing” almost immediately, especially the ones who described themselves in onboarding surveys as outgoing and eager to build rapport with customers; and (2) new hires say they received no feedback at all during training beyond a pass/fail score on a compliance checklist at the end of week two, and several say they felt like nothing they did differently made any difference to how they were treated. Using concepts from BOTH personality (Part A) and learning (Part B), diagnose what is going wrong and recommend two concrete changes.

Show answer
Personality diagnosis (Part A): The call centre is a classic strong situation — a tightly scripted, closely monitored role with little discretion. Under the interactionist approach, strong situations suppress the influence of personality, which is precisely the problem for new hires who described themselves as outgoing and eager to build rapport: their extraversion has no room to express itself, and trait activation theory predicts that a trait with no outlet in the situation will not show up as engagement or satisfaction — it will show up as frustration and disengagement instead. This is a hiring-fit problem as much as a training problem: the role may be systematically mismatched to the traits the centre is recruiting for.

Learning diagnosis (Part B): The “nothing made any difference” comment describes an environment with no functioning reinforcement at all — new hires cannot tell what behaviour is being reinforced because the only feedback is a delayed, binary pass/fail checkpoint two weeks in. This violates the guidance not to neglect important sources of reinforcement, especially performance feedback and social recognition, and it also means whatever good habits new hires do form receive no reinforcement schedule to strengthen them, so they never solidify.

Two recommendations: (1) Introduce immediate, specific performance feedback and social recognition during training itself — even brief daily check-ins that reinforce specific correct behaviours — rather than a single delayed compliance score, applying the operant learning principle that reinforcement works best when it is immediate and tied to a specific behaviour. (2) Where compliance rules allow, build in small pockets of scripted flexibility (e.g., an approved “personalize this line” moment in the call) so that self-monitoring and extraversion have at least some outlet, softening the strong-situation effect for the new hires whose personality is the best fit for a customer-facing role in the first place — and consider weighting the hiring process toward candidates who score well on conscientiousness specifically, since conscientiousness (unlike extraversion) predicts performance even within a heavily scripted, rule-bound role.
