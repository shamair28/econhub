Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every topic you will encounter for the rest of this course — personality and values, perception and decision making, motivation, teams, leadership, conflict, and organizational change — sits on top of the ideas introduced in this chapter. OB is fundamentally about three things: predicting how people in organizations will behave, explaining why they behave that way, and managing that behaviour to accomplish organizational goals. Once you internalize this "predict, explain, manage" frame, you will notice it resurfacing in almost every later chapter, from why some employees respond better to certain leadership styles to why a well-designed incentive plan can still backfire.

You do not need to aspire to a management title for this chapter to matter. Every graduate of a Commerce program works inside an organization, reports to someone, coordinates with colleagues, and is shaped by the organization's structure and culture whether or not "manager" ever appears in their job title. Understanding why organizations are structured the way they are, what separates a manager who merely supervises from one who is genuinely effective, and how today's employers think about diversity, well-being, and corporate responsibility will make you a sharper employee, a better teammate on group projects, and — if you do end up managing people one day — considerably less likely to reach for whatever "quick fix" management fad is trending that year.

Chapter Roadmap This chapter covers what organizations are and what OB studies, the three goals of OB (predicting, explaining, managing), the classical viewpoint and the human relations movement's critique of bureaucracy, the contingency approach that reconciles them, what managers actually do (Mintzberg's roles, Luthans's activities, Kotter's agendas), and five contemporary management concerns. This is Week 1 material, tested on the Midterm (Oct 29), and it falls within Group Assignment #1 (Ch 1–6).
Section 2

What Are Organizations, and What Is OB?

An organization is a social invention for accomplishing common goals through group effort. That phrase — "social invention" — is doing a lot of work: organizations do not occur naturally the way a family or a friendship might. They are deliberately created and maintained by people. Three features define any organization, whether it is a five-person startup or a multinational bank. First, organizations depend on the coordinated presence of people — not necessarily on physical assets or capital. A courier company can lease its vans and rent its warehouse, but it cannot lease its way out of needing people to coordinate. Second, organizations exist for goal accomplishment, and the most basic goal any organization has is survival and adaptation to a changing environment — everything else (profit, market share, service quality) is built on top of that baseline. Third, organizations depend on group effort, both the formal teamwork spelled out in an org chart and the informal alliances and favour-trading that develop among employees regardless of what the org chart says.

Organizational behaviour (OB) is the study of the attitudes and behaviours of individuals and groups in organizations. It is a genuinely interdisciplinary field, drawing on psychology (individual attitudes and perception), sociology (group dynamics and structure), and economics (incentives and decision making). OB does not just describe what people do at work — it systematically studies why they do it, which gives managers and employees alike real insight into how to manage and change behaviour and how to structure an organization more effectively.

Students often confuse OB with human resources management (HRM), and the exam will occasionally test whether you can tell them apart. HRM refers to the concrete programs, practices, and systems an organization uses to acquire, develop, motivate, and retain its employees — job postings, interview processes, training programs, performance reviews, compensation structures. OB is the underlying theory that HRM is built on. Personality theory (Chapter 3) is what tells HR which traits to screen for in a selection interview; learning theory is what tells a training department how to design an onboarding program that actually sticks. You could think of HRM as the applied toolkit and OB as the science that explains why the tools work.

Two more terms worth locking in early: human capital is the knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) embodied in an organization's employees — their education, training, and accumulated experience — and it is strongly and positively related to organizational performance. Social capital is a related but distinct idea: the social resources an individual obtains through participation in a social structure, meaning the value that comes from relationships and networks rather than from personal skill. Internal social capital comes from relationships within one's own organization (the colleague who always knows who to call to get something approved faster); external social capital comes from relationships with outside constituents — suppliers, clients, industry contacts. A brilliant analyst with excellent human capital but no internal social capital may struggle to get their ideas adopted, precisely because getting things done in an organization runs through relationships as much as through individual competence.

Section 3

The Three Goals of OB: Predict, Explain, Manage

OB pursues three distinct goals, and the exam draws a hard line between them. Predicting behaviour means anticipating when and whether it will occur. Explaining behaviour means understanding why it occurs — and explanation is a strictly harder problem than prediction, because a given behaviour can have multiple causes, and those causes can shift over time. The textbook's own illustration is a useful anchor: ancient societies could reliably predict when the sun would set without having the faintest explanation of orbital mechanics. Prediction does not require explanation. The two goals are related but separable, and a manager can achieve one without the other.

Managing is the art of getting things accomplished in organizations through others. Note the phrase "through others" — a manager who is personally excellent at a task but cannot get a team to reproduce that excellence is not managing well, no matter how technically skilled they are. Effective management is supposed to rest on evidence-based management: translating principles based on the best available scientific evidence into organizational practice. This is explicitly contrasted with managing by unsystematic personal experience, gut instinct, or — worse — the "quick fix," a trendy-sounding intervention adopted because it is fashionable rather than because it is supported by evidence. On the exam, "evidence-based" is a defined term, not a vague synonym for "good decision making," and distractor answers often substitute "intuition-based" or "experience-based" management, which the textbook explicitly positions as what evidence-based management is not.

Worked Example — The Vanishing Developers
A engineering director at a Waterloo software firm has noticed a pattern: every time the company ships a major product launch, two or three senior developers hand in their resignation within the following month. She has tracked this pattern across the last four launches and now budgets for the turnover in advance — but she has never gotten to the bottom of why it happens, and the departures keep recurring. Which goal(s) of OB has she achieved, and which has she not?
PREDICTING — achieved.
  She can reliably anticipate that senior developers will resign
  after a launch. This lets her plan hiring pipelines and stagger
  offboarding, but prediction alone does not tell her whether the
  pattern is fixable.

EXPLAINING — not yet achieved.
  She does not know WHY it happens. Plausible, un-investigated
  causes include: post-launch burnout, a post-launch dip in role
  clarity, competitors poaching visibly successful engineers, or
  senior staff using a shipped launch as a natural resignation
  point. Each cause implies a different fix.

MANAGING — not achieved, and cannot be until explanation improves.
  Because she has not identified the cause, any intervention she
  tries (a retention bonus, a post-launch break policy, an exit-
  interview overhaul) is a guess rather than a solution grounded
  in evidence — exactly what evidence-based management warns
  against. Effective management requires closing the gap between
  predicting and explaining first.
Exam Trap — Predict ≠ Explain MC questions love to describe someone who can reliably anticipate an outcome and then ask whether they have "explained" it. They have not. Predicting only requires a reliable pattern; explaining requires identifying the underlying cause(s), which is harder because causes are often multiple and can change over time. If a scenario says a person "doesn't know why" something happens but can still see it coming, the answer is predicting, not explaining.
Section 4

Early Prescriptions: The Classical Viewpoint and Human Relations

Long before OB became a formal discipline, experienced managers and consultants were writing down prescriptions for the "correct" way to run an organization. The earliest and most influential of these, developed mostly in the early 1900s by practitioners such as Henri Fayol, General Motors executive James D. Mooney, and consultant Lyndall Urwick, became known as the classical viewpoint. It advocated a very high degree of specialization of labour combined with intensive coordination through centralized decision making by upper management — each department minds its own affairs, and coordination across departments flows down from the top.

Frederick Taylor, the father of scientific management, contributed to the classical school but focused specifically on job design and the structure of work on the shop floor rather than the organization as a whole. Taylor rejected informal "rules of thumb" in favour of careful research to determine the optimal degree of specialization and standardization, championed written instructions that clearly defined work procedures, and pushed supervisors to standardize workers' movements and breaks for maximum efficiency. He even extended the logic to supervision itself through "functional foremanship," where different supervisors specialize by function — one trains workers, another disciplines them, and so on.

Max Weber, a German social theorist, gave the classical school its academic backbone by making the term bureaucracy famous as a means of rationally managing complex organizations. Weber's ideal type of bureaucracy has five defining qualities, and this list is worth memorizing verbatim because "which of the following is NOT a quality of bureaucracy" is a near-guaranteed exam question: (1) a strict chain of command in which each member reports to only a single superior; (2) selection and promotion based on impersonal technical skill rather than nepotism or favouritism; (3) a set of detailed rules, regulations, and procedures ensuring the job gets done regardless of who the specific worker is; (4) strict specialization matching duties to technical competence; and (5) centralization of power at the top of the organization. Weber intended bureaucracy as a trade: in exchange for conformity to rules, workers would get security and a fair chance at promotion.

The classical view did not go unchallenged. The human relations movement grew out of the famous Hawthorne studies, conducted in the 1920s and 1930s at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant near Chicago. The studies began as strict industrial-engineering research into how fatigue, rest pauses, and lighting affected productivity, but researchers — including Harvard's Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger, and Hawthorne's own William Dickson — discovered that psychological and social processes, not just physical working conditions, drove productivity and work adjustment. After the Second World War, academics including Chris Argyris, Alvin Gouldner, and Rensis Likert extended this critique of bureaucracy, identifying four specific problems: strict specialization conflicts with human needs for growth and achievement, causing alienation; strong centralization wastes the creative knowledge of lower-level employees, hurting learning and innovation and increasing resistance to change; impersonal rules become a performance ceiling rather than a floor, since the minimum level specified by a rule becomes the accepted norm; and strong specialization causes employees to lose sight of overall organizational goals — the "red-tape mentality," where forms and procedures become ends in themselves.

Classical Viewpoint

High specialization of labour, intensive and centralized coordination, a strict chain of command, detailed rules and procedures, and promotion based on technical merit. Best associated with Taylor (scientific management, shop-floor job design) and Weber (bureaucracy, organization-wide structure).

Human Relations Movement

A people-oriented, more participative and decentralized style, grown out of the Hawthorne studies' discovery that psychological and social processes drive productivity. Advocates open communication, employee participation in decisions, and less rigid control, as a direct critique of bureaucracy's dysfunctions.

Worked Example — Diagnosing a Workplace's Underlying Assumptions
A call-centre floor requires every representative to follow a scripted call flow, log every interaction in a standardized field, and escalate any deviation to a supervisor. Promotion to team lead requires passing a certification exam, not manager nomination. Section 5

Contemporary Management: The Contingency Approach

How was the tension between the classical viewpoint and the human relations movement resolved? Not by declaring a winner. Contemporary scholars recognized merit in both: the classical advocates were right that control and coordination matter for getting organizations to achieve their goals, while the human relationists were right that certain forms of control create real dangers and that flexibility and adaptability matter too. The resolution is the contingency approach: there is no single "one best way" to manage. The appropriate management style depends on the demands of the specific situation.

The textbook's own go-to illustration makes the logic concrete: a payroll department is generally managed more bureaucratically than a research and development department. Running payroll every week is routine work with essentially zero tolerance for error — standardized procedures, close supervision, and a strict chain of command reduce the risk of costly mistakes. R&D work, by contrast, requires creativity that is fostered by a flexible, more autonomous environment; imposing payroll-style rigidity on a team solving novel problems would suppress exactly the initiative the work depends on. The contingency approach therefore does not abandon either viewpoint — it tells you which one to reach for, and when.

Worked Example — One Firm, Two Departments
A mid-sized Canadian accounting firm runs a year-end tax-filing department (high volume, zero tolerance for error, hard statutory deadlines) alongside a new advisory-consulting division (small teams solving novel, one-off client problems). The COO proposes rolling out identical, highly bureaucratic management — detailed procedures, tight supervision, standardized workflows — across both departments. Evaluate the plan.
IDENTIFY THE SITUATION IN EACH DEPARTMENT
  Tax-filing dept  → routine, high-volume, error-intolerant,
                     deadline-driven: resembles the payroll-
                     department example almost exactly.
  Advisory division → novel, one-off problems requiring creative,
                       flexible solutions: resembles R&D.

APPLY THE CONTINGENCY PRINCIPLE
  Tax filing:   centralized coordination, standardized procedures,
                and close supervision FIT the situation — errors
                are costly and the work does not benefit from
                improvisation.
  Advisory:     the same rigid structure would suppress the
                creativity and responsiveness the work depends on,
                exactly the dysfunction the human relations
                critique predicts (lost innovation, red-tape
                mentality, resistance to change).

RECOMMENDATION
  Reject the COO's one-size-fits-all plan. Keep standardized,
  closely supervised workflows for tax filing; give the advisory
  division more autonomy, looser rules, and decentralized
  decision-making. Management style should fit the task's
  routineness and creativity requirements, not be applied
  uniformly firm-wide.
Exam Tip Whenever a question describes two different departments, teams, or firms and asks which management style "should" be used, it is testing the contingency approach. Look for cues about routineness (error tolerance, repetition, deadlines) versus novelty (creativity, one-off problems, changing conditions) to decide which style fits.

Course Handout — Two Week 1 Thinking Tools: S + T = R and the Thermostat

From a Week 1 supplementary handout for this course. Neither tool is in the Johns & Saks textbook, but both are framed as tools to use all term — especially for the in-class group assignments, which pose consulting-style dilemmas.

S + T = R — Situation + Thinking = Response (behaviour). People don’t react to a situation directly; they react to what they think about it. Two managers facing the same situation can behave completely differently because they are carrying different assumptions. Applied to the management schools in this chapter: each school is really a different T — a different set of beliefs about employees — and each produces a different R, a different management style.

SchoolT — what the manager believes about employeesR — how the manager behaves
Classical / traditionalWork is best done when it is tightly specified; employees need clear rules and close control to be productiveNarrow roles, detailed job descriptions, rules and regulations, direct supervision
Human relationsEmployees have social and psychological needs; people who feel recognized and satisfied will be productiveAttention to morale, recognition, participation, and relationships
Human resources (handout only)Employees are capable, self-directed resources whose potential should be developedDelegation, development, responsibility, involvement in decisions
Contingency / systemsThere is no one best way; the right approach depends on the situation, and the organization is a system of interrelated partsDiagnose the situation first, then fit the style to it
Vocabulary Trap — Three Schools or Four? The textbook teaches three approaches: the classical viewpoint, the human relations movement, and the contingency approach (Sections 4–5). The handout lists four, adding a human resources perspective and pairing contingency with a systems view. Those two additions come from general management history, not from Johns & Saks. On a multiple-choice question worded from the textbook, “human resources” is unlikely to be the intended answer for a school of management — and don’t confuse it with human resources management (HRM), the discipline introduced in Section 2.

The handout’s bigger point is that S + T = R is a diagnostic habit for the rest of the course. Whenever a case describes a problem behaviour, ask what the person might be thinking that produces it: What could this employee be thinking that makes them demotivated? Unproductive? Dissatisfied with the job? Nearly every later chapter supplies candidate “T”s — perceptions and attributions (Ch 3), fairness judgments (Ch 4), expectancy and equity beliefs (Ch 5).

Worked Example — S + T = R on a Consulting Dilemma
A retail chain rolls out a new sales-target dashboard. Sales at one store fall, and a previously strong associate, Dev, has started calling in sick. His manager’s response is to add more targets. Use S + T = R to diagnose what is happening, for both Dev and his manager.
S (situation): a new dashboard publicly ranks each associate daily.

Dev
  T: "My numbers depend on which shifts I'm given, not my effort."
     "Getting ranked last in front of everyone is humiliating."
  R: withdrawal - sick days, lower effort.

Manager
  T: "Falling numbers mean people aren't trying hard enough."
     (a classical-style belief about employees)
  R: add more targets and tighter monitoring.

Diagnosis: the manager is changing S again without asking about
Dev's T. The manager's own T drives an R that makes Dev's T worse.

Fix: find out what employees are thinking (ask them). Then change
the situation to address that thinking - for example, adjust for
shift differences and stop the public ranking.

The thermostat analogy is the handout’s four-step model for managing change — for an individual, a team, or a whole organization, and whether you are an internal manager or an outside consultant. A thermostat reads the room, compares it to the setting, acts to close the gap, and checks again:

  1. Current state (the current temperature) — diagnose where the organization is now; the handout recommends a SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats).
  2. Desired state (the thermostat setting) — where do you want to be at a specific future point? For example, the lowest-cost producer, the most innovative team, or the best supply-chain operation.
  3. Close the gap — size the gap between current and desired, then set goals and action steps and carry them out.
  4. Measure and repeat — how close did you get? Then start the four steps again.
Memory Aid — “Read, Set, Close, Check” Read the current temperature (SWOT) → Set the desired temperature → Close the gap (goals and action steps) → Check the result and loop back. It’s a cycle, not a line: the last step feeds the first. Self-test: take any group-assignment scenario and write one sentence for each of the four steps.
Using Both Tools in a Group Assignment The two tools fit together. S + T = R explains why the current state looks the way it does — it’s how you do step 1 properly. The thermostat structures what to do about it. A strong 10-mark answer to a “what should the manager do?” question can follow that order: diagnose the thinking behind the behaviour, name the desired state, propose concrete steps using a chapter concept, and say how you would measure success.
Section 6

What Do Managers Do?

Canadian management theorist Henry Mintzberg conducted an in-depth study of practising managers (research that earned him a PhD from MIT) and found that the day-to-day work of managing does not resemble the tidy "plan, organize, lead, control" textbook description most people expect. Instead, Mintzberg identified ten managerial roles grouped into three categories, and this is the single most testable list in the chapter. The interpersonal roles involve establishing and maintaining relationships: figurehead (symbolic duties, not active decision making — signing documents, entertaining clients), leader (selecting, mentoring, rewarding, and disciplining employees), and liaison (maintaining horizontal contacts inside and outside the organization). The informational roles involve gathering and transmitting information: monitor (scanning the environment for information and trends — attending a conference, for instance), disseminator (passing information already gathered on to people inside the organization), and spokesperson (sending messages outward into the organization's external environment, such as media interviews or shareholder reports). The decisional roles involve using information to make things happen: entrepreneur (turning problems and opportunities into plans for change), disturbance handler (dealing with conflicts and threats to resources or turf), resource allocator (deciding how to deploy time, money, and personnel), and negotiator (conducting major negotiations with other organizations or individuals). Scenario-matching questions are extremely common: "attending a conference to learn industry trends" is the monitor role, not the disseminator role, because gathering information and transmitting it are two different roles even though the same person often does both in sequence.

Worked Example — Tagging a Morning with Mintzberg's Roles
Priya manages a small customer-service team at a Toronto tech startup. In one morning she: fields informal questions from teammates, updates her boss on team morale, steps in to resolve a shouting match between two reps, and decides who gets first pick of upcoming overtime shifts. Tag each activity with a Mintzberg role.
Updating her boss on team morale
  → DISSEMINATOR. She is transmitting information she has
    already gathered from her team to another organizational
    member — an informational role, not a new act of gathering.

Resolving the shouting match between two reps
  → DISTURBANCE HANDLER. This is a decisional role: addressing
    an interpersonal conflict that threatens team functioning.

Deciding who gets first pick of overtime shifts
  → RESOURCE ALLOCATOR. Deploying a scarce resource (overtime
    hours) among employees is a decisional role.

Fielding informal questions from teammates
  → Best read as LIAISON / informal LEADER activity — maintaining
    day-to-day contact and guiding her team, interpersonal roles
    that don't fit neatly into a single decisional bucket.

Conclusion: Priya is playing several roles simultaneously and
somewhat reactively across all three categories in a single
morning — exactly what Mintzberg's research found is typical of
real managerial work, as opposed to the tidy sequential "plan,
then organize, then lead" textbook myth.

Fred Luthans, Richard Hodgetts, and Stuart Rosenkrantz took a different angle, studying what managers actually spend their time on rather than what roles they occupy. They identified four managerial activities: routine communication (formal information exchange and paperwork), traditional management (planning, decision making, controlling), networking (interacting with outsiders plus informal socializing and politicking with insiders), and human resource management (motivating, disciplining, staffing, training). Their most striking finding is a genuine trade-off: managers who spend the most time networking are promoted fastest, but managers who spend more time on HRM activity (and correspondingly less on networking) achieve higher unit effectiveness and employee satisfaction. In other words, the activities that make a manager successful (fast promotion) are not the same as the activities that make a manager effective (a well-run, satisfied team) — a genuinely counterintuitive finding that the exam loves to test in reverse.

John Kotter studied a separate group of successful general managers and found three shared elements in how they operated, known as Kotter's managerial agendas: agenda setting (developing informal, unwritten, people-focused goals for what to accomplish, often before formally starting the role), networking (building a wide formal and informal network of key people inside and outside the organization), and agenda implementation (using that network, plus a wide range of influence tactics from direct orders to subtle storytelling, to get the agenda accomplished). Notice that "networking" appears in both the Luthans framework and the Kotter framework — but they are otherwise distinct three- and four-item lists from different research programs, and merging them into one combined list is a common and costly exam mistake.

Beyond these frameworks, Herbert Simon and Daniel Isenberg studied managerial intuition and found that it is not the opposite of rationality — experienced managers use intuition to sense that a problem exists before it is formally reported, to perform well-learned mental tasks rapidly, to synthesize isolated pieces of information into a coherent picture, and to double-check the results of more formal or mechanical analysis. And Geert Hofstede's cross-cultural research (previewed here, developed further in Chapter 4) observed that national culture shapes how the role of "manager" itself is understood: managers are cultural heroes in individualist North America, but are comparatively downplayed relative to engineers in Germany, group solidarity in Japan, modesty and consensus in the Netherlands, or family control in Taiwan and Singapore. National culture is one of the most important contingency variables in all of OB.

Exam Trap — Successful vs. Effective Managers Luthans's finding is a classic reversed-intuition question: the managers who are promoted fastest (spend the most time networking) are not the same managers who run the most effective, satisfied teams (who spend more time on HRM activity). If a question asks which activity predicts promotion, the answer is networking; if it asks which predicts unit effectiveness and employee satisfaction, the answer is human resource management — do not assume the "best" manager on one dimension is best on both.
Exam Trap — Kotter's Three vs. Mintzberg's Ten Do not merge Mintzberg's ten roles with Kotter's three agenda elements just because both researchers studied managers and both lists include "networking." Mintzberg's ten roles (interpersonal, informational, decisional) describe the kinds of activity managers perform; Kotter's three elements (agenda setting, networking, agenda implementation) describe the process successful general managers use to get their agenda accomplished. They come from different studies and answer different questions.

Section 7

Contemporary Management Concerns

Beyond the classic frameworks above, today's managers navigate five contemporary concerns that show up repeatedly across later chapters and, occasionally, in the news. The first is diversity — local and global, driven by changing demographics within Canada and by globalization pulling organizations into contact with an increasingly wide range of cultures, both inside their workforce and across their markets.

The second is employee health and well-being, a cluster of related ideas. Mindfulness is being highly aware of and attentive to the present moment. Workplace spirituality is a workplace that provides meaning, purpose, community, and connection — note that this is explicitly not about religion. Organizational care reflects an organization's genuine investment in employee welfare. All of these feed into positive organizational behaviour (POB), the study and application of positive human-resource strengths, which rests on psychological capital (PsyCap) — an individual's positive psychological state built on four components: self-efficacy, optimism, hope, and resilience. Closely related is thriving at work, a joint sense of vitality and learning that contributes to both employee health and long-term human sustainability at work.

The third concern is talent management and employee engagement. Talent management refers to an organization's processes for attracting, developing, retaining, and deploying people with the skills it needs. Work engagement — a concept you will revisit in more depth later in the course — is a positive state characterized by three components: vigour, dedication, and absorption.

The fourth concern is alternative work arrangements: remote and hybrid work, gig employment, and — a particularly exam-relevant term — precarious work, meaning work that is risky, uncertain, and unpredictable: unstable, low or unreliable pay, few or no benefits, and little protection. Precarious work disproportionately affects women, immigrants, and racialized workers, and its consequences (job insecurity, financial distress, exposure to unsafe conditions) have become more visible and more widely studied since the pandemic accelerated shifts in how and where work happens.

The fifth concern is corporate social responsibility (CSR) — an organization taking responsibility for the impact of its decisions and actions on its stakeholders. CSR splits into external CSR (community, environment, consumers) and internal CSR (the organization's own workforce), and it connects to the broader framework of ESG (environmental, social, governance) evaluation that investors and job seekers increasingly use to judge an organization. The opening case in your textbook — Klick Health, a Canadian company that donated intubation boxes and PPE during the pandemic, runs a DE&I council, conducts pay-equity reviews, and formally recognizes employees — ties several of these five concerns together in a single real organization, and it is a useful anchor if a question asks you to identify which concern a given practice belongs under.

Editions Shift This List Textbook publishers periodically revise which concerns make the "contemporary" list — some editions fold well-being and engagement together, others add a distinct entry for technology or AI in the workplace. If your instructor's lecture slides present a slightly different five (or six) concerns than the ones listed here, trust the slides for the exam — the underlying ideas (diversity, well-being, talent/engagement, alternative work arrangements, CSR) are stable even when the packaging around them changes between editions.
Exam Trap — Placing a Term Under the Right Concern The five contemporary concerns are commonly tested by giving you a specific term and asking which of the five it belongs under — for example, PsyCap and thriving at work fall under health and well-being, not talent management; precarious work falls under alternative work arrangements, not CSR; and ESG falls under CSR, not diversity. Build a simple two-column flashcard (term → concern) rather than trying to reason it out from first principles under time pressure.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Mintzberg Role Identification

A regional sales manager spends Thursday afternoon giving a media interview about her company's new product line for a local business newspaper. According to Mintzberg, which role is she performing?

Show answer
Spokesperson. Sending information into the organization's external environment — media interviews, stockholder reports — is precisely the spokesperson role. This is easy to confuse with disseminator, but disseminator sends information to people inside the organization; a media interview is by definition external.
Problem 2 — Which Is NOT a Bureaucracy Quality?

Which of the following is NOT one of Weber's five qualities of bureaucracy?
a) A strict chain of command with each member reporting to a single superior
b) Selection and promotion based on impersonal technical skill
c) Frequent job rotation to build generalist skills across departments
d) Centralization of power at the top of the organization

Show answer
(c). Frequent job rotation to build generalists is not one of Weber's five qualities — bureaucracy in fact calls for the opposite: strict specialization matching duties to technical competence. (a), (b), and (d) are three of the five actual qualities (the other two are detailed rules/regulations/procedures, and strict specialization).
Problem 3 — Predict, Explain, or Manage?

A store manager notices that returns spike every year during the first week of January but has never investigated why, and has no plan to change it — she simply schedules extra return-desk staff for that week. Which goal of OB has she achieved, and which has she not?

Show answer
She has achieved predicting (she reliably anticipates the spike) but not explaining (she does not know the underlying cause — post-holiday gift returns, sizing issues, buyer's remorse, or some mix) and therefore not managing in the fuller sense of changing the pattern, since scheduling extra staff responds to the symptom rather than addressing a cause. If the question asks whether she is "managing" the situation, accept that staffing up is a minimal management action, but she has not managed the underlying cause of the returns.
Problem 4 — Theorist Attribution

Match each researcher to their contribution: (1) Fred Luthans and colleagues; (2) John Kotter; (3) Henry Mintzberg; (4) Max Weber.
a) Ten managerial roles grouped into interpersonal, informational, and decisional categories
b) Five qualities of an "ideal type" bureaucracy
c) Successful vs. effective managers — the networking/HRM trade-off
d) Agenda setting, networking, and agenda implementation

Show answer
(1)–(c) Luthans, Hodgetts & Rosenkrantz identified the four managerial activities and the successful-versus-effective trade-off. (2)–(d) Kotter identified the three managerial agenda elements. (3)–(a) Mintzberg identified the ten managerial roles. (4)–(b) Weber defined bureaucracy's five qualities.
Problem 5 — Full Application: One Logistics Company, Two Operations

A Canadian logistics company runs a high-volume parcel-sorting warehouse (repetitive scanning and sorting tasks, strict safety protocols, tight shift schedules, near-zero tolerance for mis-sorted packages) alongside a small in-house design studio that develops new packaging concepts for major retail clients (open-ended creative briefs, changing client requirements, small project teams). Senior leadership is debating whether to manage both operations under one standardized, tightly supervised management system to "keep things simple and consistent" across the company. Using the concepts from this chapter — the classical viewpoint, the human relations critique, and the contingency approach — evaluate this plan and give a recommendation, including what specifically could go wrong if the plan is adopted as proposed.

Show answer
Evaluation: The proposal applies a single classical-viewpoint/bureaucratic management style — standardized procedures, tight supervision, centralized decision making — uniformly across two operations with very different demands. The contingency approach holds that there is no one best way to manage; the appropriate style depends on the situation, so "keeping things simple and consistent" is the wrong design goal on its own.

The warehouse closely resembles the payroll-department example from Section 5: routine, high-volume, safety-critical, and error-intolerant work. A classical, tightly supervised structure with detailed procedures fits well here and is likely to reduce mis-sorts and safety incidents.

The design studio resembles an R&D-type unit: open-ended, creative, client-driven work. Applying the same rigid, closely supervised structure would very likely trigger the human relations movement's predicted dysfunctions — strict specialization and centralized approval processes could stifle the creative risk-taking the studio's work depends on, designers might default to the minimum acceptable standard specified by any imposed rules rather than pushing for the best client outcome, and the studio could lose sight of client goals amid procedural sign-offs (the "red-tape mentality").

Recommendation: Reject uniform standardization. Keep the warehouse's classical, tightly supervised structure largely as is, but give the design studio a more decentralized, human-relations-oriented structure — looser procedures, more autonomy for project teams, and less centralized sign-off — consistent with the contingency principle that management style should track the routineness and creativity demands of the actual work, not a company-wide preference for administrative simplicity.

Chapter Summary

Concept Definition Exam Tip
Organization A social invention accomplishing goals through group effort; built on coordinated people, goal accomplishment, and group effort None of the three features requires size, profit, or assets — a 2-person startup and a hospital both qualify
OB vs. HRM OB is the theory of workplace attitudes/behaviour; HRM is the applied programs and practices built on that theory OB explains WHY HRM practices work (e.g., personality theory underlies selection)
Human vs. Social Capital Human capital = KSAs an individual holds; social capital = value from relationships/networks (internal or external) A skilled employee can still lack internal social capital and struggle to get ideas adopted
Three Goals of OB Predicting, explaining, managing — in increasing order of difficulty Predicting does not require explaining; the ancient-sunset example is the textbook's own analogy
Evidence-Based Management Translating the best scientific evidence into management practice Explicitly contrasted with intuition-based or "quick fix" management — a defined term, not a vague synonym for good judgment
Classical Viewpoint / Bureaucracy High specialization, centralized coordination; Weber's bureaucracy adds 5 specific qualities (chain of command, technical selection, detailed rules, specialization, centralization) "Which is NOT a quality of bureaucracy" is a classic trap — know all 5 verbatim
Human Relations Movement Grew from the Hawthorne studies; critiques bureaucracy as causing alienation, wasted knowledge, minimum-standard performance, and red-tape mentality Don't swap which movement advocated centralization vs. participation
Contingency Approach No one best way to manage; style should fit the situation (routine → classical; creative/uncertain → human relations) Payroll vs. R&D department is the textbook's benchmark comparison — reuse this logic on any "two departments" question
S + T = R (handout) Situation + Thinking = Response: behaviour follows from how a person interprets a situation Each management school is a different T about employees; in cases, ask what the person is thinking
Thermostat Analogy (handout) Four-step change cycle: current state (SWOT) → desired state → close the gap → measure and repeat “Read, Set, Close, Check” — a ready-made structure for group-assignment recommendations
Mintzberg's 10 Roles Interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison); informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson); decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator) The single most testable list in the chapter — drill scenario-matching, not just memorization
Luthans's 4 Activities Routine communication, traditional management, networking, HR management Networking predicts fastest promotion; HRM activity predicts higher unit effectiveness — a genuine trade-off
Kotter's 3 Agenda Elements Agenda setting, networking, agenda implementation Don't merge with Mintzberg's or Luthans's lists just because "networking" appears in more than one
PsyCap / Thriving at Work PsyCap: self-efficacy, optimism, hope, resilience. Thriving at work: joint sense of vitality and learning Both fall under the health and well-being concern, not talent management
