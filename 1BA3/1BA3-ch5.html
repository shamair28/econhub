Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Every chapter you have read so far in this course has been building toward a single question this chapter answers directly: why do people try? Personality (Chapter 2) shapes how someone is disposed to behave. Values and attitudes (Chapter 4) shape how someone evaluates their job and how satisfied and committed they are. But none of that explains why one employee pours discretionary effort into a task while another, with an identical job description and identical pay, does the bare minimum. That is the question of motivation, and it is the psychological engine underneath almost everything else in this course — pay design and job design (Chapter 6), leadership (Chapter 12), team performance (Chapter 8), and decision-making under pressure (Chapter 11) all assume some theory of what makes people direct effort toward organizational goals, whether or not that theory is stated explicitly.

This chapter surveys six named theories of motivation — Maslow’s hierarchy, Alderfer’s ERG theory, McClelland’s theory of needs, self-determination theory, expectancy theory, equity theory, and goal-setting theory (that is actually seven if you count them individually, and the exam will) — each with its own vocabulary, its own originator, and its own components. That combination of breadth and precision is exactly what makes this chapter the single densest block of verbatim-recall material on the midterm: it is not enough to have a fuzzy sense of “Maslow talks about needs”; you need to know that ERG theory’s frustration–regression premise has no equivalent in Maslow, that McClelland’s effective-manager profile is low affiliation and high power, and that expectancy and instrumentality point in opposite directions of the same causal chain. Budget your study time accordingly.

Section 2

What Is Motivation?

Motivation is the extent to which persistent effort is directed toward a goal. That definition packs in four separate, testable properties, and the exam will ask you to identify which one a scenario is describing. Effort is the strength or amount of work-related behaviour a person exhibits — how hard someone is working, in the crudest sense. Persistence is the sustaining of that effort over time; a person who works furiously for twenty minutes and then coasts for the rest of the shift is high on effort but low on persistence. Direction is the channelling of effort toward outcomes that actually benefit the organization — working smart as well as hard, in the informal phrase. And goals are the ultimate object toward which motivated behaviour is aimed. That last property has a genuinely counterintuitive wrinkle worth sitting with: goals can be dysfunctional for the organization and the behaviour aimed at them is still, by definition, motivated. An employee who calls in sick every Monday to extend the weekend, or who deliberately slows production to protest a policy, is exhibiting motivated behaviour in the technical sense of the term — effort is being persistently directed toward a goal, just not one management wants.

The textbook also draws a sharp line between intrinsic motivation, which stems from the direct relationship between the worker and the task itself and is usually self-applied (a sense of achievement, competence, or genuine interest in the work), and extrinsic motivation, which stems from the work environment external to the task and is usually applied by someone else (pay, benefits, supervision, formal policy). Some rewards blur the line — a promotion or a sincere compliment from a respected manager can carry both qualities at once. Under narrow conditions, extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation, but this effect is limited and avoidable, not an inevitability you should assume every time a bonus is introduced. Both types of motivation predict performance, but they predict different things well: extrinsic motivation is more tied to the quantity of performance, while intrinsic motivation is more tied to quality and to performance on complex tasks.

One specific, testable finding worth flagging on its own: the motivation purity bias. Job candidates who openly express interest in extrinsic rewards — pay, benefits, advancement — are perceived, unfairly, as less intrinsically motivated, and as a direct consequence are less likely to be hired, even though wanting to be paid well says essentially nothing about whether someone also finds the work itself interesting. It is a bias worth knowing both as an exam fact and as a caution for your own job search: naming compensation as something you care about does not make you look like a mercenary, whatever the interviewer’s gut reaction might suggest.

Section 3

Motivation and Performance

It is tempting to treat “motivated” and “high-performing” as synonyms, and the textbook is explicit that this is a mistake. Performance is the extent to which an organizational member contributes to achieving the objectives of the organization — and the relationship between motivation and performance is not one-to-one, because several other factors also determine performance. Exhibit 5.1 lays out the full list the textbook holds you responsible for: motivation (itself broken into amount, persistence, and direction of effort), personality, general cognitive ability, emotional intelligence, task understanding, and chance. All six feed into performance; motivation is necessary but never sufficient on its own.

General cognitive ability is a person’s basic information-processing capacities and cognitive resources — verbal, numerical, spatial, and reasoning abilities, the cluster most people mean by “intelligence.” It is usually measured with aptitude tests (the GMAT, LSAT, and MCAT are the test examples the lecture uses as familiar reference points). Cognitive ability predicts learning, training success, and job performance across virtually every job and occupation, and it is a stronger predictor for complex, higher-level jobs that demand more information processing than for simple, routine ones.

A natural follow-up question — which of cognitive ability or motivation matters more for performance — has a specific, testable answer. Intuition suggests a multiplicative model (Performance = Ability × Motivation), which predicts that performance collapses to near zero whenever either factor is low, since a zero anywhere in a product zeroes the whole thing. The research evidence does not support this. Instead, it supports an additive model, in which ability and motivation contribute independently and partially compensate for each other — a highly motivated but only moderately able employee, and a highly able but only moderately motivated one, can land at similar performance levels, because the two factors add rather than multiply.

Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to understand and manage one’s own and others’ feelings and emotions. Peter Salovey and John Mayer, credited with coining the term, developed a four-branch model in which the branches form a hierarchy — each one builds on the one below it. In ascending order: (1) perceiving emotions accurately in oneself and others (the most basic level — reading a facial expression or a tone of voice correctly); (2) using emotions to facilitate thinking (drawing on emotional experience to guide decisions, creativity, and reasoning); (3) understanding emotions, their causes, and the signals they send (knowing, for instance, not to ask a favour of someone who is visibly in a bad mood); and (4) managing emotions in oneself and others to achieve specific goals — stated as the highest branch because it requires having mastered all three below it, for example staying calm while angry or being able to defuse someone else’s anger.

Research on EI finds that it predicts job and academic performance above and beyond both cognitive ability and the Big Five personality traits, and that it is especially predictive in jobs high in emotional labour — police officers and customer service representatives are the textbook’s examples. One further finding is worth remembering precisely because it is counterintuitive: EI matters more for the job performance of employees with lower cognitive ability, and less for employees who are already high in cognitive ability — EI functions partly as a compensating asset rather than simply stacking on top of raw intelligence.

Lecture Supplement — Daniel Goleman’s Emotional Intelligence Competency Model

The content below comes from Dr. Kassaye’s Week 2 lecture slides (slides 3–6), which frame cognitive ability, emotional intelligence, and cultural intelligence together as three abilities relevant to performance, and go beyond the textbook’s four-branch model by introducing Daniel Goleman’s competency framework (slide 5, labelled “Daniel Goleman, 2003 — Emotional Intelligence and Leadership”). The slide’s speaker notes add three points worth knowing verbatim: self-awareness is the starting point and the core of the model; highly effective leaders exhibit a critical mass of strength in at least six competencies overall and at least one per domain; and the four domains are highly connected — self-awareness facilitates both empathy and self-management, and those two in turn help a person build strong relationships.

How the Textbook and the Lecture Differ on EI Johns & Saks present Salovey and Mayer’s four-branch ability model — perceiving, using, understanding, and managing emotions, a hierarchical sequence of mental abilities. The lecture presents Goleman’s competency model — four domains (self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship management) made up of specific, learnable leadership competencies. These are not competing claims about the same fact; they are two different, legitimate models of the same underlying construct, built for different purposes — Salovey and Mayer’s is an ability model aimed at measurement, Goleman’s is a competency model aimed at leadership development. For a multiple-choice question: if it asks about the sequential, hierarchical stages of processing emotion (“which branch is most basic”), answer with the textbook’s four-branch model. If it asks about leadership competencies grouped into self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management, or names Goleman directly, answer with the lecture’s model. The two models sharing the term “emotional intelligence” while listing entirely different components is itself a natural distractor to watch for.
Worked Example — The Brilliant Analyst Who Derails Meetings
Priya is the strongest technical analyst on her team — her financial models are consistently the most accurate in the department. But client meetings involving Priya routinely go badly: she interrupts clients mid-sentence to correct minor inaccuracies, seems visibly irritated when a client asks a question she considers obvious, and has twice made a client so uncomfortable that a colleague had to step in to salvage the meeting.
DIAGNOSE via the four-branch model, lowest to highest:

Branch 1 (Perceiving) -- Priya may not be accurately reading
  the client's discomfort or irritation in real time; if she
  doesn't perceive the negative reaction, she has no signal
  to adjust on.

Branch 2 (Using) -- even if she does notice tension, she may
  not be using that emotional information to guide her
  in-the-moment decisions (e.g., choosing to let a minor
  inaccuracy go rather than correcting it mid-meeting).

Branch 3 (Understanding) -- she may not understand *why*
  interrupting a client to correct small errors reliably
  produces defensiveness and irritation -- the causal link
  between her behaviour and the client's emotional reaction.

Branch 4 (Managing) -- the highest, most visibly broken step:
  she is not managing her own irritation (at "obvious"
  questions) or the client's discomfort, which is the
  branch most directly responsible for the meetings going
  badly.

CONCLUDE: this is a low-EI pattern layered on top of high
general cognitive ability -- exactly the combination the
textbook flags as the one where EI coaching (not more

already more than sufficient for the technical content.
Because the highest branch depends on the lower three,
coaching should start with perceiving and understanding
client reactions in the moment, not jump straight to
"stay calmer," which addresses only Branch 4 in isolation.
Exam Trap — Motivation Is Not the Same As Performance A highly motivated employee with low general cognitive ability, low emotional intelligence, or poor task understanding can still underperform — and a low-performance scenario on an exam is not automatically describing a low-motivation employee. Before you conclude “this person isn’t trying,” check whether the scenario has actually ruled out the other five factors in Exhibit 5.1.

Section 4

Need Theories: Maslow, Alderfer, McClelland, and SDT

Motivation theories split into two families that the exam is likely to test as a direct sorting task. Need theories specify what motivates people — needs and the incentives or goals associated with them, in a chain running from needs to behaviour to incentives and goals. Process theories, covered in Sections 5–7, specify how motivation occurs. Maslow, Alderfer, McClelland, and self-determination theory (SDT) are the four need theories; expectancy theory, equity theory, and goal-setting theory are the three process theories.

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs proposes five levels, from most basic to most advanced: physiological needs (food, water, shelter — met at work by minimum survival pay and working conditions), safety needs (security and stability — met by safe conditions, job security, and insurance), belongingness needs (social interaction and friendship — met by teamwork opportunities and supportive supervision), esteem needs (competence and recognition — met by mastery opportunities, awards, and promotions), and self-actualization needs (developing one’s true potential — met by creative, growth-oriented work). The theory’s motivational premise is that the lowest unsatisfied need has the greatest motivating power, and a satisfied need generally stops motivating — with one explicit exception: Maslow treated self-actualization as a “growth” need that becomes more compelling, not less, as it is fulfilled. One further textbook clarification worth knowing precisely: Maslow never actually presented his theory as a pyramid; that is a popular misconception. A ladder — allowing movement up and down, and partial satisfaction at several levels simultaneously — is a more accurate picture, and research support is weak for the rigid five-level version but fair for a simpler two-level (lower-order/higher-order) split.

Alderfer’s ERG theory compresses Maslow’s five categories into three: existence needs (roughly physiological plus material safety needs), relatedness needs (roughly belongingness plus the esteem needs satisfied through others’ feedback), and growth needs (roughly self-actualization plus the achievement-based parts of esteem). ERG shares one premise with Maslow — the more lower-level needs are gratified, the more higher-level need satisfaction is desired — but adds a second premise that is Alderfer’s real departure from Maslow and the single most commonly tested contrast in this section: the frustration–regression premise, meaning the less a higher-level need is gratified, the more a lower-level need is desired. Maslow has no equivalent claim; for Maslow, a satisfied need simply stops motivating, full stop — it does not reactivate. ERG’s flexibility (needs do not have to activate in a fixed order, and frustration can push a person back down the hierarchy) gives it noticeably better research support than Maslow’s more rigid version.

McClelland’s theory of needs takes a different approach entirely: it is non-hierarchical, and instead asks under what conditions specific needs produce specific, identifiable patterns of motivated behaviour.

Need for Achievement (n Ach)
A strong desire to perform challenging tasks well. Prefers personal responsibility for outcomes, moderately difficult goals involving calculated risk, and regular performance feedback. Best organizational fit: sales, entrepreneurship.
Need for Affiliation (n Aff)
A strong desire to establish and maintain friendly, compatible interpersonal relationships. Avoids conflict and competition; tends to conform to friends’ wishes. Best organizational fit: social work, customer relations.
Need for Power (n Pow)
A strong desire to influence others and make a significant impact or impression. Seeks out influential social settings; comfortable with risk and personal prestige. Best organizational fit: journalism, management.

The single most commonly reversed fact from this section: McClelland found that the most effective managers combine a high need for power directed at organizational goals with a low need for affiliation — not high affiliation, which is the intuitive but wrong answer most students default to. The reasoning is that a manager high in n Aff struggles to make the unpopular calls — discipline, resource allocation, difficult feedback — that the role requires, because those actions threaten the very interpersonal harmony an n Aff-driven person is motivated to protect.

Sitting alongside the need theories, though it does not slot into a hierarchy the way Maslow and Alderfer do, is self-determination theory (SDT). SDT proposes three innate psychological needs — competence (a sense of mastery and effectiveness), autonomy (choice and volition in one’s own behaviour), and relatedness (feeling connected to others) — that SDT treats as universal necessities for psychological well-being, not needs that vary in strength from person to person the way McClelland’s do. SDT’s central contribution is a distinction in the quality, not the quantity, of motivation: autonomous motivation occurs when a person feels in control of their own motivation, is doing a task because they find it interesting, and has chosen to do it — internally regulated. Controlled motivation occurs when a person is pressured, feels they have no real choice, and is acting to obtain a reward, avoid punishment, or because they are being watched — externally regulated, and closely related to (though not a perfect synonym for) extrinsic motivation. When the three basic needs are satisfied, motivation tends to become autonomous; when they are not, motivation tends to become controlled. The key managerial lever SDT identifies is autonomy support — giving employees a genuine rationale for a task, offering real choice rather than control, acknowledging their perspective and feelings, and encouraging initiative — which research shows can be trained into managers and reliably predicts need satisfaction and autonomous motivation.

The need theories converge on two practical managerial implications. Appreciate diversity: the weak support for a single rigid hierarchy means managers cannot assume every employee has the same need profile, and must survey and adapt rather than apply a one-size-fits-all incentive package. Appreciate intrinsic motivation: higher-order needs (however each theory labels them) drive the creative and innovative behaviour organizations depend on, and Alderfer’s frustration–regression finding warns that neglecting those needs can trap an organization in a costly cycle — unmet growth needs push employees back toward demanding more pay and benefits, which are easy for management to grant and which then further crowd out attention to the harder, more valuable work of designing genuinely engaging jobs.

Worked Example — The Star Salesperson Who Flounders as a Manager
Marcus was the top-performing salesperson in his division for three straight years — relentless about hitting individual targets, energized by closing difficult deals, and always asking his manager for a harder territory. He was promoted to sales manager six months ago. Since then, team morale has dropped, two reps have quit, and Marcus admits he finds himself personally closing deals his reps should be handling, because he "just does it faster himself."
DIAGNOSE via McClelland:

Marcus's sales record is a textbook signature of HIGH n Ach --
personal responsibility for outcomes, a preference for
calculated-risk targets, and a craving for direct

are strongly self-oriented: satisfaction comes from
*personally* bettering a performance number, not from
enabling others to do so.

The management role instead requires the profile McClelland
found in effective managers: HIGH n Pow directed at

to direct a team toward collective targets, without needing
either personal credit for each win or to be liked by every
report.

Marcus closing deals himself rather than coaching his reps

doesn't fit: he is optimizing for his own sense of

Exam Trap — Three Reversed-Intuition Pairs in This Section (a) The effective-manager profile is low affiliation and high power — the opposite of what most students guess on first read. (b) Maslow vs. Alderfer: the frustration–regression premise exists only in ERG theory; Maslow holds that a satisfied need simply stops motivating and does not reactivate a lower one. (c) SDT’s autonomous/controlled distinction is not a perfect synonym for intrinsic/extrinsic motivation — the two pairs are closely related and often taught together, but SDT’s framing is specifically about the degree of internal versus external regulation, a slightly different lens that a precisely worded MC question can exploit.

Section 5

Process Theory 1: Expectancy Theory

Where need theories ask what people want, expectancy theory asks how people actually decide where to direct their effort. Developed by psychologist Victor Vroom, the theory holds that motivation is determined by the outcomes people expect to occur as a result of their actions on the job — a fundamentally perceptual, forward-looking model.

The theory has five components. Outcomes are the consequences that may follow work behaviour, split into two levels: first-level outcomes are things like high productivity versus average productivity, or good attendance versus poor attendance — the performance levels themselves. Second-level outcomes are the personally relevant consequences that follow a first-level outcome — pay, a sense of accomplishment, peer acceptance, or even fatigue. Instrumentality is the probability that a given first-level outcome will actually be followed by a given second-level outcome — the performance → outcome link (the textbook’s example: a bank teller who figures the odds are roughly 50:50, instrumentality = 0.5, that a good performance rating will lead to a pay raise). Valence is the expected value of an outcome — how attractive or unattractive it is to that particular individual. Expectancy is the probability that the person can actually achieve a given first-level outcome — the effort → performance link (the textbook’s example: a machinist who is certain, expectancy = 1.0, that she can hit an average output of 15 units a day, but less certain, expectancy = 0.6, that she can reach a high output of 20 units a day). And force is the end product — the resulting degree of effort actually directed toward a given first-level outcome.

The formula that ties it together: Force = ∑(Valence × Instrumentality) × Expectancy. In plain language: people are motivated to perform activities they find attractive and that they feel they can actually accomplish. Because expectancy multiplies the whole expression, a highly valent, strongly instrumental outcome still produces zero motivational force if expectancy is close to zero — no amount of attractiveness compensates for believing you simply cannot hit the target. Everything in the theory is explicitly perceptual: two employees in the identical job can attach different valences to the same reward, estimate different instrumentalities, and hold different expectancies, and will therefore show entirely different motivation patterns even though nothing about the job itself has changed.

The theory carries three clear managerial levers. Boost expectancies — training, clear procedures, and proper tools raise a person’s belief that effort will actually translate into the required performance level. Clarify reward contingencies — make instrumentalities explicit and, critically, honour them, since a workforce that stops believing performance leads to reward will stop directing effort toward it. Appreciate diverse valences — individualize the reward package, since a reward that is highly valent to one employee (public recognition, say) may be near-neutral or even mildly negative to another.

Worked Example — A Numeric Force Calculation
A junior analyst is deciding how hard to push for the "high productivity" performance level this quarter rather than settling for "average." She estimates: valence of the pay raise tied to high productivity = 8/10, instrumentality (high productivity → raise) = 0.7; valence of peer recognition = 5/10, instrumentality (high productivity → recognition) = 0.9. Her expectancy that she can actually reach the high-productivity bar, given her workload, is 0.4.
STEP 1 -- valence of the first-level outcome
  = (Valence_pay x Instr_pay) + (Valence_recognition x Instr_recognition)
  = (8 x 0.7) + (5 x 0.9)
  = 5.6 + 4.5
  = 10.1

STEP 2 -- apply expectancy
  Force = 10.1 x 0.4 = 4.04

COMPARE -- if she instead targeted "average productivity,"
  with lower valence attached (say a combined 4.0) but a much
  higher expectancy (0.95, since it's well within her
  current capacity):
  Force = 4.0 x 0.95 = 3.8

CONCLUDE: the two forces are close (4.04 vs. 3.8) despite the
"high productivity" path offering much more attractive
outcomes on paper -- because her low expectancy (0.4) for
that path drags its force back down toward the safer option.
A manager who wants her pushing harder for the high bar needs
to raise her expectancy (more support, training, or workload
relief), not simply raise the reward -- increasing valence or
instrumentality on the high-productivity path will move the
force number less than raising expectancy would, because
expectancy applies to the *entire* combined valence total,
not to one term in it.
Worked Example — Zero Expectancy, Zero Motivation
A retail associate has just been told about a new incentive: whoever achieves the highest customer satisfaction score this month wins a paid trip. He personally finds the trip extremely appealing (valence is very high) and fully believes management will honour the prize (instrumentality is high). But he also knows the store’s satisfaction-score system is effectively broken for his shift — the survey link only reaches customers who check out before 2 p.m., and he is scheduled exclusively for closing shifts. He is certain, no matter how hard he tries, that his recorded score will not reflect his actual performance.
APPLY the force formula:
  Valence = very high
  Instrumentality = high
  Expectancy (effort -> first-level outcome of a high
    RECORDED score) =~ 0

  Force = (Valence x Instrumentality) x Expectancy
        = (high x high) x ~0
        =~ 0

CONCLUDE: no matter how attractive the trip is or how much he
trusts management to award it, his motivation toward the
incentive collapses to roughly zero, because the expectancy
term -- his belief that effort can even produce the required
first-level outcome -- is broken by a measurement problem,
not a reward problem. The fix is not a bigger prize; it's
fixing the survey system so his actual performance can
register as a high score at all. This is the practical
version of the theory's core warning: don't diagnose a
"low reward" problem when the real fault is in the
effort-to-performance link.
Exam Trap — Expectancy vs. Instrumentality This is the most frequently reversed pair in the chapter. Expectancy is the effort → performance link (“can I actually do it?”). Instrumentality is the performance → outcome link (“if I do it, will I actually get the reward?”). Practice translating word problems into E, I, and V before the exam, and remember that a zero anywhere in the force product — including a near-zero expectancy — zeroes the entire result, regardless of how large the other terms are.

Section 6

Process Theory 2: Equity Theory

Chapter 4 introduced equity theory to explain job satisfaction; here, the same theory reappears as an account of motivation. The mechanism is the same ratio comparison — a worker compares their own outcome/input ratio to that of a relevant comparison person or group — but the motivational claim goes further than the satisfaction claim: inequity is unpleasant and produces tension, and people are motivated to expend real energy reducing it, not just to feel dissatisfied and stop there.

Psychologist J. Stacey Adams identified five tactics people use to restore equity, and the exam expects you to know all five and to sort them into the two categories the textbook distinguishes: the first three are essentially psychological or perceptual, and the last two are overtly behavioural.

Perceptual Distortion (Psychological)
Distort perceptions of one’s own inputs or outcomes, or distort perceptions of the comparison person’s inputs or outcomes — without anything about the actual situation changing.
Change the Comparison (Psychological)
Choose a different, more favourable comparison person or group, resolving the perceived inequity by changing the benchmark rather than the ratio itself.
Alter Inputs/Outcomes, or Leave (Behavioural)
Actually change one’s own inputs (work less hard) or outcomes (seek a raise), or exit the relationship altogether (quit) — the two overtly behavioural responses.

Research support is well established for underpayment inequity: workers who are underpaid on an hourly basis tend to lower their inputs by producing less; workers underpaid on a piece-rate basis tend to raise a high volume of low-quality output instead, since that raises their outcomes without requiring more effort per unit; and underpayment is also linked to higher resignation rates, as some underpaid workers seek equity elsewhere. Predictions about overpayment inequity are much more weakly supported — either people simply tolerate being overpaid more readily than being underpaid, or they resolve the discomfort through perceptual distortion rather than through the behavioural routes the theory predicts.

One specific factual detail the Exam Focus flags as easy to overlook: the gender and equity finding. Both men and women show some tendency to choose same-sex comparison others when judging the fairness of what they receive. This matters beyond the abstract theory — it offers a partial explanation for the persistence of gender pay gaps, since if women’s equity comparisons are restricted to other (frequently lower-paid) women, the motivation to notice and correct a broader, cross-gender wage inequity is correspondingly reduced.

The managerial implication is straightforward to state and harder to execute: perceived underpayment produces a cluster of real organizational costs — low productivity, low quality, theft, turnover — while simply overpaying people to solve morale problems (disguised bribery, in effect) does not reliably produce the intended motivational boost. Because equity is a perceptual, socially constructed comparison — the employee, not the organization, controls which inputs, outcomes, and comparison people count as relevant — managers must actually understand which comparisons their employees are making, rather than assuming pay alone settles the question.

Section 7

Process Theory 3: Goal-Setting Theory

Developed by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, goal-setting theory holds that goals are most motivational when they are specific and challenging, when the individual is genuinely committed to them, and when feedback about progress is provided along the way. A specific goal states an exact level of achievement within a particular time frame — “increase net sales by 20 percent this quarter” qualifies; “I will do my best” does not, because both the achievement level and the time frame are vague. Challenge is more personal than specificity, since it depends on the individual’s existing skill and experience — a goal perceived as genuinely impossible loses its power to motivate, so challenge is best pegged to the person’s current competence and raised as they master the task (a practical version: base a new salesperson’s target on a smaller percentage increase than an experienced one’s). Commitment means the person actually accepts the goal and intends to work toward it — a goal nobody has bought into is not functionally a goal at all, however specific and challenging it looks on paper. Feedback lets the person compare current performance against the goal as they go, and is most useful when it is accurate, specific, credible, and timely.

These four characteristics work through four distinct mechanisms, another verbatim-recall favourite: goals (1) direct attention toward goal-relevant activities, (2) increase effort, (3) increase and prolong persistence, and (4) prompt the discovery and use of task-relevant strategies for reaching the goal.

Goal commitment itself is shaped by three factors. Participation in setting the goal helps mainly when trust between employee and supervisor is low, or when the employee has information needed to set a realistic target — when trust and understanding of capability are already high, participation adds little. Rewards matter less than intuition suggests; commitment is largely independent of monetary incentives. Management support matters a great deal — coercive pressure to hit a goal badly damages commitment, while a supervisor who is genuinely supportive if failure occurs (including, when warranted, adjusting an unrealistic goal downward) protects it.

Goal orientation is a stable individual difference in the kind of goal a person prefers in achievement situations, and research has found a clear, asymmetric pattern across its three types. A learning goal orientation (focused on acquiring new skills and mastering new situations) is positively related to both learning and performance. A performance-avoid goal orientation (focused on avoiding negative judgments about one’s performance) is negatively related to both. A performance-prove goal orientation (focused on demonstrating competence and earning favourable judgments) shows no consistent relationship to either outcome — the “null result” of the three, and a natural target for a “which of the following is NOT related to performance” question.

Goal proximity distinguishes distal goals (long-term, end-state goals, like a full-year sales target) from proximal goals (short-term sub-goals instrumental to reaching a distal goal). Proximal goals matter most on novel or complex tasks, because breaking a distal goal into smaller sub-goals generates far more frequent feedback, letting a person evaluate their progress and adjust strategy along the way — something a distant, far-off goal cannot provide on its own. Pairing proximal milestones with a distal goal is the most effective strategy specifically when someone is learning something new; on tasks people already know how to do well, a specific, challenging performance goal on its own works fine.

The clearest managerial implication of goal-setting theory previews next chapter’s content directly: Management by Objectives (MBO) is essentially an organization-wide system for applying specific, challenging, jointly committed-to goals with regular feedback — Chapter 6 covers it as a formal motivational technique built on exactly the mechanisms described here.

Worked Example — Fixing a Vague Team Goal
A department head tells her team, "Let's improve customer service this quarter." Three months later, nobody can say whether it worked, and the team’s day-to-day behaviour hasn’t noticeably changed.
DIAGNOSE the original goal against the four
characteristics:
  Specific?     NO -- no exact achievement level, no metric
  Challenging?  UNKNOWN -- can't assess challenge without a
                target to compare against current performance
  Commitment?   WEAK -- vague goals are hard to genuinely
                commit to because "success" is undefined
  Feedback?     NONE POSSIBLE -- with no metric, there is
                nothing concrete to give feedback against

REWRITE using goal-setting theory:
  DISTAL GOAL (this quarter): raise the customer
  satisfaction survey score from 72% to 85%, and cut average
  ticket-resolution time from 48 hours to 24 hours.

  PROXIMAL MILESTONES (novel initiative for this team --
  proximal goals apply):
    Week 2:  every rep completes the new de-escalation
             training module
    Week 6:  satisfaction score checkpoint -- target 78%
    Week 10: resolution-time checkpoint -- target 32 hours
    Week 13: final measurement against the 85% / 24-hour
             distal targets

CONCLUDE: the rewritten goal is specific (exact numbers,
exact time frame), challenging but achievable (large but not
impossible jumps), reinforced by proximal milestones that
generate feedback every few weeks instead of one verdict at
the end, and -- because it was built with the team rather
than handed down -- has a real shot at commitment. The
proximal structure matters especially here because
"improving customer service" via a new de-escalation
approach is a relatively novel task for this team, which is
exactly the condition under which proximal milestones add
the most value.
Exam Trap — Goal Orientation and “Do Your Best” Goals Performance-prove orientation is the one with no consistent relationship to learning or performance — a common trap on “which of the following is NOT” questions, since it is easy to assume all three orientations behave similarly. Separately, remember that vague “do your best” goals are reliably less effective than specific, challenging goals — a comparison the exam likes to phrase as a trick question implying vague goals are somehow safer or more flexible.

Section 8

Across Cultures, and Putting It Together

Every theory in this chapter was developed and tested primarily in individualist, North American settings, and each carries assumptions that do not automatically transfer elsewhere. The need theories built on a social-to-growth progression — Maslow and Alderfer alike — implicitly treat individual growth and self-actualization as the “top” of the hierarchy, an assumption that does not hold in more collectivist cultures where group belonging can outrank individual achievement even at higher levels of need satisfaction. Self-determination theory fares better cross-culturally: its three basic needs (competence, autonomy, relatedness) have been found to matter across a wide range of cultures, even though their relative importance varies. Equity theory’s fairness norm is itself culturally contingent — collectivist cultures often favour equality-based reward allocation (splitting outcomes evenly) over the equity-based allocation (splitting outcomes proportional to input) that the North American version of the theory assumes, meaning a reward system that looks perfectly equitable by North American standards can register as unfair elsewhere. Expectancy theory, because it is built entirely from an individual’s own perceptions, is inherently flexible across cultures — nothing in its structure assumes any particular cultural value. Goal-setting theory generalizes reasonably well in its broad strokes, but implementation needs real adaptation: individual goals fit poorly in collectivist cultures, where group goals should generally be substituted; large-power-distance cultures expect goals to be assigned by superiors rather than set participatively; and specific, difficult goals can trigger face-saving concerns in cultures where a visible, public failure carries a much higher social cost.

With all six theories on the table, the textbook closes the chapter by showing how they fit together into a single integrative model of motivation theories (Exhibit 5.7), which also ties this chapter back to Chapter 4’s discussion of job satisfaction. Expectancy and instrumentality perceptions (Box 1) and goal-setting characteristics — specific, challenging, committed-to, and fed back on (Box 2) — both feed directly into motivation itself (Box 3: effort, persistence, and direction, whether autonomous or controlled). Motivation then combines with the intervening factors from Exhibit 5.1 — general cognitive ability, emotional intelligence, personality, task understanding, and chance (Box 4) — to produce actual performance (Box 5). Performance in turn generates rewards, both intrinsic and extrinsic (Box 6); the connection between performance and intrinsic rewards is strong and reliable because those rewards are self-administered (a nurse who helps a patient recover feels competence directly, with no manager involved), while the connection to extrinsic rewards is far less reliable, since it depends on management actually acting on it. Those rewards feed back into motivation (closing the loop to Box 3) and also become the raw material for equity perceptions (Box 7), which in turn affect both motivation and job satisfaction (Box 8) — the same equity-to-satisfaction link Chapter 4 established, now shown as one part of a larger, continuously feeding-back system rather than an isolated finding.

Worked Example — An Individual Bonus Plan in a Collectivist Subsidiary
A Canadian head office rolls out a new individual performance bonus — the top 10% of individual performers in each department receive a significant cash bonus — across all its subsidiaries, including a manufacturing plant in a strongly collectivist national culture. Head office is surprised when productivity at that plant does not rise and morale visibly drops.
DIAGNOSE using the cross-cultural limits above:

1. EQUITY / ALLOCATION NORM MISMATCH
   The plan assumes an equity-based allocation norm --
   rewards proportional to individual input -- which is the
   North American default. In a collectivist culture, workers
   are more likely to expect an equality-based norm (rewards
   split evenly, or at least visibly shared), so singling out
   a top 10% for individual reward can register as unfair
   rather than motivating, regardless of how "accurately" it
   measures individual output.

2. INDIVIDUAL GOAL IN A COLLECTIVIST SETTING
   Even setting equity aside, an individual competitive
   bonus is itself a poor cultural fit -- goal-setting theory's
   cross-cultural guidance is that individual goals are not
   likely to be accepted or motivational in collectivist
   cultures, where group-level goals generally work better.

3. LIKELY SOCIAL COST
   Being publicly identified as a top-10% individual
   performer, ahead of one's group, may itself carry a social
   cost in a culture where group harmony and belonging are

   avoid standing out, which would explain productivity not
   rising even though a real cash incentive is on the table.

team-level bonus tied to group performance targets, consistent

Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Name the Need Theory

An employee tells her manager, "I liked this job fine when I started, but lately even though my pay and benefits haven't changed, I find myself wanting more say in how I do my work and more chances to actually develop new skills." Which need theory's core premise does this best illustrate, and which specific level or category is she describing?

Show answer
This best illustrates Maslow's hierarchy of needs (or, just as validly, Alderfer's ERG theory): her lower-level needs — pay and benefits, which correspond to physiological/safety needs in Maslow or existence needs in ERG — are already satisfied and, per the theory, have stopped motivating her. Her attention has shifted upward to esteem and self-actualization needs (Maslow) or growth needs (Alderfer) — wanting more autonomy in how she works and opportunities to develop new skills are classic organizational expressions of these higher-order needs. If the question specifies Alderfer, the correct label is "growth needs"; if it specifies Maslow, "esteem and self-actualization." A good answer names which theory's vocabulary the question is using rather than mixing the two.
Problem 2 — Which Is NOT

Which of the following is NOT one of the five tactics Adams identified for restoring equity?

a) Perceptually distorting the comparison person's outcomes   b) Requesting a formal performance review from HR   c) Altering one's own inputs   d) Leaving the exchange relationship

Show answer
(b) Requesting a formal performance review from HR is not one of Adams's five tactics. The actual five are: perceptually distorting one's own inputs or outcomes; perceptually distorting the comparison person's inputs or outcomes; choosing a different comparison person or group; altering one's own inputs or outcomes; and leaving the relationship. (b) is a plausible-sounding organizational action, which is exactly why it works as a distractor, but it isn't one of the theory's named tactics — requesting a review doesn't itself change inputs, outcomes, or the comparison being made.
Problem 3 — Compare Two Concepts

A colleague says, "Expectancy theory and goal-setting theory are basically the same idea — they're both just about setting a target and trying to hit it." Explain what's wrong with this claim, using the specific components each theory actually relies on.

Show answer
The claim conflates two theories that explain motivation through fundamentally different mechanisms. Goal-setting theory is about the properties of the target itself — whether a goal is specific, challenging, committed to, and accompanied by feedback — and says nothing formally about whether the person believes they can achieve it or whether achieving it will be rewarded. Expectancy theory is about the person's perceptions surrounding a target — expectancy (can I actually achieve this performance level?), instrumentality (will achieving it actually lead to a reward I care about?), and valence (do I actually want that reward?) — and says nothing formally about whether the target itself is specific or challenging. A goal can be perfectly specific and challenging (satisfying goal-setting theory) while the person has near-zero expectancy of reaching it (collapsing force to zero under expectancy theory) — the two theories are complementary, not interchangeable, and a full diagnosis of a motivation problem often needs both.
Problem 4 — Theorist and Profile Attribution

Match each finding to its theory: (a) the most effective managers combine low n Aff with high n Pow directed at organizational goals   (b) autonomy support from a supervisor is the key predictor of autonomous motivation. Theories: (i) McClelland's theory of needs   (ii) Self-determination theory

Show answer
(a) The effective-manager profile (low n Aff, high n Pow directed at organizational goals) → (i) McClelland's theory of needs. (b) Autonomy support as the key predictor of autonomous motivation → (ii) Self-determination theory. A common distractor swaps these, or attributes the autonomy-support finding to McClelland because "autonomy" sounds similar to n Ach's emphasis on personal responsibility — but n Ach is about a person's own need for challenging, self-attributable tasks, while SDT's autonomy is about a psychological need for choice and volition that a manager can actively support through their own behaviour.
Problem 5 — Application: A Call Centre With High Absenteeism Despite a Generous Bonus Plan

A call centre introduces a generous individual bonus plan tied to monthly call-resolution targets, expecting it to fix a long-standing absenteeism and low-effort problem. Six months later, absenteeism is unchanged and supervisors report reps "going through the motions" on calls. Exit interviews reveal a common theme: the call-routing software frequently assigns the most complex, time-consuming cases to specific reps regardless of skill level, making the monthly target feel arbitrary and, in many reps' words, "basically impossible some months no matter how hard you work." Reps also say they were never consulted on how the targets were set. Using expectancy theory and goal-setting theory, diagnose the problem and propose fixes.

Show answer
Diagnosis — expectancy theory: The bonus plan is almost certainly high on valence (a generous bonus is attractive) and plausibly high on instrumentality (reps likely believe management will pay out if the target is hit). The breakdown is in expectancy — the effort → performance link. Because case assignment is uneven and effectively random, many reps cannot reliably control whether they can even reach the call-resolution target in a given month, regardless of effort. Per the force formula, Force = Σ(Valence × Instrumentality) × Expectancy, a near-zero expectancy collapses total force toward zero even with a highly valent, credible reward sitting on the other side of the equation — which explains why a "generous" bonus is not moving behaviour.

Diagnosis — goal-setting theory: The target may be specific, but it fails the challenge criterion in its current form — goals that feel impossible lose their motivational power, and a target that varies wildly in difficulty based on an arbitrary case-assignment system is not calibrated to individual competence the way effective goal-setting requires. The target is also weak on commitment: reps report never being consulted on how it was set, and while participation is not always necessary for commitment, it becomes important precisely when trust is low or when employees have information (here, about how unevenly cases are actually assigned) that management lacks. The absence of meaningful feedback tied to the case-difficulty problem compounds this — reps have no accurate way to interpret a missed target as "the system, not me."

Recommendations: First, fix the expectancy problem directly — adjust the routing software to distribute complex cases evenly, or weight the resolution target by case-difficulty so a hard month doesn't automatically produce a missed goal; this restores the effort-performance link the whole incentive depends on. Second, apply goal-setting theory properly: recalibrate targets to individual and monthly case-mix reality (challenge without impossibility), and bring reps into the target-setting process given the low trust and real information asymmetry exit interviews revealed. Third, until the assignment system is fixed, consider shorter-cycle proximal targets with more frequent feedback checkpoints rather than a single monthly cliff-edge target, so a difficult stretch of assignments doesn't erase an entire month's effort in the reps' eyes. No amount of increasing the bonus itself (raising valence) will fix this, because the diagnosis points squarely at expectancy and goal calibration, not reward size.
