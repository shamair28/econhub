Introduction & History

0 of 12 marked as known

1

What is OB?

Model answer

Organizational behaviour (OB) is the study of the attitudes and behaviours of individuals and groups in organizations. It tries to understand, predict, explain and manage how people behave at work.

  • Organizations are social inventions for accomplishing common goals through group effort. OB looks at them at three levels: individuals pursuing goals, interacting teams/groups/departments, and the whole organization.
  • The text gives three goals: predict, explain and manage. Explaining is harder than predicting because a behaviour can have several causes that shift over time.
  • Why study it: it is interesting, important and makes a difference. It draws on human capital and social capital.
  • Closely related field: human resource management (HRM).

Exam trap: The slide says OB tries to understand, predict, explain and manage; the textbook's three goals are predict, explain, manage. Do not confuse OB with HRM, the programs and practices for acquiring, developing, motivating and retaining employees.

Memory aid: P → E → M climbs in difficulty: Predict, Explain, Manage.

2

What is Traditional/Classical managerial thinking in general?

Model answer

The classical viewpoint is the early-1900s prescription for how to manage. Key writers in the text: Henri Fayol, James Mooney and Lyndall Urwick.

  • A very high degree of specialization of labour and a very high degree of coordination.
  • Each department tends to its own affairs; decision making is centralized at the top, and that provides the coordination.
  • Two main branches: scientific management (Taylor, job design) and bureaucracy (Weber, whole-organization structure).

Handout (S + T = R): the classical manager's thinking is that employees need tight control, very specific job descriptions, narrowly defined roles, and rules and regulations to follow. That thinking produces a rule-driven management style.

Exam trap: Classical = specialization, standardization and centralization. "Participative" or "decentralized" options belong to human relations / human resources, not classical.

Ch 1 §1.4 · Week 1 slide 14 · Week 1 handout (S + T = R)

3

What is Bureaucracy?

Model answer

Bureaucracy is Max Weber's "ideal type" of organization for rationally managing complex organizations. Its qualities:

  • A strict chain of command: each member reports to a single superior.
  • Selection and promotion based on impersonal technical skills (merit), not nepotism or favouritism.
  • Detailed rules, regulations and procedures, so the job gets done whoever the worker is.
  • Strict specialization matching duties to technical competence.
  • Centralization of power at the top.

It standardizes behaviour and gives workers security and a fair chance of promotion in exchange for conformity.

Exam trap: "Which is NOT a quality of bureaucracy?" Distractors are decentralized decision making, participative management, or hiring on personal connections. Functional foremanship is Taylor's (scientific management), not Weber's.

Memory aid: "Can Smart Rules Stop Chaos?": Chain of command, Skill-based selection, Rules, Specialization, Centralization.

4

What is Scientific Management?

Model answer

Scientific management is Frederick Taylor's system for using research to determine the optimum degree of specialization and standardization of work tasks. Taylor was mainly concerned with job design and the structure of work on the shop floor.

  • Careful research instead of informal "rules of thumb".
  • Written instructions that clearly define work procedures (the slide: clearly defined work responsibility).
  • Supervisors standardize workers' movements and breaks for maximum efficiency.
  • Functional foremanship: supervisors specialize in particular functions (one trains workers, another disciplines).

Exam trap: The slide says "equality between management and workers"; the textbook does not use that wording. Know the text's version (research, specialization and standardization, written instructions, functional foremanship). Taylor = job design; Weber = whole-organization structure.

Ch 1 §1.4 · Week 1 slide 14

5

What was the Human Relations movement critical of, and why?

Model answer

The human relations movement was a critique of classical management and bureaucracy. It called attention to their dysfunctional aspects and advocated more people-oriented, participative styles that cater to employees' social and psychological needs. Its four specific complaints:

  1. Strict specialization is incompatible with human needs for growth and achievement, so it leads to alienation.
  2. Strong centralization wastes the creative ideas of lower-level members, so the organization fails to learn from mistakes, innovation suffers and resistance to change follows.
  3. Strict, impersonal rules make people settle for the minimum acceptable performance (e.g., eight claims a day becomes the norm).
  4. Strong specialization makes people lose sight of overall goals: the "red-tape mentality".

Memory aid: A-W-C-R: "Alienated Workers Can't Rise": Alienation, Wasted knowledge, Ceiling on performance, Red tape.

6

What did the Hawthorne Studies illustrate?

Model answer

The Hawthorne studies were research conducted in the 1920s and 1930s at the Hawthorne plant of Western Electric, near Chicago. They illustrated how psychological and social processes affect productivity and work adjustment.

  • They began in the tradition of industrial engineering, studying the effects of fatigue, rest pauses and lighting on productivity.
  • The researchers (Harvard's Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger, and Hawthorne's William Dickson) noticed psychological and social effects instead.
  • One sign: strong informal group norms that limited productivity below what management wanted (resistance to management).
  • This started the human relations movement.

Exam trap: Slide 16 is titled "Hawthorne Effect" (a video link), but neither the slides nor the text define it. The textbook's takeaway is that psychological and social processes matter, not just physical conditions like lighting.

7

What did Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs teach us?

Model answer

Week 1 lists Maslow's hierarchy of needs under the human relations movement. The textbook covers it in Ch 5: humans have five sets of needs arranged in a hierarchy, from most basic to highest.

  1. Physiological
  2. Safety
  3. Belongingness
  4. Esteem
  5. Self-actualization
  • The motivational premise: the lowest-level unsatisfied need has the greatest motivating potential. No need is the "best" motivator.
  • For managers: employees are motivated by social and psychological needs, not only pay and safety (belongingness: teamwork and supportive supervision; esteem: mastery and recognition; self-actualization: creative, growth jobs). That fits the human relations view.

Exam trap: The textbook says the pyramid picture is a misconception; Maslow never presented it that way, and a ladder is more accurate (people can be partially satisfied at several levels at once).

Memory aid: "Please Stop Being Egotistical, Sam": Physiological, Safety, Belongingness, Esteem, Self-actualization.

10

What does the Contingency Approach to management suggest?

Model answer

The contingency approach recognizes that there is no one best way to manage; an appropriate management style depends on the demands of the situation. The slides call it the Contingency/Systems approach.

  • It resolves the tension between classical and human relations: both have merit (classical: control and coordination; human relations: flexibility and adaptability).
  • Example: manage a payroll department more bureaucratically than a research and development department. Payroll is routine with no margin for error; research needs creativity fostered by a flexible environment.
  • The general answer to most OB questions is "it depends"; the text is not a "cookbook" of quick fixes.

Memory aid: "It depends" = contingency. Routine, error-intolerant work gets bureaucratic management; creative work gets flexible management.

11

What do Mintzberg’s managerial roles, Luthan’s Managerial Activities, Kotter’s Managerial Agendas, and Simon & Isenberg’s Managerial Minds, teach us about the kinds of core competencies needed by managers/leaders today?

Model answer

Together they show that managers need people skills and thinking skills, not just planning and controlling.

  • Mintzberg's managerial roles: 10 roles in 3 groups. Interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator). Competencies: leadership, communication, negotiation, and balancing different roles for different audiences.
  • Luthans' managerial activities: routine communication, traditional management, networking, human resource management. All involve dealing with people. Fast-promoted managers network more; managers with effective units and satisfied employees spend more time on HRM.
  • Kotter's managerial agendas: agenda setting, networking, agenda implementation. High informal interaction and concern with people issues; influence tactics from direct orders to stories.
  • Simon and Isenberg's managerial minds: intuition, built on experience and not the opposite of rationality. The slide lists intuition, situational sensing, rapid mental processing/analysis, synthesizing, and analyses (data analytics).

Exam trap: Networking appears in both Luthans and Kotter; the other items differ. Functional foremanship is Taylor's. Intuition is not "unanalytical".

Memory aid: Mintzberg roles are 3 + 3 + 4. Monitor brings information IN, Disseminator passes it AROUND, Spokesperson sends it OUT.

Abilities, Personality, Values & Attitudes

0 of 12 marked as known

1

What are the three top abilities? What is the difference between ability and personality?

Model answer

The lecture's three abilities are three kinds of intelligence:

  • Cognitive ability (general cognitive ability, GCA): the capacity to learn and process cognitive information (reading, comprehension, mathematical and spatial patterns). Tests: GMAT, LSAT, MCAT.
  • Emotional intelligence (EI): the ability to accurately identify emotions in self and others, and to understand and manage them.
  • Cultural intelligence (CQ): a person's capability to function effectively in situations characterized by cultural diversity.

Ability vs personality: ability is what a person is capable of. Personality is the relatively stable set of psychological characteristics that influences the way an individual interacts (thinks, acts, behaves) with their environment, and it can include values and motives.

Exam trap: The textbook's Ch 5 covers only two forms of intelligence, general cognitive ability and emotional intelligence. Cultural intelligence is in Ch 4 (knowledge, motivation, behaviour); the lecture adds CQ-Strategy for four sub-skills.

Ch 5 §5.2 · Ch 4 §4.1 (CQ) · Week 2 slides 2–8

2

What is personality? In what situations does personality have the greatest impact?

Model answer

Personality is the relatively stable set of psychological characteristics that influences how a person interacts with their environment: how they think, act and behave.

Three approaches to explain behaviour (slide 2): dispositional (stable traits), situational (the setting) and interactionist (both). The interactionist approach is the most widely accepted: behaviour is a function of both dispositions and the situation.

  • Personality has the greatest impact in weak situations: roles are loosely defined, there are few rules, and reward and punishment contingencies are weak (e.g., a newly formed volunteer community organization).
  • In strong situations (defined roles, rules, contingencies; e.g., routine military operations) personality has less impact.
  • Trait activation theory: a trait leads to behaviour only when the situation makes that trait salient.

4

What are core values?

Model answer

An individual's value system: a broad tendency to prefer certain states of affairs over others (the text's definition of values).

  • Enduring beliefs about what is most important in the world; non-negotiable, deeply held beliefs (slide 14).
  • They are motivational (what we seek or avoid) and say how we should behave.
  • "Broad" means they predict behaviour in specific situations poorly.
  • Learned mainly through reinforcement from parents, teachers and religious figures.
  • People differ in the degree to which they hold each value (e.g., achievement, power, autonomy, conformity, tradition, social welfare).
  • Values awareness helps determine fit with others in jobs, occupations and organizations (person–organization fit).

5

What are Hofstede’s Cultural Values?

Model answer

Geert Hofstede surveyed more than 116,000 IBM employees in 40 countries about work-related values. His dimensions:

  • Power distance: the extent to which an unequal distribution of power is accepted.
  • Uncertainty avoidance: the extent to which people are uncomfortable with uncertain and ambiguous situations.
  • Masculinity/femininity
  • Individualism/collectivism: independence, individual initiative and privacy vs interdependence and loyalty to family or clan.
  • Long-term/short-term orientation: long-term stresses persistence, perseverance and thrift; short-term stresses personal steadiness, face saving and social niceties. Added through work with Canadian Michael Bond.

Canada and the United States have very similar profiles; both have a short-term orientation. Slide 15 pairs Hofstede with work centrality as cultural differences in values.

Exam trap: The first four dimensions are Hofstede's original; long/short-term is the fifth, added with Michael Bond. The GLOBE project (Robert House) later verified and supplemented Hofstede.

Memory aid: "Please Understand My Individual Leanings": Power distance, Uncertainty avoidance, Masculinity, Individualism, Long-term.

Ch 4 §4.1 · Week 2 slide 15

6

What is an attitude?

Model answer

An attitude is a fairly stable evaluative tendency to respond consistently to some specific object, situation, person or category of people.

  • Chain on slide 16: belief + value → attitude → behaviour.
  • Example: belief "my job interferes with my family" + value "I dislike anything that hurts my family" gives the attitude "I dislike my job", which may lead to the behaviour of looking for another job.
  • Attitudes are more specific than values and are only weakly linked to behaviour. The link is stronger when the person has direct experience and holds the attitude strongly.

Memory aid: B + V → A → B: Beliefs plus Values make Attitudes, which drive Behaviour.

7

What are the determinants and consequences of job satisfaction/dissatisfaction?

Model answer

Determinants of job satisfaction (slide 17):

  • Discrepancy: the gap between the job outcomes you want (from your values) and the outcomes you perceive you get (your beliefs).
  • Fairness: of outcomes (distributive), of the process (procedural) and of treatment (interactional).
  • Disposition: stable personality tendencies.
  • Mood and emotion.
  • Work factors: mentally challenging work (challenge), compensation, career opportunities, people (colleagues and supervisors); the text adds meaningful work.

Consequences (slide 18): absenteeism, turnover, performance, organizational citizenship behaviours (OCBs), counterproductive work behaviours (CWBs), and customer satisfaction and profit.

Dissatisfied employees are more likely to be absent, quit and engage in CWBs, and less likely to show OCBs. The absence link is not strong.

Exam trap: The text's Exhibit 4.6 has four determinants (discrepancy, fairness, disposition, mood and emotion); the slide adds "work factors" as a fifth box. The text's key contributors are mentally challenging work, meaningful work, adequate compensation, career opportunities, and friendly or helpful colleagues.

Memory aid: "Don't Forget Daily Moods": Discrepancy, Fairness, Disposition, Mood/emotion.

Ch 4 §4.3 · Ch 4 §4.4 · Week 2 slides 17–18

8

What is facet job satisfaction? What is overall job satisfaction?

Model answer

Job satisfaction is a collection of attitudes people have about their jobs. It has two aspects:

  • Facet satisfaction: the tendency to be more or less satisfied with the various facets of the job. Facets include the work itself, compensation, career opportunities, recognition, benefits, working conditions, supervision, co-workers and organizational policy. Example: "I love my work but hate my boss."
  • Overall satisfaction: an overall or summary indicator of a person's attitude toward the job that cuts across the facets (an average or total of the facet attitudes). Example: "On the whole, I really like my job, although a couple of aspects could stand some improvement."

Two employees can have the same overall satisfaction for different reasons. The Job Descriptive Index and the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire measure both.

9

What job satisfaction facet is most likely to stimulate high performance?

Model answer

The facet that matters most for performance is the content of the work itself. Interesting, challenging, meaningful jobs are the most likely to stimulate high performance.

  • The satisfaction–performance connection is stronger for complex, high-tech jobs (science, engineering, computers) than for routine labour jobs, partly because people in complex jobs have more control over their performance.
  • Not every satisfaction facet stimulates performance equally; the text qualifies the idea that "a happy worker is a productive worker".

Exam trap: Pay (compensation) is the tempting wrong answer. The text singles out the work itself. The same facet is also the best predictor of absenteeism.

Ch 4 §4.4 (performance)

10

What is organizational commitment?

Model answer

Organizational commitment is an attitude that reflects the strength of the linkage between an employee and an organization. This linkage has implications for whether someone tends to remain in the organization.

  • The lecture's version: identifying with a particular organization and its goals, and wishing to maintain membership in it.
  • Developed by Canadian researchers John Meyer and Natalie Allen, who identified three types.
  • Employees can be committed to the organization and also to constituencies such as their team, union or profession.
  • The best predictor of strong affective commitment is interesting, satisfying work; role clarity and met expectations also help.

Ch 4 §4.5 · Week 2 slide 19

11

What are the three types of organizational commitment?

Model answer

Meyer and Allen's three types of organizational commitment:

  • Affective commitment: based on identification and involvement with the organization. They stay because they want to.
  • Continuance commitment: based on the costs of leaving (e.g., losing a pension) or a lack of suitable job alternatives. They stay because they have to.
  • Normative commitment: based on ideology or a feeling of obligation. They stay because they think they should (ought to).

Example: an employee who stays only for the pension has high continuance commitment. The best predictor of affective commitment is interesting, satisfying work.

Exam trap: Continuance commitment is negatively related to performance, so commitment is not always good. Match "want / have / should" to affective / continuance / normative.

Memory aid: Want to, Have to, Ought to = A, C, N. "Affection, Costs, Norms."

12

How do abilities, personality, values, and attitudes influence behaviors?

Model answer

Slide 20 asks how personality, EI, cultural intelligence, values and attitudes affect behaviour in areas such as stress management, time management, change management, motivation, leadership and management. In the text:

  • Abilities: general cognitive ability predicts learning, training and job performance in all kinds of jobs; EI predicts job and academic performance above and beyond cognitive ability and the Big Five.
  • Personality: behaviour is a function of disposition and situation (interactionist), with the most impact in weak situations. The Big Five and core self-evaluations predict performance.
  • Values: broad, so weak predictors of specific behaviour, but they shape what people want from a job (discrepancy) and person–organization fit.
  • Attitudes: belief + value → attitude → behaviour. Job satisfaction and commitment affect absenteeism, turnover, performance, OCBs and customer satisfaction.

Perception

0 of 12 marked as known

1

What is perception? What are the three components of perception?

Model answer

Perception is the process of interpreting the messages of our senses to provide order and meaning to the environment. The key word is interpreting: people act on their interpretation of reality, not on reality itself. (Example: if you perceive your pay as low, you may job-hunt even if you are the best-paid person in your department.)

The three components of perception:

  • Perceiver: the perceiver's experience, needs (motivational state) and emotions (emotional state) affect the perception of the target. Emotions can trigger perceptual defence.
  • Target: perception involves interpretation of the target. Ambiguous targets are especially susceptible to interpretation.
  • Situation: the context (for example, timing) can affect what one perceives, and it adds information about the target.

Exam trap: Perceptual defence is a perceiver effect (the system protecting you from unpleasant emotions), and more information about the target does not automatically make perception more accurate.

Memory aid: "Who's looking, at what, and where": Perceiver, Target, Situation.

Ch 3 §3.1 · Week 3 slides 8–9

2

Explain Social Identity Theory. What are Prototypes?

Model answer

Social identity theory: people form perceptions of themselves based on their personal characteristics and their memberships in social categories. The sense of self has two parts:

  • Personal identity: based on our unique personal characteristics (interests, abilities, traits).
  • Social identity: based on our perception that we belong to social groups (gender, nationality, religion, occupation).

We categorize ourselves and others to make sense of the social environment, and we also form perceptions of others from their social category memberships. In the slides' terms, the perceiver forms perceptions of the target (self or other) based on social categories (the situation). Identities are relational and comparative (a professor is meaningful relative to a student).

Prototypes: once a category is chosen, we tend to see its members as embodying the most typical attributes of that category. We also see ourselves as embodying the prototype of the categories we belong to.

People see their own social categories more favourably, so the theory helps explain stereotyping and discrimination.

Ch 3 §3.2 · Week 3 slides 10–11

3

What does the 5-Stage Model of Perception tell us? What does Bruner’s Model of Perception tell us?

Model answer

5-Stage Model of Perception (lecture, slides 12–14): it shows how any perception is assembled from raw sensory input.

  1. Environmental stimuli.
  2. Observation (feeling, tasting, hearing, seeing, smelling).
  3. Perceptual selection: only some stimuli are sensed. Sensory overload occurs when there are too many. External factors (size, intensity, contrast) and internal factors (familiarity, source credibility) drive selection.
  4. Perceptual organization/construction: perceptual grouping (proximity, similarity); what we select is partly due to what others are perceiving.
  5. Interpretation: where attribution errors come in.

Bruner's Model (text, slide 15): how the perceiver puts together target and situation information to form a picture of the target. Unfamiliar target → openness to target cues → familiar cues encountered → target categorized → cue selectivity (contradictory cues ignored or distorted) → categorization strengthened. Example: a new co-worker is a Stanford grad, so he is a "good man" with "good potential", and his poor performance is ignored.

It shows three properties of perception: selectivity, perceptual constancy (target seen the same way over time) and perceptual consistency (cues selected, ignored or distorted to form a homogeneous picture).

Exam trap: Slide 28 allows "either perceptual model". Wording like categorization, cue selectivity or a new co-worker means Bruner; sensory overload, selection or grouping means the 5-stage model. Only Bruner is in the textbook.

Memory aid: "SOS, Oh I see": Stimuli, Observation, Selection, Organization, Interpretation.

5

What are Consistency cues? Consensus cues? Distinctiveness cues? How are they helpful?

Model answer

The three cues are the implicit questions we ask to decide between a dispositional and a situational cause:

  • Consistency cues: does the person engage in the behaviour regularly and consistently (over time)? High consistency leads to a dispositional attribution.
  • Consensus cues: do most people engage in the behaviour, or is it unique to this person? Unusual, low consensus behaviour leads to a dispositional attribution.
  • Distinctiveness cues: does the person do it across many situations, or only in one? Behaviour across many situations is low distinctiveness and points to disposition; behaviour in only one situation is high distinctiveness and points to the situation.

Dispositional = high consistency, low consensus, low distinctiveness. Situational = high consistency, high consensus, high distinctiveness. Temporary situational = low consistency (Sam).

How they help: without being able to read minds, managers use the cues to make a reasoned attribution and decide what action is warranted. Example: Roshani is absent a lot (high), co-workers seldom are (low consensus), and she was absent in her last job (low distinctiveness): dispositional. Mika is also absent a lot, but so are her co-workers and she was not absent before: situational.

Exam trap: Slide 19 says "High distinctiveness across situations is attributed to disposition", which contradicts the text and the slides' own Roshani/Mika/Sam exercise. Reason from the behaviour: many situations means low distinctiveness, which means dispositional.

Memory aid: Dispositional is "High-Low-Low" (consistency, consensus, distinctiveness).

6

What is meant by each of the following: The fundamental attribution error? The actor observer effect? The self-serving bias?

Model answer
  • Fundamental attribution error: the tendency to overemphasize dispositional explanations for behaviour at the expense of situational explanations (when judging other people). Example: citing laziness while ignoring poor training or a bad sales territory.
  • Actor-observer effect: the propensity for actors and observers to view the causes of the actor's behaviour differently. Actors stress the situation for their own behaviour; observers stress disposition for the same behaviour. Example: you blame a cancelled bus for being late, your boss calls you unreliable.
  • Self-serving bias: the tendency to take credit for successful outcomes and to deny responsibility for failures. Example: "I studied hard" for an A, "unfair questions" for a C.

Exam trap: Read whose perspective it is: one observer judging someone is the fundamental attribution error, two viewpoints on the same event is actor-observer, and one person explaining their own success versus failure is self-serving bias.

Memory aid: FAE is "blame them"; actor-observer is "two people, one event"; self-serving is "my wins, not my losses".

How are stereotyping and self-fulfilling prophecy connected?

Model answer
  • Stereotyping: the tendency to generalize about people in a social category and ignore variations among them (slide example: assuming someone is rude because of a perceived group association). It has three steps: distinguish a category, assume its members share certain traits, assume everyone in the category has them.

What is the halo effect? What is implicit personality theory? How is the halo effect different from implicit personality theory?

Model answer
  • Halo effect: one trait forms a general impression. Slide example: a well-dressed person is perceived as more competent, even without evidence of ability. In the textbook (rater errors), the rating of someone on one trait colours the ratings on other traits, and it can work for or against the ratee.

What is projection?

Model answer

Projection is the tendency for perceivers to attribute their own thoughts and feelings to others (assuming others are like us).

What are the four basic characteristics/components of motivation?

Model answer

Motivation is the extent to which persistent effort is directed toward a goal. Its four basic characteristics (slide 4: Motivation = Effort + Persistence + Direction + Goals):

  • Effort: the strength of work-related behaviour, or the amount of effort exerted on the job (a loading-dock worker carrying heavier crates).

How are Maslow’s “Notion of Prepotency” and Herzberg’s “Satisfaction Progression Hypothesis” similar?

Model answer

Maslow's notion of prepotency: the lowest-level unsatisfied need has the greatest motivating potential. Physiological needs come before safety, safety before belongingness, and so on. Once a need is satisfied, it is no longer an effective motivator and attention turns to the next level up.

Herzberg's two-factor theory (lecture, slides 10–11 and 13): hygiene factors (salary, job security, working conditions, quality of interpersonal relations) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth, achievement, recognition) increase satisfaction.

The similarity: both separate basic needs from higher-order needs. Meeting basic needs (Maslow) or hygiene factors (Herzberg) is a starting point that gets people to the point where higher-order needs can matter, but it does not by itself create lasting motivation. Slide 11 lines them up: Maslow's physiological and safety needs sit with hygiene factors, and self-actualization and esteem sit with motivators. Managers should meet basic needs first, then build motivation through meaningful work, achievement and growth.

The text states the idea this way: as lower-level needs are satisfied, desire for higher-level needs increases (Maslow, and Alderfer's first premise). Herzberg is lecture-only: his theory is hygiene versus motivators, not a step-by-step hierarchy.

What are the three needs proposed by McClelland?

Model answer

McClelland's theory of needs is a nonhierarchical need theory: it asks under what conditions each need produces particular patterns of motivation. Needs reflect relatively stable personality characteristics acquired in early life.

  • Need for achievement (n Ach): a strong desire to perform challenging tasks well. These people prefer personal responsibility for outcomes, set moderately difficult goals with calculated risks, and want performance feedback. Suited to sales and entrepreneurial jobs.

5

What does Self-Determination Theory tell us?

Model answer

Self-determination theory (SDT) relates the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs to autonomous or controlled motivation:

  • Competence: feeling capable and effective.
  • Autonomy: feeling choice in your own behaviour (it does not mean independence or doing whatever you want).
  • Relatedness: feeling connected, respected and cared for.

When the needs are satisfied, motivation is autonomous (self-motivated; you do the task because you chose it or enjoy it). When they are not, motivation is controlled (to get a reward, avoid punishment, or because the boss is watching). Autonomous motivation leads to more effective performance and positive outcomes. SDT is about the quality of motivation, not just the quantity: why you are motivated.

Implications for managers: create environments that satisfy the three needs; give autonomy support (choice, a meaningful rationale, acknowledging employees' perspectives, encouraging initiative, meaningful feedback); offer interesting, challenging jobs.

Exam trap: The three needs are not a hierarchy like Maslow's; they all matter at once. Controlled motivation is similar to extrinsic motivation, and autonomous is similar to intrinsic, but SDT's lens is internal versus external regulation.

Memory aid: SDT needs = "CAR": Competence, Autonomy, Relatedness.

6

Explain the three multiplicative components of Expectancy Theory.

Model answer

Expectancy theory (Vroom) is a process theory: motivation is determined by the outcomes people expect to result from their actions. The three components are multiplied, so if any is near zero, motivation collapses.

  • Expectancy: the probability that effort will lead to a first-level outcome, i.e. effort → performance. ("With training I can hit my sales target.")
  • Instrumentality: the probability that performance will be followed by a second-level outcome, i.e. performance → outcome. (A bonus promised but never paid means low instrumentality.)
  • Valence: the expected value of an outcome, how attractive it is to this person. (One values a bonus, another flexible hours.)

Formula: Force = Σ (valence × instrumentality) × expectancy. The lecture's three questions: Can I succeed if I try? Will success lead to an outcome? Do I value the outcome? Slide 17's table: only high-high-high gives high motivation; one low gives moderate or low; all low gives very low.

Managers: build expectancy (training, resources), make reward contingencies clear and honour them, and match rewards to individual valences.

Exam trap: Slide 17 heads the performance → outcome column "Expectancy" as well, which mislabels it. In the textbook (and slide 16's notes) performance → outcome is instrumentality; expectancy is only effort → performance.

Memory aid: E = "Can I do it?", I = "Will I get it?", V = "Do I want it?"

According to Equity Theory, what do individuals feel when they experience inequity? Why is this considered a motivating state? What is the importance of the Ratio of Exchange? What are the outcomes of perceived inequity?

Model answer

Equity theory (J. Stacy Adams): workers compare the inputs they invest and the outcomes they receive with those of another person or group.

  • Feeling under inequity: unequal ratios mean perceived inequity, which is unpleasant and produces tension (and dissatisfaction, especially when you are disadvantaged). The lecture notes say employees are demotivated when they perceive inequity.
  • Why it is a motivating state: people are motivated to maintain an equitable exchange and will devote considerable energy to reducing the tension and restoring equity.
  • Ratio of exchange: my outcomes ÷ my inputs compared with the other's outcomes ÷ inputs. Equal ratios mean fair exchange. It is the ratio, not the absolute reward, so relative perception matters more than the amount (someone well paid can still be demotivated).
  • Outcomes of perceived inequity (ways to reduce it): (1) perceptually distort your own inputs or outcomes; (2) distort the comparison person's; (3) choose another comparison person or group; (4) alter your own inputs or outcomes (reduce effort, ask for a raise); (5) leave the exchange relationship. The first three are psychological; the last two are behavioural.

Slide 20 examples: inputs include quality and quantity of work, experience, education, initiative; outputs include pay, job security, recognition, status.

Exam trap: The lecture slides say inputs and "outputs"; the textbook says inputs and "outcomes". Same idea. Underpayment effects are well supported; the textbook says predictions about overpayment have received less support.

Memory aid: "Distort me, distort them, swap them, change me, leave."

Slide 33: pay for performance plans for production jobs, white-collar jobs, and pay to motivate teamwork.

What are the characteristics of a well-structured problem?

Model answer

A problem is a perceived gap between an existing state and a desired state. A well-structured problem is one where:

  • the existing state is clear
  • the desired state is clear
  • how to get from one to the other is fairly obvious

These problems are simple, repetitive and familiar, and their solutions arouse little controversy. They can be handled with programmed decisions (rules, routines, standard operating procedures). Lecture wording: a clear starting point, clear goals, an established procedure, the information is available, and you can check whether the answer meets the goal.

  • Textbook examples: which of 10 car loan applications to approve; which delivery route to use.
  • Lecture example: you need 120 units, have 40, and boxes hold 10, so (120 - 40) / 10 = 8 boxes.

By contrast, an ill-structured problem has unclear states and an unknown method, is often unique and controversial, and needs non-programmed decision making.

What is framing?

Model answer

Framing is the aspects of the presentation of information about a problem that are assumed by decision makers. A frame can include assumptions about the boundaries of the problem, the possible outcomes, or the reference points used to judge success. It is one reason bounded rationality occurs (lecture reason 1).

  • Same facts, different frame: a $10,000 expenditure seen as a cost (avoid) or as an investment (pursue).
  • A new product introduction framed as a military campaign against competitors or as a crusade to help customers.
  • Lecture: a survey result reported as "customers are satisfied" stresses success; reported as "customers are dissatisfied" stresses a problem. The information is the same but the reaction changes.

Framing also drives risk behaviour: a choice between losses invites risky decisions, and a choice between gains invites conservative ones.

Remedies: be self-conscious about your frame, try out alternative frames, and avoid overarching, universal frames (for example, treating every problem as a customer service problem).

What is an “availability bias” and a “representativeness bias”?

Model answer

Both are cognitive biases (tendencies to acquire and process information in an error-prone way). Both are mental shortcuts.

  • Availability bias: the tendency to favour information that can be accessed quickly and easily, so we remember vivid, recent events. It asks "what can I remember quickly?" Example: one customer complains loudly about price, so you conclude price is why customers leave. A memorable complaint does not show what most customers think. (Textbook: ER doctors misdiagnosing patients with "COVID brain".)
  • Representativeness bias (lecture): judging something by how closely it resembles a familiar category or stereotype, while overlooking other evidence. It asks "what does this look like?" Example: "this restaurant looks like other successful restaurants, so it must give excellent service."

The shortcut simplifies the decision but can lead us away from the evidence.

Model answer

Slide 12 lists three problems with the information search stage:

  • Confirmation bias: the tendency to seek out information that conforms to your own definition of, or solution to, a problem. If you believe price is the problem, you focus on price complaints and overlook long waits or poor service.
  • Information overload: the reception of more information than is necessary to make effective decisions. It leads to errors, omissions, delays and cutting corners.
  • Not-invented-here bias: the tendency to ignore or hold negative attitudes toward ideas from outside your own organization or project team. Judge an idea on its merit, not its source (Nokia kept developing its own software despite a good open-source option).

The textbook's "too little information" list also includes availability bias and overconfidence.

Exam trap: The lecture lists three; the textbook adds availability and overconfidence under too little information. Questions on the slide list expect the three above.

Memory aid: Biased (confirmation), Buried (overload), Blocked (not-invented-here).

8

What is anchoring? Satisficing?

Model answer
  • Anchoring effect: the inadequate adjustment of subsequent estimates from an initial estimate that serves as an anchor. Example: a seller opens at $10,000 for equipment and "reduces" to $8,000. You feel you got a bargain, but comparable equipment sells for about $6,000. The first price anchored your judgment. Textbook examples: real estate agents let the asking price pull their valuation; acquirers are swayed by the last premium paid in their industry. Defence: get independent comparisons and set your own criteria before negotiating.
  • Satisficing: establishing an adequate level of acceptability for a solution to a problem and then screening solutions until one that exceeds this level is found. Evaluation then stops. Example: you need a supplier that delivers within two days, meets quality and fits the budget, and you pick the first that clears all three.

Satisficing is what bounded decision makers do; the perfectly rational person maximizes (chooses the alternative with the greatest expected value). Both anchoring and satisficing fall under alternative development, evaluation and choice (slide 12).

Exam trap: Satisficing is a rational response to limited information and time, not careless settling. Maximization needs all alternatives, values and probabilities.

Ch 11 §11.3 · Week 5A slides 12–13

9

What are problems in decision-making when risk is involved?

Model answer

The central problem is framing: the same choice produces different risk behaviour depending on whether it is seen as a loss or a gain.

  • When people view a problem as a choice between losses, they tend to make risky decisions.
  • When people view a problem as a choice between gains, they tend to make conservative decisions.

Textbook example (Bazerman): a firm faces a 50% chance of losing $50 million, or can settle for a sure $25 million. Framed as a sure loss versus a gamble, about 80% of students gamble. Framed as saving $25 million versus a gamble at saving $50 million, about 80% take the sure thing. The options are worth the same.

Lecture adds three attitudes toward risk: risk-averse (prefers the certain outcome), risk-neutral (indifferent) and risk-taking (prefers the gamble). A risk preference alone does not make someone irrational.

Textbook: managerial risk taking is also stimulated by pay tied to the stock price, poor relative performance, abundant resources and personality.

Exam trap: Framing effect (the same person changes behaviour with the presentation) is not the same as risk attitude (a standing preference). The three attitudes are lecture-only.

Memory aid: Losses lead to leaps; gains lead to guarding.

10

What are problems with the solution implementation stage of decision-making?

Model answer

The main problem: decision makers are often dependent on others to implement their decisions, and it may be hard to anticipate those people's ability and motivation to do so.

  • Textbook example: products designed, engineered and produced in a series of stages. Engineering implements designers' decisions and production implements engineering's, which leads to confusion, conflict and delay unless cross-functional teams are used during decision making.
  • Lecture: a promising decision disappoints if conditions are missing. Staff must be trained, responsibilities clear, standards established, and there must be enough time.

The lecture's closing test asks two questions of any option: is it a good option, and can we implement it effectively? Both must be answered.

The perfectly rational decision maker factors implementation problems into the choice; the bounded decision maker may find it difficult because of reliance on others (Exhibit 11.2).

Exam trap: Do not confuse this with solution evaluation (justification, escalation of commitment, hindsight), which is about looking back at a decision already made.

11

What is the escalation of commitment? What are the reasons this occurs? How can it be prevented?

Model answer

Escalation of commitment is the tendency to invest additional resources in an apparently failing course of action. It involves sunk costs, permanent losses of resources from a past decision that should not enter future decisions (people "throw good resources after bad").

Reasons (slide 17 and text):

  • dissonance reduction (escalating "proves" the decision was right)
  • the social norm for consistent behaviour
  • motivation to not appear wasteful
  • the way the problem is framed (a sure loss versus an uncertain larger loss, so people gamble)
  • personality, moods and emotions

Prevention (textbook):

  • watch for excessive optimism or early positive media attention
  • keep reframing the problem (saving rather than spending)
  • set specific goals in advance that must be met before more resources go in
  • evaluate managers on how they made decisions, not only on outcomes
  • separate initial and subsequent decision making (others decide whether to continue)

Exam trap: Slide 17 only asks "what are your ideas?" for prevention; the five above are the textbook's. Groups are more prone than individuals to escalate, so handing the decision to a group is not a fix. Justification is defending a faulty decision to avoid dissonance; escalation is the extra investment.

Memory aid: "Don't Say We Failed, Please": Dissonance, Social norm, Wasteful, Framing, Personality.

Ch 11 §11.4 · Week 5A slide 17

12

How can emotion and mood affect decision-making?

Model answer

Slide 18: emotion and mood both affect decisions, and emotionless decision making would be poor decision making.

Emotion can:

  • correct ethical errors (whistle-blowers often say emotion motivated them)
  • aid in developing creative solutions to problems
  • be distracting and unsettling to decision making and lead to poor choices (stress, excessive pride making CEOs overpay for acquisitions)

Mood (a mild, unfocused positive or negative feeling) can affect: information recall, evaluation, time reference, projected outcomes and creativity.

  • Good mood: remember positive information, evaluate more positively, overestimate good events, use simplified shortcut strategies, more creative and intuitive.
  • Bad mood: remember negative information, evaluate more negatively, more deliberate, systematic and detailed.

In a currency-trader simulation, traders in a neutral mood did best.

Exam trap: The slide's fifth item is "time reference", while the textbook bullets say likelihood of events and decision strategy. Mood has its greatest impact on uncertain, ambiguous decisions, not routine ones.

Memory aid: "Red Elephants Truly Prefer Carrots": Recall, Evaluation, Time reference, Projected outcomes, Creativity.

Communication

1

What are examples of each of the six stages of communication?

Model answer

Communication is the process by which information is exchanged between a sender and a receiver. Slide 4 (Basic Model of Communication) shows these labelled stages. Example: a manager wants a new hire to finish a report by Friday.

  1. Thinking (sender): decides what to communicate. "I need the sales report by Friday."
  2. Encoding (sender): puts the idea into words, symbols or gestures. She writes "need it by end of week".
  3. Transmitting (sender): sends it through a channel such as email, phone or conversation.
  4. Perceiving (receiver): receives the message (reads or hears it).
  5. Decoding (receiver): interprets it based on his own understanding. He reads "end of week" as Sunday.
  6. Understanding (receiver): makes sense of the message.
  7. Feedback: goes back to the sender to check it was understood. "So you want it Friday at 5?"

The model shows the many points where errors can occur, especially when a message is ambiguous or emotional (humour sent by email).

Exam trap: The slide shows seven labelled terms, not six, so answer from the slide. The textbook describes the process in prose (encode, perceive, decode, understanding, feedback) and has no "thinking" step; a count of six most plausibly drops Thinking. Learn all seven, and remember the sender encodes while the receiver decodes.

Memory aid: "The Eager Teacher Prepares Daily Useful Feedback": Thinking, Encoding, Transmitting, Perceiving, Decoding, Understanding, Feedback.

LectureCh 10 §10.1 · Week 5B slide 4

2

What is the difference between upward, horizontal and downward communication?

Model answer

The chain of command is the lines of authority and formal reporting relationships. Three necessary forms of communication run along it (slide 5):

  • Downward: flows from the top of the organization toward the bottom. Example: a vice president of retail banking instructs regional managers, who instruct branch managers, who instruct financial advisors. Lecture: instructions, policy, goals, performance feedback.
  • Upward: flows from the bottom toward the top. Example: a chemist passes a new plastic formula to the R&D manager, who informs the vice president. Lecture: progress reports, concerns, suggestions.
  • Horizontal (across): occurs between departments or functional units, usually to coordinate effort. In a strict chain it goes up to a common manager and back down. Example: a salesperson's product idea must go up through marketing and down through R&D. Lecture: sales coordinating with delivery.

Exam trap: Direction (where the message goes) is a different question from deficiency (why the chain fails). A salesperson's idea travelling up and down to reach R&D is horizontal communication suffering from slowness.

Ch 10 §10.1 · Week 5B slides 5–6

3

What is the difference between formal and informal communication?

Model answer
  • Formal communication follows the chain of command: the lines of authority and formal reporting relationships shown on the organizational chart. It runs downward, upward and horizontally. Examples: a manager's instructions, a progress report to your boss, the McMaster structure where faculty report to department heads, who report to deans.
  • Informal communication takes place among members outside the official channels: friendships and workplace networks, with employees using their connections to exchange ideas. The grapevine is the organization's informal communication network and often cuts across formal lines. It can use word of mouth, email or social media.

The strict chain of command fails to consider informal communication, but good ideas don't respect organizational boundaries, and informal networks can be important sources of innovation. Effective organizations need clear formal channels and room for informal sharing.

Exam trap: Failure to consider informal communication is the first deficiency of the chain of command. The grapevine is informal, not a formal channel.

Ch 10 §10.1 · Week 5B slides 5, 9

4

What are the deficiencies of a formal chain of command (with examples)?

Model answer

Slide 5 lists three deficiencies of the chain of command:

  • Failure to consider informal communication: the chain ignores friendship and workplace networks, yet good ideas cross organizational boundaries. Example: an engineer and a marketer chat in the cafeteria and spot a product improvement that the official channels would never carry.
  • Filtering: messages are watered down or stopped altogether during transmission. Upward filtering often happens because people fear management will use the information against them. Downward filtering may come from time pressure or inattention, or from sinister motives ("information is power": a manager withholds information so a rising subordinate looks bad at a staff meeting).
  • Slowness: the message must pass through several people, which delays decisions, especially horizontal communication and responses to customer problems. Example: a salesperson's idea going up to the marketing VP and down to R&D.

Fixes: cross-functional teams, employee empowerment and, at Boston Children's Hospital, the I-PASS handoff system.

Exam trap: Slide 5 lists informal, filtering, slowness; the textbook order is informal, slowness, filtering. Same three. Filtering is a message altered in transit; the mum effect is avoiding passing on bad news at all.

Memory aid: "IFS": Informal ignored, Filtering, Slowness.

5

What is meant by “voice”? “psychological safety”? the mum effect? (with examples for each)

Model answer

Slide 8: effective communication requires a free flow of information, which means voice, psychological safety and no mum effect.

  • Voice: the constructive expression of disagreement or concern about work unit or organizational practices ("speaking up"); its opposite is silence, withholding relevant information. Example: an employee tells her supervisor a new procedure creates safety risks and suggests a fix. Satisfied, identified, conscientious and extraverted employees are most likely to speak up.
  • Psychological safety: a shared belief that it is safe to take social risks. Lecture: people feel comfortable asking questions and admitting mistakes or disagreement without fear of humiliation or punishment. Example: a nurse reports her own dosing error because mistakes are not unfairly punished.
  • Mum effect: the tendency to avoid communicating unfavourable news to others. Example: a nurse who gave the wrong dose is reluctant to tell the head nurse; Nokia's middle managers did not challenge top management's optimism. It also runs downward: bosses avoid giving bad performance ratings.

Exam trap: Mum effect is avoiding bad news (it is more likely when the sender is responsible for it); filtering is a message altered as it travels; voice is constructive speaking up, not any complaint.

Ch 10 §10.2 · Week 5B slide 8

6

What is the “grapevine”?

Model answer

The grapevine is an organization's informal communication network. It cuts across the formal lines of communication, carrying information through discussion and relationships rather than the official reporting channel. It can use word of mouth, email or social media, and an organization often has several loosely linked grapevines.

  • It carries performance-relevant information as well as personal gossip.
  • At least 75% of the non-controversial, organization-related information it carries is correct; personal and emotionally charged information is most likely to be distorted.
  • Pros (slide 9): can keep employees informed; provides a test of employee reactions to proposed changes; a potential informal recruiting source.
  • Con: a pipeline for rumours (a rumour is an unverified belief in general circulation).

Rumours spread fastest when the information is ambiguous, important, credible and the recipient is anxious. Example: steel-mill workers learned the details of a fatal accident within five minutes of seeing the sign reset to zero.

Exam trap: The textbook gives two pros (keeps employees informed; tests reactions); the slide adds a third, informal recruiting source, which is lecture-only. The grapevine is the network; a rumour is unverified content on it.

Ch 10 §10.3 · Week 5B slide 9

7

What is “jargon”? Pros? Cons?

Model answer

Jargon is specialized language used by job holders or members of particular occupations or organizations. Example: "OB" means organizational behaviour to management professors and obstetrics to physicians; "PC" can mean placebo control, personal computer, politically correct or preclinical in different parts of a pharmaceutical company.

Pros

  • An efficient means of communicating with peers; lets people who share knowledge communicate quickly and precisely
  • Gives a touch of status to those who have mastered it; strengthens professional belonging

Cons

  • A barrier to clear communication between departments (sales and engineering) and to people outside the organization or profession
  • Newcomers find it intimidating and confusing and may be reluctant to ask questions

Exam trap: The same term can mean different things in different departments, which is why jargon blocks communication between departments as well as with outsiders.

8

What are examples of the non-verbal language of work?

Model answer

Non-verbal communication is the transmission of messages by some medium other than speech or writing. Slide 11 gives two forms, with examples:

  • Body language: communication by the sender's bodily motions, facial expressions or physical location relative to the receiver. Examples: directing the torso toward the receiver, sitting close, eye contact and leaning forward signal liking and interest. A relaxed posture (casual arms and legs, reclining, no fidgeting) signals higher status.
  • Props, artifacts and costumes:
    • Office décor and arrangement: a tidy office with posters and plants and the desk against the wall feels welcoming; diplomas on display signal expertise; chairs around a shared table invite discussion.
    • Clothing: a lab coat identifies a researcher; formal dress signals an official occasion; conventional dress communicates high status, competence and authority.

Interpret signals in context, since meaning differs across cultures, individuals and situations.

Exam trap: Non-verbal is the whole category; body language is one form and props, artifacts and costumes is the other. Relaxation signals status, not liking.

10

What is information richness? What are two importance dimensions of information richness?

Model answer

Information richness is the potential information-carrying capacity of a communication medium. Lecture wording: a medium's ability to express meaning and support understanding, especially for complex, vague or ambiguous messages.

Two important dimensions (slide 15):

  1. The degree of synchronization: whether information is exchanged between sender and receiver two-way, in real time. A conversation allows immediate clarification; email usually involves delay.
  2. The degree of non-verbal and para-verbal cues: signals that go beyond the words. Non-verbal cues include facial expression, eye contact and gestures; para-verbal cues include tone of voice (the lecture also mentions volume).

So face-to-face is very high in richness (synchronous, with both kinds of cues). A phone call is fairly rich (audio only). Memos, letters and emails are essentially one-way, with no non-verbal or para-verbal cues; text is the lowest.

Exam trap: Synchronization is about timing; cues are about the signals carried. Do not mix them up, and do not call richness the amount of information.

11

How is the level of information richness different for routine versus non-routine messages?

Model answer

The textbook rule: less routine communication requires richer communication media.

  • Routine messages: reports and email are fine for recurrent, non-controversial, impersonal communication in which information is merely being disseminated. Lower-richness media are enough.
  • Non-routine messages: important decisions, intended changes, controversial messages and emotional issues generally call for richer media (face-to-face, video). Use these also when a message is complex, vague or ambiguous.

Text is often emotionally ambiguous because verbal, non-verbal and para-verbal cues are missing, so avoid trying to resolve conflicts through text. Example: announcing a meeting time by email is routine; announcing layoffs or settling a dispute with a colleague calls for a face-to-face or video conversation.

Exam trap: Match the medium to the message: richer media do not make sense for every message. Slide 15 covers only the two dimensions; the routine versus non-routine rule comes from the end of textbook section 10.7.

  • Do not confuse the person with the problem
  • Say what you feel (congruence)
