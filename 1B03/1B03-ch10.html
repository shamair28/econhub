Section 1

Why Perfect Competition Is the Benchmark

Chapter 9 gave you the firm’s cost curves — MC, ATC, AVC and AFC. Chapter 10 finally puts them to work: how much should a firm produce, and should it produce at all? We answer that for the simplest, most extreme market there is. Hubbard (10.1) groups markets by three characteristics: (1) the number of firms in the industry, (2) how similar the firms’ products are, and (3) how easily new firms can enter. (An industry is all the firms selling a particular good or service.) Different mixes of the three give four market structures:

CharacteristicPerfect competitionMonopolistic competitionOligopolyMonopoly
Number of firmsManyManyFewOne
Type of productIdenticalDifferentiatedIdentical or differentiatedUnique
Ease of entryHighHighLowEntry blocked
ExamplesGrowing wheat; producing applesClothing stores; restaurantsManufacturing computers or automobilesDelivery of mail by Canada Post

Textbook (Hubbard Table 10.1). The course takes the other three structures in Units 9–11, starting with monopolistic competition (Ch. 11, Unit 9) — and each one is judged against the benchmark you build this week.

Memory Aid — “N · P · E”: the three ways to sort a market Number of firms · Product (identical, differentiated or unique) · Ease of entry.
  • Direction rule: the more firms and the easier the entry, the closer to perfect competition.
  • Trap: “many firms” alone is not enough — monopolistic competition has many firms too, but differentiated products.
Self-test: which structure has few firms, low entry and either kind of product? (Oligopoly.)

Perfect competition is rare in pure form, but the logic is the same one that disciplines every market with easy entry. Hubbard’s opener shows it. By 2015, cage-free eggs sold for up to double the price of conventional eggs. Cage-free production costs more (fewer hens per area, hens that eat more), but the higher price meant bigger profits — so farms entered. By mid-2017 the U.S. had more than 40 million cage-free hens, about 13% of egg production (up from 6% in 2015), and supply grew faster than demand, so the premium shrank. In the long run, competition pushed the price down to the level that just covers costs. That one story contains this whole chapter.

The yardstick Perfect competition is the benchmark every later market structure is judged against. When Units 9–11 ask “what goes wrong with monopoly?”, the answer is always a comparison with what this chapter says a competitive market does: price equal to marginal cost, and zero economic profit once entry has done its work.
Where this sits on the exam Unit 8 is the last unit on Midterm Test 2 (Sat Nov 7, 12:30 pm), which covers Units 5–8 = Ch 6, 8, 9 and 10. Lesson map: 8.1 (perfect competition and profit maximization) → Sections 2–3 · 8.2 (short-run profits) → Sections 4–6 · 8.3 (long-run profits) → Sections 7–8.

Two sources: Dr. Mang’s slides list five conditions and tie producer surplus to profit (PS = TR − VC, π = PS − FC); Hubbard lists three conditions and adds the terms sunk cost, allocative efficiency, productive efficiency, and the long-run supply curves of constant-, increasing- and decreasing-cost industries. MyLab and the textbook homework use Hubbard’s wording, so this page weaves both together and labels which is which.
Section 2

The Five Conditions and the Price-Taking Firm

In Dr. Mang’s Lesson 8.1, a market is perfectly competitive only if all five of these hold at the same time:

1 · Many small buyers and sellers

There are many buyers and many sellers, each “small” relative to the market, so no single one can affect the market price.

2 · A homogeneous (identical) good

The good is the same whoever sells it — perfect substitutes — so buyers consider only the price and buy from the lowest-price seller.

3 · Perfect information

Buyers and sellers know the prices on offer and the quality of the good.

4 · No additional transaction costs

There are no extra fees or commitments attached to buying or selling.

5 · Easy entry and exit

Firms can easily enter the market or leave it — no barriers to entry.

Slides vs textbook — five conditions or three? Dr. Mang’s slides list five conditions. Hubbard (10.1) lists three: (1) many sellers, all small relative to the market, (2) all firms sell identical products, (3) no barriers to new firms entering. Mang adds perfect information and no transaction costs; the other three line up with Hubbard’s. The book says its second and third conditions are “very restrictive” — its examples are agricultural products such as wheat, corn and apples. Learn Mang’s five for the test, and recognize Hubbard’s three when a MyLab question words it that way.
Memory Aid — all five must “SHINE” Small & many buyers and sellers · Homogeneous product · Information is perfect · No transaction costs · Easy entry and exit.
  • Direction rule: any one condition failing means the market is not perfectly competitive.
  • Direction rule: a brand or a loyal customer base = not homogeneous; a licence, patent or huge start-up cost = a barrier to entry.
  • Trap: “stiff competition” or “lots of rivals” is not the same thing. A market can feel cut-throat and still fail one condition.
Self-test: which condition is broken by Toronto taxi licences, by iPhones, and by a farmers’ market with haggling fees? (Easy entry · Homogeneous product · No transaction costs.)

The price-taking firm

The market price is set where market demand meets market supply. A firm in a perfectly competitive market is a price taker — Hubbard’s definition is “a buyer or seller that is unable to affect the market price.” It is so small and its product so identical to everyone else’s that whatever it does, the price doesn’t budge. Ask even a cent more than the market price and every buyer simply buys from one of the thousands of identical sellers; charge less and you give away money you could have had.

So the firm’s own demand curve, d, is horizontal at the market price — perfectly elastic. That is the “perfectly elastic demand” you met in Unit 5, now showing up as a firm-level picture. Because every unit sells at the same price P:

For a price taker: d = MR = AR = P

Average revenue AR = TR ÷ q = (P × q) ÷ q = P. Marginal revenue MR = ΔTR ÷ Δq = P, because each extra unit adds exactly the price. Total revenue TR = P × q is a straight line through the origin with slope P.

Hubbard’s Farmer Parker sells wheat at $7 a bushel. His revenue climbs $0, $7, $14, $21, … for 0, 1, 2, 3, … bushels; AR is $7 at every quantity and MR is $7 for every bushel. His 20,000 bushels are far less than 0.001% of the market’s 1.04 billion, so he can’t move the price.

Worked Example 1 — Wheat at $60 a Tonne (illustrative numbers)
Market supply and demand set the wheat price at $60 per tonne. A farm sells 80 tonnes, and then considers selling 81. What are its TR, MR and AR, and what happens if it tries to charge $61?
Sell 80 tonnes:   TR = 60 × 80 = $4,800
Sell 81 tonnes:   TR = 60 × 81 = $4,860

MR of the 81st tonne = ΔTR ÷ Δq = (4,860 − 4,800) ÷ 1 = $60  = P
AR = TR ÷ q = 4,800 ÷ 80 = $60                               = P

Ask $61 per tonne: buyers can get identical wheat at $60 from
other farms, so this farm sells 0 tonnes. Ask less than $60 and
it loses revenue on every tonne for no gain.

So d is horizontal at $60: sell as much as you want at $60,
nothing above it.
Don’t Let This Happen to You — firm demand ≠ market demand The market demand curve for wheat slopes down, as always. One farmer’s demand curve is a horizontal line. The scales differ too: the market trades in millions or billions of bushels, one farm in thousands. Mixing the two up — drawing a downward-sloping curve for a single competitive firm, or a horizontal one for the whole market — is a classic error.

Section 3

Choosing Output: MR = MC

Lesson 8.1’s big question: how many units should the firm produce? Profit is π = TR − TC, and there are two equivalent ways to find where it peaks.

  • Total view (TR/TC graph). TR < TC is a loss, TR = TC is a break-even point, TR > TC is a profit. Profit is the vertical gap between the curves, and it is largest where TR and TC have the same slope.
  • Marginal view. The slope of TR is MR = ΔTR ÷ Δq and the slope of TC is MC = ΔTC ÷ Δq. So “same slope” means MR = MC.
The marginal rule

MR > MC → the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so profit rises: produce it. MR < MC → profit falls: don’t. Produce while MR ≥ MC; for the very last unit MR = MC. For a price taker MR = P, so the rule becomes P = MC — “the market price consumers pay equals the marginal cost of making the good.”

Memory Aid — “Keep going while the next unit pays for itself” MR ≥ MC → produce that unit; stop where MR = MC.
  • Direction rule: MR > MC → produce more. MR < MC → cut back.
  • For a price taker: MR = P, so P = MC picks q*. A higher price pushes q* out along the MC curve; a lower price pulls it in.
  • Trap: MR = MC is the rule for any firm; P = MC only works for price takers.
Self-test: MC(7) = $6.50 and MC(8) = $9.00 at P = $7 — how many units? (7: the 7th still pays for itself, the 8th doesn’t.)
Vocabulary Trap — “maximize profit” ≠ “maximize profit per unit” Firms maximize total profit, not profit per unit (and not revenue). The quantity where P − ATC is biggest is smaller than q*; at that quantity MR > MC, so the firm could still raise total profit by producing more. And q* is not where ATC is lowest — the two coincide only in long-run equilibrium (Section 7). Hubbard calls this “Don’t Let This Happen to You”: remember that firms maximize their total profit, not their profit per unit.

Fixed cost never changes q* Fixed cost doesn’t change MC or MR, so a $10 rise in Parker’s fixed cost leaves him producing 7 bushels — it only lowers his profit, from $13.50 to $3.50. Fixed cost changes the level of profit, not the profit-maximizing quantity.
Section 4

Profits and Losses in the Short Run

MR = MC tells you how much to produce. Lesson 8.2 asks whether that quantity makes money. Divide profit by q and you get a per-unit measure:

Profit = (P − ATC) × q

Since π = TR − TC, then π ÷ q = P − ATC. At the profit-maximizing q*, draw a rectangle q* wide and P − ATC tall: its area is the profit. Hubbard’s three cases at q*: P > ATC → profit; P = ATC → break even (zero economic profit); P < ATC → loss, of area (ATC − P) × q*. In that last case q* is the loss-minimizing quantity: “maximizing profit amounts to minimizing loss.”

Worked Example 3 — A Profit and a Loss with a Smooth Cost Function
A firm has FC = $1,000, VC = 0.01q³ − 1.2q² + 60q, so MC = 0.03q² − 2.4q + 60 and AVC = 0.01q² − 1.2q + 60. Find its output and profit (or loss) at (a) P = $60 and (b) P = $30.
(a) P = $60:  set P = MC → 0.03q² − 2.4q + 60 = 60 → q = 80
    TR  = 60 × 80 = $4,800
    VC  = 0.01(80)³ − 1.2(80)² + 60(80) = 5,120 − 7,680 + 4,800 = $2,240
    TC  = 1,000 + 2,240 = $3,240

Making the Connection — restaurants. When rents and wages rise in a city, a ramen shop’s MC and ATC curves both shift up while its price (about $16 a bowl in Toronto) can’t rise because competitors are everywhere. P falls below the new ATC and the shop loses money; the profit-maximizing quantity falls. A few deep-pocketed shops hang in for a while and the rest close — the long-run story in Section 7.

Memory Aid — read the rectangle from the top
  • Top of the rectangle = P, bottom = ATC at q*, width = q*.
  • P above ATC → profit (green). P below ATC → loss (red).
  • Trap: find q* from P = MC first; only then read ATC off the curve at that q*. Never read ATC at the lowest point of the curve unless P happens to equal it.
Section 5

To Operate or Shut Down?

If the price is below ATC, the firm is losing money. Must it close? Not necessarily. In the short run the firm has two options:

  • Keep producing: earn revenue R, pay variable cost VC, and still pay fixed cost FC. Loss = FC + VC − R.
  • Shut down temporarily: no revenue, no variable cost — but it still pays FC for the period. Loss = FC.

Compare: if R > VC, the loss from operating (FC − (R − VC)) is smaller than FC, so operate. If R < VC, operating loses more than FC, so shut down. Since R = P × q and VC = AVC × q, divide through by q:

The shutdown rule

Operate if P > AVC · shut down if P < AVC · indifferent if P = AVC (both losses equal FC). “As long as a firm can pay its variable cost of production, it should operate in the short run.” The shutdown point (Hubbard) is the minimum point on the AVC curve: if price falls below it, the firm shuts down production in the short run.

Why can fixed cost be ignored? In the short run it is a sunk cost — Hubbard’s term for a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered. You owe it whether you produce or not, so it can’t help decide between producing and not producing. “As long as a firm’s total revenue is greater than its variable costs, it should continue to produce no matter how large or small its fixed costs are.” A loss-making competitive firm also can’t escape by raising its price — sales would fall to zero.

Memory Aid — “Party, Patience, Pack up” P > ATC → Party (profit). AVC < P < ATC → Patience (operate at a loss: revenue covers all variable cost and part of fixed cost). P < AVC → Pack up (shut down; lose only FC).

Section 6

The Firm’s and the Market’s Supply Curves

Put Sections 3–5 together. At each price the firm picks the quantity where P = MC, provided P is at least minimum AVC; below that it produces nothing. Tracing quantity against price gives the firm’s supply curve:

Firm supply curve = the MC curve above minimum AVC

For prices below the shutdown point (minimum AVC), quantity supplied is zero. Market supply is the sum of the quantities every firm supplies at each price — a horizontal sum of the firms’ supply curves.

Vocabulary Trap — shutdown point vs. break-even point The shutdown point is the minimum of AVC (here $24): below it the firm stops. The break-even point is the minimum of ATC (here ≈ $39.29): at that price profit is exactly zero. Between them ($24 to $39.29) the firm produces at a loss — that’s the “Patience” zone. And the supply curve starts at the shutdown point, not the break-even point.

Textbook adding-up example. Each wheat farmer supplies 20,000 bushels at $7. With 52,000 identical farmers, the market supplies 20,000 × 52,000 = 1.04 billion bushels at $7. Real farms differ, but the principle is the same.

Section 7

The Long Run: Entry, Exit and Zero Economic Profit

In the long run, all inputs are variable and, crucially, firms can enter and leave — the fifth condition in action. Economic profit is a signal, and entry and exit respond to it:

  • P > ATC (economic profit) → new investors enter → market supply shifts right → the market price falls → each firm’s profit shrinks. Entry stops only when the profit is gone.
  • P < ATC (economic loss) → firms exit → market supply shifts left → the price rises → losses shrink. Exit stops only when the losses are gone.
Long-run competitive equilibrium

Hubbard: “the entry and exit of firms has resulted in the typical firm breaking even.” Mang’s two conditions: (1) P = ATC → zero economic profit (= normal profit: accounting profit exactly equals what investors expected to earn for the risk, so capital is allocated efficiently); (2) P = MC and P = ATC → MC = ATC, which happens only at ATC’s minimum. So P = MC = minimum ATC: production is efficient, and that is the lowest price at which firms can sustainably supply.

Worked Example 7 — Entry Drives Profit to Zero (Smooth Cost Firm)
Each identical firm has FC = $1,000 and the smooth cost function. The market price is $60. What happens in the long run? What if it were $30?
P = $60: each firm earns π = $1,560 (Example 3).
  → Economic profit attracts entry. Market supply shifts RIGHT.
  → The price falls... and keeps falling while profit remains.
  → Stop at P = min ATC ≈ $39.29: each firm produces
    ≈ 70 units, and π = (P − ATC) × q = 0.

If P were $30 (each firm loses ≈ $626):
  → Losses push firms out. Market supply shifts LEFT.
  → The price rises... until it is back to ≈ $39.29.
  → Remaining firms again produce ≈ 70 units with π = 0.

Either way, the long run ends at the same place: P ≈ $39.29
= MC = min ATC.
Memory Aid — “Profit is a magnet, losses are a repellent” Profit pulls firms in (supply → right, price ↓). Losses push firms out (supply → left, price ↑). It stops when profit = 0, and the end state is the “three-way handshake”: P = MC = min ATC.
  • Direction rule: entry → supply right → price down. Exit → supply left → price up.
  • Trap: entry and exit shift the market supply curve; each firm’s own supply curve (its MC) doesn’t move.
Self-test: price is $18 and min ATC is $22 — what happens in the long run? (Losses → exit → supply left → price rises to $22.)
Vocabulary Trap — “zero economic profit” does not mean “broke” Economic profit = revenues minus all costs, implicit and explicit. Zero economic profit = normal profit: the owners earn exactly what their time and money could earn elsewhere. Hubbard’s Farmer Gillette is the example: at the long-run break-even point an accountant sees a profit of $40,000, an economist sees zero — because the accountant leaves out $30,000 of forgone salary and the $10,000 return she could earn on her $100,000. A firm at zero economic profit stays in business. (A firm with a true economic loss is the one that exits.)
Vocabulary Trap — short-run shutdown vs. long-run exit Short run: the firm shuts down if P < AVC (fixed cost is sunk, so the firm will accept a loss as long as it covers variable cost). Long run: no cost is fixed, so a firm exits if P < ATC — it won’t accept losses forever. “Willing to lose money in the short run, not in the long run”.

The long-run supply curve (Hubbard)

Hubbard’s long-run supply curve shows the relationship between market price and quantity supplied after entry and exit have run their course. Its shape depends on what happens to the typical firm’s costs as the industry grows:

  • Constant-cost industry: input prices don’t change as the industry expands, so the typical firm’s ATC curve stays put. Long-run supply is horizontal at the minimum ATC (Hubbard’s carrot example: $10). Demand shifts move the quantity, not the long-run price.
  • Increasing-cost industry: expansion bids up the price of a limited input (Hubbard: land for a particular wine grape). Costs rise, so long-run supply slopes upward.
  • Decreasing-cost industry: expansion lowers costs (Hubbard: economies of scale in producing an input, such as a specialized memory chip). Long-run supply slopes downward.
What a demand change does in the long run (constant-cost industry) Demand rises → short run: price up along the supply curve, firms earn profits. Long run: entry shifts supply right until the price is back at its old level — more firms and a larger Q, but the same price. A demand fall works in reverse: price dips, firms take losses, exit shifts supply left until the price returns. The long-run price is set by cost (min ATC), not by demand. A cost increase for every firm (disease adds $2 a box, say) shifts the long-run supply curve up by that amount, and the long-run quantity falls.

Making the Connection — easy entry makes the long run short. Hubbard’s App Store example: with roughly 750 new games a day, a game that debuted at $2.99 was forced to $0.99 and couldn’t break even. And the cage-free eggs from Section 1: the premium shrank as farms entered. Sharon Oster’s line sums up the section: “If everyone can do it, you can’t make money at it.”

Section 8

Efficiency, Producer Surplus and Profit

Lesson 8.3 closes by tying the firm picture to the market picture from Unit 3. The firm’s supply curve is its MC curve, so the area under supply up to Q* adds up the marginal cost of every unit — that is the total variable cost. Revenue is P* × Q*, and what is left after variable cost is producer surplus:

PS = TR − VC and π = PS − FC

Producer surplus is revenue left after paying variable cost — and it is what pays the fixed cost. So profit = PS − FC. In long-run perfect competition profit is zero, so PS = FC.

Worked Example 8 — Producer Surplus Pays the Fixed Cost (Mang’s Table, P = $20, q = 5)
Using the table from Example 2 (FC = $10), find the producer surplus and profit at P = $20, q = 5, and check against Example 2.
TR = 20 × 5 = $100          VC(5) = $60
PS = TR − VC = 100 − 60 = $40
π  = PS − FC = 40 − 10  = $30   ✓  (same as Example 2)

At the long-run price (π = 0), PS = FC: producer surplus
exactly covers the fixed cost and nothing is left over.

In long-run equilibrium every firm sits at minimum ATC, so P* is the lowest sustainable price and Q* the largest sustainable quantity. Consumer surplus plus producer surplus — total surplus — is maximized. That is the sense in which perfect competition is “efficient.” Hubbard names two separate kinds of efficiency:

Productive efficiency — lowest possible cost

A good or service is produced at the lowest possible cost. Competition drives price to the minimum point on the typical firm’s ATC curve — P = min ATC — and in the long run only the consumer benefits from cost reductions (rivals copy cost-cutters and entry competes the gains away).

Allocative efficiency — the right goods in the right amounts

Production is in accordance with consumer preferences: every good is produced up to the point where the last unit’s marginal benefit to society equals its marginal cost. Hubbard’s logic: (1) the price of a good reflects the marginal benefit buyers get from the last unit; (2) competitive firms produce up to where P = MC; so (3) the last unit’s marginal benefit equals its marginal cost.

Vocabulary Trap — allocative vs. productive efficiency Both are “efficiency,” but they test different conditions. Productive = lowest cost = P = min ATC (made the cheapest way). Allocative = right goods = P = MC (made until marginal benefit = marginal cost). Perfect competition delivers both in the long run, which is why it’s the benchmark; non-competitive markets (Units 9–11) usually fail one or both. A graph that shows P = MC at a point above min ATC shows allocative efficiency only.
Memory Aid — “ALLocate = MC, PROductive = ATC” Allocative = allocating the right goods → P = MC. Productive = producing at the lowest cost → P = min ATC.
  • Direction rule: PS = TR − VC; subtract FC to get profit. PS = FC means profit is zero.
  • Trap: producer surplus is not profit — PS ignores fixed cost. A firm can have positive PS and still lose money (PS < FC).
Self-test: TR = $5,000, VC = $3,200, FC = $1,800 — PS and profit? ($1,800; $0.)
Practice

Practice Problems

Problems 1–2 drill the marginal rule and the shutdown rule, Problem 3 the five conditions, Problem 4 producer surplus and long-run adjustment. Problem 5 extends Mang’s slide table all the way to the long run.

Problem 1 — Choose Output, Then Operate or Not?

The market price is $12 and the firm’s fixed cost is $20. Its total cost for q = 0 to 6 is $20, $28, $34, $42, $54, $70, $92. (a) Find MC for each unit. (b) What quantity maximizes profit, and what is the profit or loss? (c) Should it operate or shut down?

Show answer
(a) MC = 8, 6, 8, 12, 16, 22 (each TC minus the one before).

(b) MC = $12 = P at q = 4. TR = 12 × 4 = $48, TC = $54, so π = −$6. (q = 3 also loses $6: 36 − 42 = −$6.)

(c) VC(4) = 54 − 20 = $34, so AVC(4) = 34 ÷ 4 = $8.50 < $12 → operate. Shutting down would lose the whole $20 fixed cost; operating loses only $6.
Problem 2 — Party, Patience, Pack Up

A firm’s minimum AVC is $15 and its minimum ATC is $22. What should it do in the short run at P = $25, $18 and $12? If the market price stays at $18, what happens in the long run?

Show answer
$25: above min ATC (MC crosses ATC at its minimum, so any price above $22 is above ATC at q*) → profit; operate (Party).

$18: $15 < $18 < $22 → operate at a loss; revenue covers all variable cost and part of fixed cost (Patience).

$12: below min AVC of $15 → shut down; lose only FC (Pack up).

Long run at $18: P < min ATC → losses → firms exit → supply shifts left → the price rises until it reaches $22, where the remaining firms break even.
Problem 3 — Which SHINE Condition Fails?

Name the broken condition: (a) Toronto taxi licences; (b) iPhones; (c) a used-car lot where prices are hidden; (d) a grain market with a $500 registration fee per trade. Which one of these markets is closest to perfect competition?

Show answer
(a) Easy entry — the licence is a barrier to entry.

(b) Homogeneous product — iPhones are differentiated, with brand loyalty.

(c) Perfect information — buyers can’t see prices and quality.

(d) No transaction costs — the $500 fee is an extra cost of trading.

Closest to perfect competition: Canadian wheat farming — many small farms selling an identical good with easy entry (the book’s own example type).
Problem 4 — Producer Surplus and the Long Run

A firm has TR = $5,000, VC = $3,200 and FC = $1,800. (a) Find its producer surplus and profit. (b) What does the result suggest about the market? (c) In a constant-cost industry, demand rises. What happens in the short run and the long run?

Show answer
(a) PS = TR − VC = 5,000 − 3,200 = $1,800. π = PS − FC = 1,800 − 1,800 = $0.

(b) PS = FC and π = 0 — this looks like long-run equilibrium.

(c) Short run: the price rises along the supply curve and firms earn profits. Long run: profit attracts entry, supply shifts right and the price falls back to the old level (min ATC). Result: more firms, larger Q, same price (a horizontal long-run supply curve).
Problem 5 — Mang’s Table, Extended to the Long Run

FC = $10. Variable cost for q = 1 to 6 is 10, 15, 25, 40, 60, 90 (so TC = 20, 25, 35, 50, 70, 100).
(a) Compute AVC and ATC for each q.
(b) Find the shutdown price and the break-even price.
(c) Give the firm’s supply at P = $5, $10 and $20, and explain each with MC.
(d) If the price is $20 now, what happens in the long run?
(e) If market demand at the long-run price is 30,000 units, how many firms are in the market?

Show answer
(a) AVC = VC ÷ q: 10, 7.50, 8.33, 10, 12, 15. ATC = TC ÷ q: 20, 12.50, 11.67, 12.50, 14, 16.67.

(b) Shutdown price: below minimum AVC = $7.50 (at q = 2). Break-even price: minimum ATC = $11.67 (at q = 3).

(c) MC = 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 for q = 2 to 6 (and $10 for the first unit).
P = $5: MC = 5 at q = 2, but AVC(2) = $7.50 > $5 → supplies 0 (shut down).
P = $10: MC(3) = 10 = P, and AVC(3) = $8.33 < $10 → supplies 3.
P = $20: MC(5) = 20 = P, and AVC(5) = $12 < $20 → supplies 5.

(d) At $20 each firm earns a profit of $30 (Example 2), which attracts entry. Market supply shifts right and the price falls until it reaches minimum ATC ≈ $11.67. Each firm then produces ≈ 3 units (MC = $10 ≤ 11.67 < MC(4) = $15) and π ≈ 11.67 × 3 − 35 ≈ 0.

(e) Number of firms = 30,000 ÷ 3 = 10,000 firms.
