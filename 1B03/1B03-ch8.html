Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Chapters 3 and 4 took the demand curve as given. Chapter 8 opens the box: every point on a demand curve is a consumer solving a small problem — get the most satisfaction from a limited budget. There are two lenses on that one problem. Lesson 6.1 uses marginal utility per dollar, which you can work out from a table of numbers. Lessons 6.2–6.4 use indifference curves and budget lines, which you work out on a graph. They give the same answer (Section 6 proves it). Lesson 6.5 then asks the awkward question: do real people actually behave this way?

Start with where a consumer's income can go. It can be used for consumption or for savings. To build up savings you must spend less than your current income. To consume more than your income you must dissave (run down savings) or borrow — and borrowing only moves the cost into the future, because you will have to consume less than your income later to pay the debt back.

  • Willingness to buy depends on (1) your natural desire for the product, (2) how much you value it relative to other goods, and (3) how much income you have available to spend.
  • Your available budget depends on (1) income from working and other sources, (2) your stock of savings, and (3) your ability to borrow.

Notice what “value” means here. Value is subjective — different people value the same good differently — and it is relative: it depends on how much of the good you already have and on the variety of other goods available. That second point is the seed of diminishing marginal utility, which is where we start.

Both: marginal utility and the equal-marginal rule (Sections 2–3). Section 2

Utility: Total and Marginal

Utility is the technical term for the satisfaction or happiness a consumer gets from consuming a good or service. Hubbard (8.1) adds an honest warning: utility cannot actually be measured, and one person's satisfaction cannot be compared with another's. The “utils” in our tables are only a teaching device, and the chapter's conclusions don't depend on being able to measure them.

Total utility (TU) is the utility from everything you consume, and it rises as consumption rises — write it as a utility function, u(Q). Marginal utility (MU) is the extra utility from consuming one more unit:

MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ

On a graph of u(Q), the marginal utility at any point is the slope of the curve there.

Diminishing marginal utility

As consumption increases, MU declines. With very little of a good, one more unit is very valuable (u(Q) is steep); with a lot of it, one more unit isn't (u(Q) is flat). Hubbard calls this the law of diminishing marginal utility: for nearly all goods, the more you consume in a period, the less extra satisfaction each additional unit gives.

Keep consuming and you eventually reach satiation: you have had “enough”, MU has fallen to zero, and TU is at its maximum (Umax at QS). Beyond satiation, some goods have negative marginal utility — over-consuming makes TU fall (the slice of pizza that makes you feel sick). Not every good does this: extra movie tickets just go unused, so MU = 0 and TU stays flat.

Section 3

Deciding to Buy: The Equal-Marginal Rule

When you decide on a purchase, you weigh the marginal utility you'd get against the price P you'd pay, and compare that with what else the money could buy. A consumer ends up with the highest possible utility when marginal utility per dollar is equal across all goods and the whole budget is spent.

The equal-marginal rule — two conditions

MUX ÷ PX = MUY ÷ PY and total spending = budget

At that bundle, the lecture's way of putting it: the consumer cannot make themselves any happier by changing what they buy.

Why does the rule work? Suppose MUX/PX > MUY/PY. The last dollar spent on X buys more happiness than the last dollar spent on Y, so move a dollar from Y to X. Buying more X lowers MUX (diminishing marginal utility), and buying less Y raises MUY, so the two ratios creep toward each other. Keep shifting until they are equal.

What you findWhat it meansWhat to do
MUX/PX > MUY/PYA dollar does more good on XBuy more X, less Y
MUX/PX < MUY/PYA dollar does more good on YBuy more Y, less X
MUX/PX = MUY/PY and the budget is all spentNo reshuffling can helpStop — this is the optimum

Section 4

Indifference Curves and the MRS

Now switch lenses. A consumer with income I must decide how much of good X and good Y to buy. Every combination — every bundle — of X and Y gives some level of utility, and sometimes two different bundles give exactly the same utility. An indifference curve collects all the bundles that give the same level of utility: the consumer is indifferent (equally happy) among them.

Dr. Mang's properties of indifference curves:

  • Same curve, same utility. Every point on one indifference curve gives the same utility.
  • An indifference map. Each utility level has its own curve; together they form an indifference map.
  • Higher is better. Curves farther from the origin mean higher utility, because they contain larger amounts of X and Y.
  • They never cross. The slides describe the curves as always parallel to one another; the reason they can't cross is transitivity (see “Why indifference curves never cross” below).
  • Downward-sloping and bowed in toward the origin, because of diminishing marginal utility (explained next).
Marginal rate of substitution (MRS)

The rate at which a person is willing to trade some of good Y for good X while staying equally happy: MRS = MUX ÷ MUY. The slope of the indifference curve is −MRS (negative because when one good goes up, the other must come down).

The MRS changes as you slide along a curve. With lots of X and very little Y, X isn't valuable to you (MUX is small) while Y is very valuable (MUY is large), so the MRS is low and the curve is flat. With very little X and lots of Y the MRS is high and the curve is steep. This diminishing MRS is just diminishing marginal utility seen on a graph — and it is why indifference curves bow in toward the origin.

Worked Example 3 — Reading the MRS
Two bundles lie on the same indifference curve. At bundle A, MUX = 12 and MUY = 4. At bundle B, which has more X and less Y, MUX = 6 and MUY = 6. Find the MRS at each bundle and say what it means.
Bundle A:  MRS = MU_X ÷ MU_Y = 12 ÷ 4 = 3
  → willing to give up 3 units of Y for 1 more X and stay equally happy
  → the curve's slope at A is −3 (steep: X is scarce here)

Bundle B:  MRS = 6 ÷ 6 = 1
  → willing to give up only 1 unit of Y for 1 more X
  → the curve's slope at B is −1 (flatter: X is plentiful here)

MRS falls as X rises — diminishing MRS, which is why the curve bows in.

Why indifference curves never cross

Mang's slide on transitivity draws separate utility curves for X and for Y. Moving along an indifference curve from a to b, the utility gained from the extra Y exactly offsets the utility lost from giving up some X: gain = loss, so total utility is unchanged.

Now suppose two curves did cross at bundle a, with b on one curve and c on the other. Since a ≡ b (same curve) and a ≡ c (same curve), transitivity says b ≡ c — the consumer would have to be indifferent between b and c. But c has more Y than b, so it can't be equally good. The contradiction means curves from the same consumer can never cross.

Memory Aid — “X on top” and “Scarce is steep”
  • X on top: MRS = MUX/MUY — the horizontal-axis good goes on top, the same pattern as the price ratio PX/PY you will meet in Section 5.
  • Scarce is steep: where X is scarce the curve is steep (high MRS); where X is plentiful it is flat (low MRS).
Vocabulary Trap — “parallel” vs. “never cross” The property you can prove, and the one MC questions test, is that indifference curves never cross (and that higher curves mean higher utility). An option saying two curves can intersect, or that a curve farther from the origin gives lower utility, is false. Also: “indifferent” does not mean the consumer doesn't care about the goods — it means they are equally happy with any bundle on that curve.

Section 5

The Budget Constraint

A consumer's resources are set by current income, existing savings and the ability to borrow. From those they choose an amount M to spend on goods. The limit this puts on purchases is the budget constraint. With prices PX and PY:

Budget constraint: M ≥ PXX + PYY

PXX is total spending on X and PYY is total spending on Y. The upper limit, M = PXX + PYY, is a straight line — the budget line. Spending less than M puts you below it.

Spend everything on X and you get X = M/PX (the x-intercept). Spend everything on Y and you get Y = M/PY (the y-intercept). Bundles on or below the line are affordable — together they are the opportunity set. Bundles above (outside) the line are unaffordable.

Rearrange PYY = M − PXX and you get the line in slope-intercept form:

Y = M/PY − (PX/PY)X

The intercept is M/PY and the slope is −PX/PY. That slope is the market's trade rate: to get one more X you must give up PX/PY units of Y — the opportunity cost of X.

Worked Example 4 — George's Budget Line
George has M = $100. Bowling (X) costs $20 a session and movies (Y) cost $10 a ticket. Write the budget line, find both intercepts and the slope, and classify the bundles (2, 3), (4, 5) and (3, 4), written as (bowling sessions, movie tickets).
Budget:  20X + 10Y = 100   →   Y = 10 − 2X

x-intercept:  100 ÷ 20 = 5 bowling sessions
y-intercept:  100 ÷ 10 = 10 movie tickets
slope:        −20 ÷ 10 = −2   (one bowling session costs two movie tickets)

Check the bundles:

Memory Aid — “M over its Own P” Each intercept is income divided by that axis's own price: x-intercept M/PX, y-intercept M/PY. The slope is −PX/PY — X on top again.
  • Traps: writing the slope as −PY/PX, or using PY for the x-intercept.
Two slopes, two stories The budget line's slope is about prices — the market's trade rate. The indifference curve's slope (the MRS) is about preferences — your personal trade rate. The optimum, next, is where the two rates agree.
Section 6

The Consumer's Optimum

The consumer tries to maximize utility, so the question is: which affordable bundle sits on the highest possible indifference curve?

  • Below the line is never best. A point under the budget line leaves money unspent; spending it reaches a higher indifference curve (the move from a to b in Figure 6).
  • Crossing the line isn't best either. At a point on the line where an indifference curve crosses it (b or d), there is a lens of affordable bundles lying above that curve, so sliding along the line toward e raises utility.
  • The optimum is a tangency. The best bundle is where the indifference curve just touches the budget line without crossing it (e).
Optimum: MRS = PX ÷ PY

Tangent means the two curves have the same slope: −MUX/MUY = −PX/PY, so MRS = PX ÷ PY. The rate at which the consumer is willing to substitute X for Y and keep utility the same equals the rate at which they can substitute X for Y and keep total spending the same.

Cross-multiply MUX/MUY = PX/PY and you get MUX/PX = MUY/PY — the equal-marginal rule from Section 3. Two lenses, one condition.

Worked Example 5 — Is This Bundle Optimal?
PX = $4 and PY = $2. At bundle K on the budget line, MUX = 30 and MUY = 10. Is K the optimum? If not, which way should the consumer move?
Price ratio:  P_X ÷ P_Y = 4 ÷ 2 = 2     (the market trade rate)
MRS:          MU_X ÷ MU_Y = 30 ÷ 10 = 3  (the consumer's trade rate)

MRS = 3 > 2, so K is NOT optimal.
Per dollar:   30 ÷ $4 = 7.5   >   10 ÷ $2 = 5      (same verdict)

The consumer would give up 3 units of Y for one more X, but the market
only charges 2 units of Y for it.  → Buy MORE X and LESS Y, moving down
the budget line, until MRS falls to 2 (MU_X falls and MU_Y rises on the way).
Reverse case: if MRS < P_X ÷ P_Y, buy more Y and less X.

Section 7

Changes in Income

Suppose income rises from M1 to M2. The most of each good you could buy goes up (the intercepts become M2/PX and M2/PY), but prices haven't changed, so the slope doesn't change either. The budget line makes a parallel outward shift.

If both goods are normal goods, consumption of both X and Y rises as the optimum moves from a to b. A change in consumption caused by a change in income is called an income effect. If income falls from M1 to M3 the line shifts in parallel the other way, and with normal goods both X and Y fall — also an income effect.

This links back to Chapter 3: a normal good is one you buy more of when income rises, and an inferior good is one you buy less of. Dr. Mang's diagrams assume both goods are normal. If Y were inferior, the new tangency would have less Y even though income rose.

Section 8

Changes in Price: Income and Substitution Effects

Now hold income fixed and change a price. Suppose PX rises from PX,1 to PX,2. You can't afford as much X as before, so the x-intercept falls to M/PX,2. The y-intercept M/PY doesn't move — someone who spends everything on Y and never buys X is unaffected by the price of X. So the line pivots inward around the Y-intercept and gets steeper, with slope −PX,2/PY.

If PX falls instead, to PX,3, the x-intercept rises to M/PX,3 and the line pivots outward around the same Y-intercept, getting flatter.

A price rise in X sets off two separate effects:

Income effect

Purchasing power falls, so the consumer buys less of both goods.

Substitution effect

X is now relatively more expensive, so the consumer switches: more Y, less X.

For X (as long as it is a normal good), both effects point the same way, so X falls — in every one of Mang's diagrams X2 < X1. For Y the two effects pull in opposite directions — a tug-of-war — and the winner tells you how X and Y are related:

  • Substitution effect stronger → Y rises when PX rises → X and Y are substitutes.
  • Income effect stronger → Y falls when PX rises → X and Y are complements.

For a price decrease the effects run the other way: the income effect means more of both goods, and the substitution effect means more X and less Y. If Y falls, substitution won (substitutes). If Y rises, income won (complements).

Memory Aid — “Watch the OTHER good” The own good always moves opposite its price here, so the verdict comes from Y. If Y moves in the same direction as PX → substitutes. If Y moves contrary to PX → complements. This is the same “Substitutes Same, Complements Contrary” rule you used for the Chapter 3 demand shifters.

Self-test: PX falls and the consumer buys less Y — which effect won? (Substitution → substitutes.)
Vocabulary Trap — “substitution effect” ≠ “substitutes” Every price change has a substitution effect, even when the two goods are complements. Whether the pair are substitutes or complements depends on which effect wins for Y.

The textbook's lens: the good whose price changed

Hubbard (8.2) asks a different question. Instead of what happens to the other good Y, it asks what happens to the quantity of the good whose price changed. It defines the income effect as the change in quantity demanded of a good that results from the price change altering purchasing power, with other factors held constant. It defines the substitution effect as the change in quantity demanded that results from the good becoming relatively cheaper or dearer than other goods, with the purchasing-power effect held constant.

Its example: pizza falls from $2 to $1.50 a slice (income $10, Coke $1). The old bundle of 3 slices and 4 Cokes now costs only $8.50, so the consumer has $1.50 of extra purchasing power (income effect). Pizza is also cheaper relative to Coke, which pulls them toward pizza (substitution effect). For a price decrease:

Effect of a price fallNormal goodInferior good
Income effectQuantity ↑Quantity ↓
Substitution effectQuantity ↑Quantity ↑ (always)
Net resultBoth push quantity up — the law of demandThe effects fight, but the substitution effect usually wins

For a price increase, flip every arrow. To tie the two lenses together: Mang's slides ask what happens to the other good Y, and the textbook asks what happens to the good whose price changed. Learn both.

Section 9

Special Preferences: Perfect Complements and Substitutes

The curves so far are the “ordinary” case: bowed in, with a diminishing MRS. Dr. Mang also asks about two extreme cases.

Perfect complements — L-shaped curves

The consumer needs the goods in a constant ratio (often 1:1) and gets no extra utility from more of just one. Indifference curves are right angles (L-shaped), with their corners on the ratio ray. Our examples (not the slides'): left and right shoes; a hot dog and a bun.

When income or a price changes, the consumer keeps consuming in exactly the same ratio. A price change has no substitution effect — the goods can't be swapped for each other — only an income effect.

Perfect substitutes — straight-line curves

The consumer doesn't care which one they buy. Indifference curves are straight lines because the MRS is constant.

The consumer buys only whichever good has the lower price — a corner solution. A rise in income means more of the cheaper good while the other stays at zero. They would switch completely only if the other good became cheaper. (If prices were equal, every bundle on the budget line would be equally good.)

Worked Example 8 — Hot Dogs and Buns, Coke and Pepsi
(a) Perfect complements. Sam eats exactly one bun with each hot dog. Hot dogs cost $2, buns cost $1, and Sam has $12. How many of each does he buy? Then hot dogs rise to $3. (b) Perfect substitutes. Section 10

Where Demand Curves Come From

Textbook 8.2 — not on Dr. Mang's slides, but in the reading, MyLab and the assigned homework.

Hubbard 8.2 closes the loop with Chapter 3. When a good's price falls, its MU/P rises above the MU/P of other goods, so the consumer buys more of it — until diminishing marginal utility pulls MU/P back into line. Each price therefore gives one optimal quantity, and that is one point on the consumer's demand curve. Repeat at other prices and you trace out the whole curve. For a normal good the law of demand holds because the income and substitution effects both raise quantity when the price falls.

Giffen goods

Could a demand curve ever slope up? Hubbard shows it can happen only for an inferior good whose income effect is bigger than its substitution effect. In practice that means a staple that takes a very large share of a poor household's budget. The evidence is Jensen and Miller's field experiment in China (rice in Hunan, wheat in Gansu): when subsidies made the staple cheaper, some very poor households bought less of it and more meat. The idea is named after Sir Robert Giffen, although the old story about British bread is now thought to be mistaken.

Memory Aid — “Giffen needs both I's” A Giffen good must be Inferior and have an Income effect bigger than the substitution effect (a huge budget share). Normal goods can never be Giffen.
Section 11

Social Influences on Decision Making

Textbook 8.3 — not on Dr. Mang's slides, but in the reading, MyLab and the assigned homework.

So far your utility has depended only on you and the goods you consume. Hubbard 8.3 points out that, in real life, utility often depends on what other people consume too — especially for things consumed in public, such as restaurants, sports events, clothes and cars. Three social influences are worth knowing.

Celebrity endorsements

Consumers may think a star knows the product, or buying it may make them feel fashionable or closer to the star. Hubbard's Canadian example is Connor McDavid's endorsement income (EA Sports, Canadian Tire, Adidas, CIBC, Rogers). An equipment deal suggests expertise; a bank deal is really about image. Either way, an endorsement raises marginal utility for some consumers, so demand shifts right.

Network externalities

A network externality exists when a product becomes more useful the more other people use it — phones and app platforms are the standard examples. The downsides are switching costs and path dependence: an early technology can lock in even if a better one arrives later. QWERTY keyboards and VHS vs. Betamax are often cited. Some economists see a possible market failure here; sceptics (Liebowitz and Margolis) argue there is little evidence the losing technologies were actually better. Hubbard calls the issue controversial.

Does fairness matter?

Pure self-interest says no, but people tip in restaurants they'll never visit again and give to charity. Two experiments test this:

  • Ultimatum game. An allocator proposes how to split a sum of money (Hubbard uses $20). The recipient either accepts (both keep their shares) or rejects (both get nothing). Pure self-interest predicts the allocator offers one cent and the recipient accepts. In experiments, allocators usually offer at least 40%, and offers under 10% are almost always rejected.
  • Dictator game (Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler). The allocator picks either an $18/$2 or a $10/$10 split of $20, and the recipient can't refuse. Of 161 allocators, 122 chose the even split — so fairness matters even with no risk of rejection. Caveat: lab settings are artificial, and John List's variants found far less giving when taking was allowed or when the money had been earned.

Fairness has business implications. Firms often don't raise prices even when there is excess demand — sold-out concerts and games, popular university places. Hubbard gives two explanations:

  • Becker: part of the appeal is being seen at a popular event, and a high price could kill the buzz.
  • Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler: price rises driven by higher costs are seen as fair; ones driven by higher demand are seen as unfair (82% called raising a snow shovel from $15 to $20 after a storm unfair).

Firms may therefore give up some short-run profit to keep customers' goodwill. Hubbard's example is Hamilton: the producers underpriced tickets while resellers captured most of the gain.

Memory Aid — the three social influences: “Stars, Networks, Fairness” Stars = who endorses it. Networks = how many others use it. Fairness = whether the price feels fair.

Behavioural Economics I: Biases and Risk

The model in Sections 2–10 makes the assumption conventional (neo-classical) economics makes: individuals act rationally, with perfect information and foresight. In reality people have incomplete information, cognitive limitations, shifting preferences, and they make mistakes — systematic ones. Behavioural economics tries to address this (Lesson 6.5).

The two approaches are complements, not rivals: each describes different behaviour. Buying staple goods is planned (neo-classical); impulse buying is not. Studying hard fits one model, skipping class the other.

Heuristics and cognitive biases

Our brains use short-cuts, called heuristics. We recognize patterns from experience and act automatically instead of recalculating from scratch. Because pattern recognition is automatic, brains can be tricked. A cognitive bias is a mental failure to fully process all available information, caused by using heuristics to simplify decisions. The slides list seven:

Behavioural economics and uncertainty

The week's lecture adds that preferences aren't always stable and can be context dependent (they change with mood), that people can be impulsive, and that they have incomplete information. People also respond to uncertainty differently, and that gives three attitudes to risk:

  • Risk-averse: low tolerance for risk; avoids situations where outcomes are uncertain.
  • Risk-neutral: some tolerance, as long as the expected benefits of a risky action are at least as large as its costs (≥).
  • Risk-loving: high tolerance; accepts situations where the costs are higher than the expected benefits.

Section 13

Behavioural Economics II: The Textbook's Three Mistakes

Textbook 8.4 — not on Dr. Mang's slides, but in the reading, MyLab and the assigned homework.

Hubbard defines behavioural economics as the study of situations in which people make choices that don't seem economically rational, where rational means taking actions appropriate to your goals given the information you have. It then picks out three mistakes consumers commonly make.

Mistake 1: counting monetary costs but ignoring nonmonetary opportunity costs

Opportunity cost (Chapter 2) is the highest-valued alternative you give up. Hubbard's example is Super Bowl ticket-lottery winners: 94% said they would not pay $3,000 for a ticket, yet 92% also would not sell theirs for $3,000. That is inconsistent, because keeping the ticket costs you the $3,000 you could have had. The cause is the endowment effect: people are unwilling to sell something they already own, even for more than they would pay to buy it if they didn't own it. A related slip: many buyers never mail in their mail-in rebates, because the effort is a nonmonetary cost they ignored.

Mistake 2: failing to ignore sunk costs

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and can't be recovered, so it should be ignored in later decisions. Suppose you hold a $75 non-refundable play ticket but a friend offers a free comedy-club night on the same evening. The $75 is gone either way, so pick whichever you'd enjoy more. In a Kahneman–Tversky study, people who “lost a $10 ticket” were far less willing to buy another (46%) than people who “lost a $10 bill” were to buy a ticket (88%) — although the two situations are economically identical.

Mistake 3: being unrealistic about future behaviour

People overvalue the present and expect their future selves to be more disciplined than they will be: delaying studying, overeating, smoking, buying gym memberships they barely use. The fix is to be realistic about what you'll actually do.

Nudges, rules of thumb and anchoring

  • Nudges (popularized by Richard Thaler, 2017 Nobel): small changes in how choices are presented that steer people toward better decisions without removing options — for example, automatic enrolment in a retirement-savings plan with the right to opt out.
  • Rules of thumb: with limited time and information, people use simple rules (keep shopping at the store that used to be cheapest). They're easier, but not always optimal.
  • Anchoring: tying an uncertain value to an irrelevant number you've just seen. In the Tversky–Kahneman wheel-of-fortune study, a wheel landing on 10 vs. 65 shifted guesses of the share of African countries in the UN from 25% to 45%. Stores show a high “regular price” next to the sale price, and a “limit of 12 per customer” sign lifted soup sales to about 7 cans per shopper versus about 3.3 with no limit.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Marginal Utility

Cups of coffee 0–6 give total utility 0, 30, 50, 62, 68, 68, 64. (a) Find the marginal utility of each cup. (b) Where does diminishing marginal utility start? (c) Where is satiation? (d) Is there any negative marginal utility?

Show answer
(a) MU = 30, 20, 12, 6, 0, −4 (each TU minus the one before).

(b) Diminishing MU starts with the 2nd cup (30 → 20) and continues.

(c) TU peaks at 68, reached with 4 cups; the 5th cup adds 0. That is satiation.

(d) Yes. The 6th cup has MU = −4, so TU falls to 64: negative marginal utility (over-consumption).
Problem 2 — Budget Line

M = $60, PX = $5, PY = $3. (a) Find both intercepts. (b) Write the budget line and its slope. (c) Classify the bundles (6, 10), (8, 8) and (3, 12). (d) PX falls to $4 — what happens to the line?

Show answer
(a) X-intercept = 60 ÷ 5 = 12; Y-intercept = 60 ÷ 3 = 20.

(b) Y = 20 − (5/3)X, slope = −5/3 ≈ −1.67.

(c) (6, 10): 30 + 30 = $60 → on the line. (8, 8): 40 + 24 = $64 → unaffordable. (3, 12): 15 + 36 = $51 → affordable, $9 left over.

(d) The x-intercept becomes 60 ÷ 4 = 15, the y-intercept stays at 20, and the slope becomes −4/3. The line pivots outward around the Y-intercept and gets flatter.
Problem 3 — Optimal or Not?

PX = $6 and PY = $2. On the budget line, at a bundle where MUX = 12 and MUY = 3: (a) find the MU per dollar of each good. (b) Compare the MRS with the price ratio. (c) What should the consumer do, and what happens to MUX and MUY as they do it?

Show answer
(a) X: 12 ÷ 6 = 2. Y: 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5.

(b) MRS = 12 ÷ 3 = 4, which is greater than PX/PY = 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

(c) Buy more X and less Y. MUX falls and MUY rises until MUX/PX = MUY/PY (MRS = 3).
Problem 4 — Which Effect Won?

(a) The price of tea falls; Jordan buys more tea and fewer coffees. (b) The price of tennis balls rises; Alex buys fewer balls and books fewer court hours. (c) Left shoes double in price (perfect complements with right shoes): how large is the substitution effect? In (a) and (b), say which effect won and what the pair is.

Show answer
(a) Coffee fell when tea got cheaper → the substitution effect was stronger → tea and coffee are substitutes.

(b) Court hours fell when balls got dearer → the income effect was stronger → balls and court hours are complements.

(c) Zero. Perfect complements have no substitution effect. The number of pairs falls but the ratio stays 1:1 — a pure income effect.
Problem 5 — Name the Textbook Mistake

Name the mistake (or bias) in each case. (a) You won a concert ticket and refuse $300 for it, but you'd never have paid $300 to buy one. (b) You sit through a terrible movie because “I paid $15 for this.” (c) You buy an annual gym pass expecting three visits a week and go twice a month. (d) A grocery sign reads “Limit 12 per customer” and shoppers buy far more than usual.

Show answer
(a) Endowment effect — ignoring the $300 nonmonetary opportunity cost of keeping the ticket.

(b) Failing to ignore a sunk cost — the $15 is gone whether you stay or leave.

(c) Being unrealistic about future behaviour.

(d) Anchoring — the number 12 pulls up how many people buy.
Problem 6 — Building Market Demand

Slices of pizza per week at $5 / $4 / $3 / $2: Dev buys 1, 2, 2, 3; Emma buys 0, 1, 2, 4; Farid buys 2, 3, 4, 5. (a) Build the market demand schedule. (b) Use MU/P to explain why each person buys more when the price falls. (c) What would have to be true for a price cut to reduce someone's quantity?

Show answer
(a) At $5, $4, $3, $2 the market buys 3, 6, 8, 12 slices (for example $4: 2 + 1 + 3 = 6).

(b) A lower price raises pizza's MU/P above the MU/P of other goods, so each person buys more pizza until diminishing marginal utility restores equality. For a normal good, the income and substitution effects both raise quantity.

(c) Pizza would have to be a Giffen good: inferior, with an income effect bigger than its substitution effect — very unlikely for pizza.
