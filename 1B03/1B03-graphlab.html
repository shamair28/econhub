Section 0

The One Recipe: Cross → Climb → Compare → Box

Every firm graph in Units 8–11 answers the same three questions in the same order: how much to produce, what price to charge, and how much profit results. The curves change shape from unit to unit, but the recipe never does.

CurveColour (as on Dr. Mang’s slides)What it tells you
d / D — demand greenThe price buyers will pay for each quantity. Prices are always read here.
MR — marginal revenue light blueExtra revenue from one more unit. Same as d for a price taker; below d for everyone else.
MC — marginal cost purpleExtra cost of one more unit. With MR it decides the quantity.
ATC — average total cost redCost per unit. Compared with P, it decides profit or loss.
AVC — average variable cost orangeVariable cost per unit. Compared with P, it decides whether to shut down.
Memory Aid — “Cross, Climb, Compare, Box”
  • Cross: q comes from where the marginals cross (MR = MC).
  • Climb: P comes from going straight up to the demand curve — never from the MR or MC height.
  • Compare: P vs ATC at that q → profit or loss. (P vs AVC → operate or shut down.)
  • Box: profit or loss is a rectangle: (P − ATC) × q. Surpluses (CS, PS, DWL) are the triangles and other areas between demand, MC and the price.
Self-test: in a graph where MR = MC at a height of $420 and the demand curve above that point is at $560, what is the price? ($560 — climb to demand.)
The three most common graph mistakes (1) Reading the price at the MR = MC height. (2) Drawing profit as the area between demand and ATC (it’s a rectangle at one q). (3) Shifting MC when only a fixed cost changed (fixed costs move ATC and AFC only).
Section 1 · Unit 7 (Ch. 9)

Production and Costs

Cost curves come from the production function. In the short run at least one input (the plant) is fixed, so adding workers eventually runs into diminishing marginal returns: each extra worker adds less output. Because MC = wage ÷ MP, falling MP means rising MC. That’s why MC is U-shaped, and why every average-cost curve it cuts is U-shaped too.

The four per-unit curves

AFC = FC ÷ q falls forever (the same fixed cost spread over more units). AVC = VC ÷ q and ATC = TC ÷ q = AVC + AFC are U-shaped, and the gap between them is AFC, which shrinks as q grows. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums: when the marginal is below the average it pulls the average down; above it, it pulls it up.

Memory Aid — “Fixed Fiddles with the Averages; Variable Visits them All”
  • Fixed cost changes (rent, licence fee, insurance) → AFC and ATC move; MC and AVC don’t.
  • Variable cost changes (wages, materials, a per-unit tax, technology) → MC, AVC and ATC move; AFC doesn’t.
  • Trap: MC crosses ATC at ATC’s minimum, not at MC’s own minimum. The order from left to right is: min MC → min AVC → min ATC.
Self-test: in the lab above, which three minimums occur at q = 40, 60 and about 70? (MC, AVC, ATC.)
Section 2 · Unit 8 (Ch. 10)

Perfect Competition

The market’s supply and demand set the price; each tiny firm takes it. So the firm’s demand curve is the horizontal line d = MR = P, and the recipe’s first two steps collapse into one: produce where P = MC. Change market demand or the number of firms in the lab and watch the firm respond. Then press Run to the long run to see entry or exit push the price to the bottom of ATC.

Memory Aid — “Party, Patience, Pack up”
  • Party (P > min ATC): profit → in the long run, firms enter, supply shifts right, P falls.
  • Patience (min AVC < P < min ATC): loss, but keep operating; you cover all variable cost and some fixed cost. Long run: firms exit, P rises.
  • Pack up (P < min AVC): shut down and lose only FC.
  • Supply: the firm’s supply curve is its MC above min AVC (tick “Trace the firm’s supply curve”).
Self-test: what are P, profit and q in long-run equilibrium here? (P = min ATC ≈ $39.29, π = 0, q ≈ 70.)
Section 3 · Unit 9 (Ch. 11)

Monopolistic Competition

Many firms, easy entry — but each sells a differentiated product (a restaurant, a hair salon, a clothing brand). Having its own customers gives the firm a downward-sloping demand curve, so MR lies below it and the firm uses the full recipe: MR = MC, then climb to d. The lab starts from Dr. Mang’s restaurant (P = 32 − 0.2q, MC = 0.4q, ATC = 300/q + 0.2q → q = 40, P = $24, π = $340). Short-run profit attracts copycats. Each entrant takes customers and adds substitutes, so the firm’s demand shifts left and becomes more elastic, until it just touches ATC. Full write-up: the Chapter 11 lesson.

Long-run equilibrium: the tangency

d is tangent to ATC at the profit-maximizing q → P = ATC, zero economic profit (like perfect competition). But because d slopes down, the tangency is on the falling part of ATC: P > MC (a markup, so some deadweight loss) and q < the minimum-ATC output (excess capacity). Hubbard’s defence: consumers get variety, which they value.

Vocabulary Trap — zero economic profit ≠ zero accounting profit In the long run the restaurant still makes an accounting profit. It just earns no more than the owner could get from the next-best use of their time and money (a normal return). That’s why it stays open.
Section 4 · Unit 10 (Ch. 12)

Oligopoly and Game Theory

A few firms, protected by barriers to entry (Dr. Mang’s four: economies of scale, government regulations, limited ownership of a resource, operating history and brand). With only a few rivals there is mutual interdependence: each firm’s best move depends on what the others do, so the main “graph” for oligopoly is a payoff matrix. A best response is the best choice given a conjecture about the rival; a dominant strategy is best no matter what the rival does; a Nash equilibrium is where both play best responses. The matrix lab opens on Dr. Mang’s Advertising Game; his Front Page Game is one click away. Full write-up: the Chapter 12 lesson.

Memory Aid — “Circle your best, find the double”
  • For each rival choice, circle your higher payoff (Mang’s circles). If the same choice is circled in both cases, that’s your dominant strategy.
  • The cell with both payoffs circled is a Nash equilibrium; if both players got there with dominant strategies, it’s also a dominant strategy equilibrium.
  • Prisoner’s dilemma: both have a dominant strategy, and the resulting equilibrium is worse for both than cooperating. That’s why collusion is unstable.
Self-test: in the Advertising Game, why don’t both firms stay at a Low budget? (Each earns $12m instead of $10m by going High, whatever the other does.)
Section 5 · Unit 11 (Ch. 13)

Monopoly and Regulation

One firm, no close substitutes, entry blocked: the firm’s demand is the market demand (d = D). Same recipe again. It opens on Dr. Mang’s slide example (P = 700 − 2q, MC = 6q, ATC = 8,000/q + 3q → q = 70, P = $560, π = $16,500). Then switch on each policy and watch which curve moves. The full write-up is in the Chapter 13 lesson.

Memory Aid — what each policy moves
  • Licence fee → ATC only → same q and P; profit ↓ by the fee.
  • Per-unit tax → MC ↑ → q ↓, P ↑, DWL ↑.
  • Price ceiling between P = MC and PM → MR becomes flat at Pmax → q ↑, CS ↑, DWL ↓. At P = MC it’s competitive. Below that: shortage.
  • Natural monopoly → P = MC loses money → regulators / SOEs set P = ATC.
  • Perfect price discrimination → demand = MR → efficient q, CS = 0, DWL = 0.
Self-test: try a $480 ceiling on Mang’s example — why is there a shortage? (It’s below the competitive price of $525; the firm stops where MC = $480.)
Section 6

All Four Structures Side by Side

Dr. Mang’s Lesson 11.2 ties the units together. The fewer close substitutes a firm’s product has, the less elastic (steeper) its demand. That means a bigger gap between MR and demand, a bigger markup over MC, and a bigger deadweight loss. Slide from many substitutes to none.
