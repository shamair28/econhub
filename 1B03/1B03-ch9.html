Section 1

Why Costs Drive Everything

Until now the supply curve was a given. Chapter 9 opens the firm’s box. Hubbard puts it this way: we look behind the supply curve to understand how firms decide, and the reason supply curves slope upward is that marginal cost increases as a firm produces more. Everything later in the course (Units 8–11, the different market structures) uses the same cost curves you build in this chapter and adds a revenue side to them. The one-line preview is profit = total revenue − total cost, so you cannot find the best output until you know the cost of every output.

The chapter has four pieces, which match Dr. Mang’s four lessons in Unit 7:

  • Lesson 7.1 — business organizations and profit (Section 2): who owns and finances a firm, and what “profit” should mean for a decision-maker.
  • Lesson 7.2 — production function and productivity (Sections 3–4): how inputs turn into output in the short run, and why extra workers add less and less.
  • Lesson 7.3 — total cost and efficiency (Sections 5–6): the short-run cost curves, then what happens to cost when the firm can change everything.
  • Lesson 7.4 — input choice in the long run (Section 7, which is Appendix 9A in the textbook): the cheapest mix of labour and capital.
Making the Connection — why MOOCs change the economics of a university Hubbard opens the chapter with online courses. A traditional university has large fixed costs (buildings, instructor and administrator salaries) that do not change with enrolment, and an extra student costs very little until classrooms fill up, then a lot. A MOOC has a high fixed cost to build the course, but the marginal cost of one more student is close to zero. That is why Georgia Tech could offer an online master’s in computer science (2017) at about $510 for a three-credit course, roughly a tenth of the on-campus price. The whole chapter is about this contrast between a cost you pay once and a cost you pay per unit.

Both: fixed/variable, short/long run, AP and MP, and the cost curves. Appendix 9A (isoquants and isocosts) is in both the lecture and the book. Section 2

Business Organizations, Financing, and Economic Profit

Business forms and financing — From Dr. Mang’s Lesson 7.1 (not in the textbook)

Lecture-only material Hubbard’s Canadian edition has no chapter or appendix on types of business organization, so everything in this subsection comes from Dr. Mang’s Lesson 7.1 slides. Expect it on the lecture side of the test, not in MyLab or the textbook homework.

A firm needs money to start and grow. There are two ways to raise it:

Debt financing

Borrowing: loans, mortgages, bonds, lines of credit. The firm pays interest and eventually repays the principal.

Equity financing

Selling ownership: shares. The money is never repaid; investors expect the firm to earn profits over time and share in them.

The three for-profit forms differ mainly in who owns the firm, who is personally liable for its debts, and how much equity it can raise.

FormOwnershipLiabilityAdvantagesDisadvantages
Sole proprietorshipOne owner who is also the managerOwner is personally responsible for all debts (unlimited personal liability)Easy to set up; profit taxed once, at the owner’s personal rateEquity limited to the owner’s own money; hard to borrow
PartnershipTwo or more ownersGeneral partners are personally liable; limited partners may not beEasy to set up; more equity than one person, so easier to borrow; taxed onceEquity limited to the partners’ money; personal liability for general partners
CorporationLegally distinct from its owners (shareholders); directors appoint the managersLimited liability: owners can lose only what they investedFar more equity can be raised, so easier to take on debtComplex legal and regulatory rules; a separate corporate tax system

Profit: accounting vs. economic

Firms try to maximise profit. But investors choose where to put their money by comparing the expected profit with the capital they tie up and the risk they take, that is, by the rate of return (profit as a percentage of capital). That is why a measure of profit that ignores the cost of capital is not enough for an investment decision.

Accounting profit

Revenue minus the firm’s input costs only. It is what appears on financial statements and what taxes are based on.

Economic profit: π = accounting profit − risk-adjusted expected return on capital

The expected return on capital is the opportunity cost of capital: what the same money would earn in an equally risky alternative. Economic profit is the profit we care about for investment decisions.

  • π > 0: actual accounting profit beats what investors expected.
  • π = 0: the firm earns exactly what investors expected (a normal profit).
  • π < 0: either revenue is below input cost (an accounting loss), or accounting profit is positive but smaller than the expected return.
Worked Example 1 — Priya’s Café
Priya invests $200,000 of equity in a café. Investors in similarly risky businesses expect an 8% return. Her revenue is $500,000 and her input costs are $470,000. Find her accounting profit and economic profit. Then redo it if her accounting profit had only been $10,000.
Expected return on capital = 8% × $200,000          = $16,000

  → she beat what investors expected, so the café is worth running.

Memory Aid — “Economic profit charges you rent on your own money” Economic = Accounting − Expected return on the money you put in.
  • π > 0 → beat expectations. π = 0 → exactly met them (a normal profit, not a failure). π < 0 → fell short, even if the books show a profit.
  • Trap: “the firm made a profit, so economic profit is positive” is false. Subtract the capital charge first.
Self-test: revenue $100, input costs $90, investors expect $15 on the capital. Accounting profit $10, economic profit −$5.
Vocabulary Trap — accounting profit ≠ economic profit Zero economic profit does not mean zero accounting profit: at π = 0 the firm can still report a healthy accounting profit, as long as it equals the expected return. Likewise a firm with a positive accounting profit can have a negative economic profit. “Profit” on its own, in this course, means economic profit unless you are told otherwise.

The textbook’s version: explicit and implicit costs (Hubbard §9.2)

Hubbard reaches the same idea from the cost side. Economists always measure cost as opportunity cost, the highest-valued alternative given up. An explicit cost involves spending money (these are the “accounting costs”). An implicit cost is a nonmonetary opportunity cost. So economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost. Accountants record only explicit costs; economists count both. Priya’s $16,000 above is an implicit cost: the return her $200,000 could have earned elsewhere.

Hubbard’s running example is Jill Johnson’s pizza restaurant, with costs for one year:

Explicit costsAmountImplicit costsAmount
Pizza dough, tomato sauce, other ingredients$20,000Forgone salary (she earned $30,000 managing someone else’s restaurant)$30,000
Wages$48,000Forgone interest on the $50,000 she took out of her bank account$3,000

Total explicit$108,000Total implicit$43,000

Her accountant sees $108,000 of costs; the economist sees $108,000 + $43,000 = $151,000. Economic depreciation is the fall in the value of her capital over the year: equipment bought for $50,000 that would resell for $40,000 has depreciated by $10,000 (only that $10,000 is a cost, because she still owns the rest).

Section 3

Inputs, the Short Run and the Long Run

Hubbard (§9.1) defines technology as the processes a firm uses to turn inputs into outputs of goods and services. It is broader than machinery: it includes managers’ skills, worker training and the layout of the workplace. A technological change is a change in the output a firm can produce from a given quantity of inputs (or the inputs it needs for a given output). It is positive if the firm gets more output from the same inputs, and negative if it gets less (hiring less-skilled workers, or a hurricane damaging a plant). The textbook’s example is Segment.com, a software firm whose engineers complained about noise. After the CEO had them measure sound levels, the firm moved the engineers to the quietest part of the office and sales to the loudest. Output rose as if it had hired 10 to 15 more workers, with no new hardware.

The link between inputs and output is the production function. Hubbard defines it as the relationship between a firm’s inputs and the maximum output it can produce with them (it “represents the firm’s technology”). With capital K and labour L:

Production function: q = f(K, L)

q is the quantity produced, the total product. In the lecture, inputs are sorted by whether the firm can change them in a given period.

Input typeMeaning (Mang)Examples
Fixed inputNeeded in a fixed amount, often before production starts; not used up by productionFactories, offices, equipment; managers and salaried administrators are often fixed too
Variable inputUsed up in production; rises with outputHourly workers, fuel, materials

Hubbard makes the same split on the cost side. Fixed costs stay constant as output changes (a lease on factory space, fire insurance, newspaper and TV advertising), and variable costs change with output (labour, raw materials, electricity). In the book’s publishing example, a medium-size academic publisher’s fixed costs total $750,000 a year, mostly salaries and benefits of editors, designers and managers who work on several books at once. For a firm that prints books, the press operators are a variable cost.

Short run, long run, very long run

Short run: at least one input (capital K) is fixed; labour L is variable. Long run: K and L are both variable. Very long run: new technology arrives, so the firm gets more output from the same inputs.

How long is “the long run”? It depends on how long it takes to change the capital. Dr. Mang’s examples: a computer (a day), a new machine (weeks or months), a new factory (years). Hubbard’s are similar: a pizza parlour might add an oven and tables in a few weeks, while General Motors needs more than a year to add assembly-plant capacity. The fixed/variable split is purely a short-run idea: in the long run every cost is variable, so total cost equals variable cost and ATC equals AVC.

Memory Aid — “Short run: Something’s stuck. Long run: Lots can change. Very long run: new Lab tech.” Something’s stuck (capital) · Lots can change (K and L) · Lab tech (new technology, R&D).
  • Short run → K fixed, only L moves. Long run → K and L both move. Very long run → the production function itself improves.
  • Trap: the short run is not “a few months” or “one year”. It is defined by whether capital can be changed. For a small business it might be a week; for a car maker, years.
Self-test: a bakery can hire more staff today but cannot add an oven for six months. Which run is it in? (Short run.)
Vocabulary Trap — Mang’s “long run” vs. Hubbard’s “long run” Hubbard’s long run is the period in which a firm can vary all its inputs and adopt new technology. Dr. Mang splits that in two: the long run (K and L variable, technology given) and the very long run (technology changes). If a question mentions R&D or a better production process, Mang’s label is the very long run. If it says only that the firm can build a bigger plant, it is the long run in both sources.
Section 4

Productivity: Average and Marginal Product of Labour

In the short run the firm changes output by changing labour only. Two measures tell you how productive that labour is:

Average and marginal product of labour

APL = q ÷ L (output per unit of labour)  ·  MPL = Δq ÷ ΔL (the extra output from one more unit of labour).

At first, extra workers are more productive: they specialise, share tasks and learn from each other, so MPL rises. Adam Smith’s pin factory (Hubbard’s example) divided pin-making into 18 operations; an average worker made about 4,800 pins a day, against about 20 pins for a worker doing every task alone. But with capital fixed, crowding sets in, the marginal worker is less productive, and MPL falls. This is the law of diminishing returns: at some point, adding more of a variable input to a fixed input makes the marginal product of the variable input decline. Past that, MPL can even reach zero or go negative (too many workers in each other’s way, or one disruptive worker).

The MP–AP rule

MPL > APL → APL is rising. MPL < APL → APL is falling. MPL = APL at the maximum of APL (so the MP curve cuts the AP curve at AP’s peak). Hubbard puts it as: the average product is the average of the marginal products.

Memory Aid — “Marginal pulls the average” Your next test mark pulls your grade-point average toward itself: a mark above your average raises it, a mark below lowers it, and a mark equal to it leaves it unchanged. Hubbard uses exactly this (a student’s semester GPA is the marginal, the cumulative GPA is the average), and another picture: a 195 cm person entering a room whose average height is 160 cm raises the average.
  • The rule applies in all four places you will use it: MPL and APL (this section), and MC with AVC and ATC (next section).
  • Trap: the average can keep rising while the marginal is already falling (Figure 1: between L = 20 and L = 30, MP falls from 120 to 90 while AP is still climbing to 90). It only turns down once the marginal drops below the average.
Self-test: if your semester GPA is 3.0 and your cumulative GPA is 3.4, which way does the cumulative GPA move? (Down.)
Vocabulary Trap — “diminishing marginal product” ≠ “falling total product” Diminishing returns means MPL is falling. Total product keeps rising as long as MPL > 0 (Figure 1: from L = 20 to L = 40). Total product falls only when MPL is negative, a further stage no profit-maximising firm would choose. Also, the first workers’ MPL rises because of specialisation, not because they are better workers (Hubbard: same pay, same quality, different productivity because of the fixed capital).
Section 5

Short-Run Costs

The short-run cost concepts all come from three building blocks. With capital fixed, fixed cost (FC) is the cost of the fixed inputs and does not change with output; variable cost (VC) is the cost of the variable inputs; and total cost is their sum. Dividing by output gives the per-unit versions, and the marginal version looks at the cost of the extra units.

ConceptFormulaHow it behaves
Total costTC = FC + VCRises with output; the gap between TC and VC is always FC
Average fixed costAFC = FC ÷ qAlways falls (the fixed cost is spread over more units)
Average variable costAVC = VC ÷ qU-shaped
Average total costATC = TC ÷ q = AFC + AVCU-shaped; the gap ATC − AVC is AFC, so it shrinks as q rises
Marginal costMC = ΔTC ÷ ΔqFalls while productivity rises, then rises (U-shaped); fixed cost does not affect it
Profit per unitπ ÷ q = P − ATCPositive when the price is above ATC

Why are MC, AVC and ATC U-shaped? Because productivity first rises and then falls. While MPL is rising, each extra unit of output needs less extra labour, so MC falls; once diminishing returns set in, each extra unit needs more labour, so MC rises. The averages follow the marginal by the same “marginal pulls the average” rule.

The MC rule

MC < average → the average falls. MC > average → the average rises. MC = average where the average is at its minimum. This holds for both AVC and ATC, so the MC curve cuts each of them at their lowest point, coming up from below.

Figures 2 and 3 use one smooth cost function so you can see all the curves together: FC = $1,000 and VC = 0.01q³ − 1.2q² + 60q. Slide along the horizontal axis in Figure 3 and the pattern is the same every time: MC reaches its minimum first (at q = 40, $12), then AVC reaches its minimum (at q = 60, $24, exactly where MC crosses it), and then ATC reaches its minimum last (at q ≈ 70, about $39.29, where MC crosses it again). ATC bottoms out later than AVC because AFC is still falling when AVC starts to rise.

Section 6

Returns to Scale and Long-Run Costs

In the long run there is no fixed input, so every cost is variable and the firm can choose the plant size that suits its output. Two things follow. First, the firm’s long-run average cost is never above its short-run ATC at the same output (Mang: LATC ≤ SATC), because in the long run it has more options. Second, Hubbard’s long-run average cost curve (LRAC) shows the lowest cost at which the firm can make each quantity when no inputs are fixed. It is the lower envelope of the short-run ATC curves, one per plant size (Figure 5).

The two sources name the three parts of the cost curve differently:

Average cost is…Dr. Mang (Lesson 7.3, on the ATC)Hubbard (§9.6, on the LRAC)What it means
FallingIncreasing returns to scaleEconomies of scaleEach extra unit is cheaper; doubling cost more than doubles output; with a fixed price, profit per unit rises
Flat (at the minimum)Constant returns to scaleConstant returns to scaleEach extra unit costs the same; doubling cost exactly doubles output; profit per unit is constant
RisingDecreasing returns to scaleDiseconomies of scaleEach extra unit is dearer; doubling cost less than doubles output; profit per unit falls

Most ATC curves have an increasing-returns part and a decreasing-returns part, separated by constant returns at the minimum. Dr. Mang labels the minimum of the short-run ATC Minimum Efficient Scale. Hubbard defines minimum efficient scale (MES) on the LRAC: the level of output at which all economies of scale are exhausted, the left end of the flat segment. A firm producing below MES may struggle to survive, because competitors at MES have lower average costs.

Hubbard’s automobile example uses numbers: a small car maker selling 20,000 cars a year builds a small plant with a lowest average cost of $52,000 per car; a big maker producing 200,000 cars can get the cost down to $27,000 (economies of scale). Between 200,000 and 400,000 cars the average cost stays the same (constant returns; a 200,000-car plant has reached MES). Above 400,000 it rises (diseconomies of scale). The book’s Solved Problem on Pon Holdings (Santa Cruz bikes) buying Accell Group (Raleigh) uses this logic: Pon sold about 800,000 bicycles and Accell about 1.5 million (2.3 million together), so for Pon to expect a benefit it must have been below MES, moving down its LRAC.

Why costs fall, then rise

Hubbard gives four reasons for economies of scale:

  1. Technology: the firm can raise production with a smaller proportional increase in at least one input (Ford produces about 10 times as many cars as Tesla but needs only about 6 times as many workers).
  2. Specialisation: workers and managers can become more specialised, and so more productive, as output grows.
  3. Bulk buying: large firms (Ford, Walmart, Apple) can buy inputs more cheaply than small rivals.
  4. Borrowing: a larger firm may be able to borrow at a lower interest rate.

For diseconomies of scale the book gives one main reason: managers have difficulty coordinating a very large operation. Its example is Ford’s River Rouge plant (93 structures, about 75,000 workers), which was too big to manage. Ford fixed the problem by building smaller factories spread across the US.

Memory Aid — “Big Buyers Trim Spending” (B-B-T-S) The four reasons for economies of scale: Bulk buying · Borrowing more cheaply · Technology (output grows faster than inputs) · Specialisation.
  • Direction rule: output up → LRAC down while any of the four applies. The flat part appears when they run out.
  • Diseconomies have ONE reason: management and coordination problems. Do not add “workers get lazy”.
  • Trap: the four reasons are about size of the firm (long run), not about adding workers to a fixed plant.
Self-test: cover the box and name all four reasons, then the one reason for diseconomies.
Vocabulary Trap — three phrases that sound alike Diminishing marginal returns (short run): adding more labour to a fixed plant, so MPL falls and MC rises. Decreasing returns to scale / diseconomies of scale (long run): all inputs scaled up and the average cost per unit rises. They are unrelated: one explains why the MC curve slopes up, the other why the LRAC slopes up. Then there are two uses of “scale”: Dr. Mang applies “increasing / constant / decreasing returns to scale” to the falling / flat / rising parts of the (short-run) ATC and calls its bottom “Minimum Efficient Scale”, while Hubbard uses “economies / constant / diseconomies of scale” and MES for the LRAC. On the exam, answer in the vocabulary the question uses; the underlying shape is the same.

Economies of scope, learning-by-doing and technology (lecture)

  • Economies of scope: producing several goods together lowers the cost per unit (shared equipment, shared staff).
  • Learning-by-doing: as a firm accumulates experience, workers and managers find better ways to produce, so unit cost falls.
  • Very long run: technological progress (often from R&D) shifts the whole ATC curve down; the firm produces the same output for less.
Why long-run cost is never above short-run cost Hubbard’s review question asks why short-run average cost can never be below long-run average cost for the same output. The answer is the envelope: in the long run the firm can pick the best plant for each output, so the LRAC is the lowest of all the short-run curves. At best the two are equal, at the output the plant was built for.
Section 7

Choosing Inputs in the Long Run: Isoquants and Isocosts

In the long run a firm can make the same output with different mixes of capital K and labour L: a capital-intensive technology (many machines, few workers) or a labour-intensive one (few machines, many workers). Which is cheapest depends on technology and on input prices. The tools are the firm’s versions of the indifference curve and the budget line from Chapter 8. By Hubbard’s convention, capital is on the vertical axis and labour on the horizontal.

Consumer (Chapter 8)Firm (this section)
Indifference curve: same utilityIsoquant: same quantity of output
Budget line: same spendingIsocost line: same cost
MRS = MUX ÷ MUYMRTS = MPL ÷ MPK
Optimum: MUX/PX = MUY/PYOptimum: MPL/w = MPK/r
Isoquant and MRTS

An isoquant (“same quantity”) collects every K–L combination that produces the same output q1. A higher isoquant, farther from the origin, means more output. Its slope is −MRTS, where the marginal rate of technical substitution is the rate at which the firm can swap one input for the other and keep output the same: MRTS = MPL ÷ MPK. Diminishing marginal products give the isoquant its bowed-in shape. Hubbard’s example: Jill can make 5,000 pizzas a week with 10 workers and 2 ovens, or with 6 workers and 3 ovens.

Isocost line: C = wL + rK

An isocost line (“same cost”) collects every K–L combination that costs the same total C, where w is the wage and r is the rental price of capital. Its slope is −w/r (the rate at which the firm can trade capital for labour at constant cost). A higher isocost line, farther from the origin, means a higher cost.

Cost minimisation: MRTS = w/r ⇔ MPL/w = MPK/r

The cheapest way to produce a given output is where the isocost line is tangent to the isoquant: the rate the firm is able to substitute (MRTS) equals the rate it has to substitute (w/r). Equivalently, the extra output per dollar is equal across labour and capital: a firm should hire inputs until the last dollar spent on each adds the same output.

Worked Example 4 — Three Ways to Make 40 Units (Tangency)
A firm’s production function is q = 2√(L·K). The wage is w = $10 per hour and the rental price of capital is r = $40 per machine-hour. Compare three ways to make q = 40 units: A (L = 10, K = 40), B (L = 20, K = 20) and C (L = 40, K = 10). Which is cheapest, and does it satisfy the cost-minimisation rule?
The isoquant for q = 40:  2√(L·K) = 40  →  √(L·K) = 20  →  L·K = 400
All three points satisfy it: 10 × 40 = 400, 20 × 20 = 400, 40 × 10 = 400.

Cost = wL + rK = 10L + 40K:
  A (L 10, K 40):  10 × 10 + 40 × 40 = 100 + 1,600 = $1,700
  B (L 20, K 20):  10 × 20 + 40 × 20 = 200 +   800 = $1,000
  C (L 40, K 10):  10 × 40 + 40 × 10 = 400 +   400 = $  800   ← cheapest

Marginal products for this function: MP_L = √(K ÷ L), MP_K = √(L ÷ K).

At C (L 40, K 10):  MP_L = √(10 ÷ 40) = 0.5     MP_K = √(40 ÷ 10) = 2
  MP_L ÷ w = 0.5 ÷ 10 = 0.05    MP_K ÷ r = 2 ÷ 40 = 0.05      ✓ equal

  MP_L ÷ w = 2 ÷ 10 = 0.20  >  MP_K ÷ r = 0.5 ÷ 40 = 0.0125
  → a dollar spent on labour buys 0.20 ÷ 0.0125 = 16 times the output of
    a dollar spent on capital. Shift TOWARD labour (use less K, more L).
Worked Example 5 — The Wage Rises from $10 to $40
Same firm and target (q = 40, isoquant L·K = 400, r = $40). The wage rises from $10 to $40. Find the new least-cost input mix and cost, and describe the substitution and scale effects.
Before: w = $10, the $800 isocost reaches L = 80 (K = 20) and is
        tangent to the isoquant at L = 40, K = 10.

After:  the SAME $800 now reaches only L = 800 ÷ 40 = 20  (K-intercept
        still 800 ÷ 40 = 20). The isocost has rotated inward around its
        K-intercept: it no longer touches the isoquant, so $800 can't
        make 40 units any more.

Least-cost mix at the new prices:
  For q = 2√(L·K), MRTS = MP_L ÷ MP_K = K ÷ L, so the tangency needs
     K ÷ L = w ÷ r = 40 ÷ 40 = 1   →   K = L
  Put K = L in L·K = 400:  L² = 400  →  L = 20, K = 20
  Cost = 40 × 20 + 40 × 20 = $800 + $800 = $1,600

  Labour:  40 → 20 (down)       Capital: 10 → 20 (up)
  Cost:    $800 → $1,600 (doubled)

Substitution effect: labour has become relatively more expensive, so the firm substitutes capital for labour while still making 40 units (it moves along the isoquant from C to (20, 20)). Scale effect: the same output now costs twice as much, so the firm may also choose to cut its output; how far depends on the price it can charge (Unit 8).

Worked Example 6 — Quick Drill: Is the Firm Minimising Cost?
w = $25, r = $50, MPL = 20, MPK = 30. Is the firm minimising cost? If not, what should it change?
MP_L ÷ w = 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8 units of output per dollar spent on labour
MP_K ÷ r = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.6 units of output per dollar spent on capital

0.8 > 0.6  → labour gives more output per dollar → NOT minimising cost.
Hire MORE labour and use LESS capital. As L rises MP_L falls, and as K
falls MP_K rises, so the two ratios move toward each other until equal.
  1. Compute the output per dollar of each input: MPL ÷ w and MPK ÷ r.
  2. The input with the larger ratio is the better buy: use more of it and less of the other.
  3. Diminishing marginal products pull the two ratios together as the mix changes.
  4. Stop when they are equal (MRTS = w/r) and the target output is still produced: that is the tangency.
Memory Aid — “Bang for the buck must balance” Cost is minimised when MPL/w = MPK/r. IsoQUANT = same QUANTity; isoCOST = same COST.
  • Direction rule: if the labour ratio is larger, hire more labour and rent less capital; if the capital ratio is larger, the reverse.
  • Price rule: if the wage rises (or r falls), the firm substitutes toward capital; if r rises (or the wage falls), it substitutes toward labour.
  • Trap: the isocost slope is −w/r (the wage over the rental rate) with L on the horizontal axis, not −r/w. A wage rise rotates the line inward along the L axis (the L-intercept falls, the K-intercept stays).
Self-test: r rises and w stays put; which input does the firm substitute toward? (Labour.)
The textbook’s versions (Appendix 9A) Different prices, different mixes. Hubbard compares a Canadian firm (ovens $1,000 a week, wage $500, isocost slope −1/2) with a Chinese firm (ovens $1,500, wage $300, slope −1/5) using the same technology. For $6,000 a week Canada’s cheapest way to make 5,000 pizzas is 3 ovens and 6 workers; China’s is 2 ovens and 10 workers. Nissan, similarly, uses far more robots in Japan than in India because labour is relatively dearer in Japan.

Jill’s check. With MPK = 3,000 pizzas per oven and MPL = 1,200 per worker, w = $300 and r = $600: MPL/w = 1,200 ÷ 300 = 4 pizzas per dollar and MPK/r = 3,000 ÷ 600 = 5. Ovens are the better buy, so Jill should use more ovens and hire fewer workers.

Long run beats short run. A bookcase firm (machines $100 a day, wage $25 a day) minimises cost at 20 machines and 80 workers for 100 bookcases a day, for $2,000 + $2,000 = $4,000. If it were stuck with 15 machines, it would need 110 workers: $1,500 + $2,750 = $4,250. Being able to adjust capital in the long run lowers cost, the same idea as LATC ≤ SATC. The line through all such least-cost points is the expansion path.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Maya’s Bakery

Maya quits a $50,000-a-year job to run a bakery. She invests $100,000 of her savings, which could have earned 5% elsewhere. Her revenue is $180,000 and her explicit costs are $120,000. Find her accounting profit and economic profit. Should she stay in the bakery business?

Show answer
Accounting profit = $180,000 − $120,000 = $60,000.

Implicit costs (Hubbard) = forgone salary $50,000 + forgone return 5% × $100,000 = $5,000, so $55,000.

Economic profit = $60,000 − $50,000 − $5,000 = $5,000 > 0.

Yes, she should stay: she earns more than her best alternatives (the job and the investment) combined, by $5,000.

Problem 3 — Work Backwards

At q = 200, ATC = $12 and AVC = $9. Find AFC, FC, TC and VC. What is AFC at q = 300?

Show answer
AFC = ATC − AVC = $12 − $9 = $3.

FC = AFC × q = $3 × 200 = $600.

TC = ATC × q = $12 × 200 = $2,400; VC = AVC × q = $9 × 200 = $1,800 (and $600 + $1,800 = $2,400 ✓).

At q = 300, AFC = $600 ÷ 300 = $2 (fixed cost is the same, spread over more units).
Problem 4 — Cost Minimisation

w = $25, r = $50, MPL = 20 and MPK = 30. (a) Is the firm minimising cost? What should it do? (b) If the rental price of capital r rises, what happens to the cost-minimising K/L ratio?

Show answer
(a) MPL/w = 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8; MPK/r = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.6. The ratios differ, so it is not minimising cost. Labour gives more output per dollar: use more labour and less capital until the ratios are equal.

(b) A higher r makes the isocost line flatter (−w/r is smaller in size). The tangency moves toward labour, so the firm substitutes labour for capital and the K/L ratio falls.
