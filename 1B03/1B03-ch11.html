Section 1

Product Differentiation and Monopolistic Competition

Unit 8’s perfectly competitive firms all sold the same thing, so none could charge a cent above the market price. Most firms you buy from every day — restaurants, coffee shops, hair salons, clothing stores — sell something a little different from their rivals. That difference is the whole story of Unit 9.

Product differentiation

Product differentiation refers to features of one firm’s product that make it different from the offerings of other firms. When products are differentiated, the output of different firms are not perfect substitutes.

Dr. Mang’s Lesson 9.1 lists five sources of differentiation:

  1. Actual or perceived differences in materials, quality, etc. (organic ingredients; “hand-made”)
  2. Brand name and reputation (you trust Tim Hortons’ coffee to taste the same in every city)
  3. Location and convenience (the coffee shop in your building beats a cheaper one across campus)
  4. Transaction costs, lock-in and switching costs (your loyalty points; the hassle of moving your phone plan)
  5. Incomplete information (you don’t know the other place is just as good, so you stick with what you know)
Memory Aid — the five sources are “Quality Brands Live Locked-In, Uninformed” Quality / materials · Brand & reputation · Location & convenience · Lock-in (transaction & switching costs) · Uninformed (incomplete information).
  • Direction rule: differentiation can be real or perceived. If buyers believe two products differ, they do, for this model.
  • Trap: a lower price is not a source of differentiation. Differentiation is what lets a firm charge a different price from its rivals without losing every customer.
Self-test: which source explains why you keep the same bank even though another offers a slightly better rate? (Lock-in / switching costs.)

A monopoly dominates a whole market. A monopolistically competitive firm does face competition, but it dominates its own little segment of the market. Mang’s three conditions:

Monopolistic competition (Slides, Lesson 9.1)
  1. Many buyers, each small relative to the market, so no buyer can affect the price.
  2. Many sellers, each small relative to the market, but each produces a differentiated good, so sellers have some control over their price and can charge prices different from their competitors.
  3. Firms can easily enter or exit the market.

Textbook (Hubbard 11.1): many firms, selling similar but not identical products, with low barriers to entry. Same idea: like perfect competition except for the product.

Section 2

Demand and Marginal Revenue for a Price-Setter

Because a monopolistically competitive firm has some control over its price, Mang calls it a price-setter. Its demand curve slopes down, so to increase sales it must lower its price, and (charging one price) that lower price applies to every unit it sells. Each price cut has two effects:

  • Revenue gained: the extra unit sells at the new price.
  • Revenue lost: every unit that would have sold anyway now brings in less.

MR = revenue gained − revenue lost, so marginal revenue is less than price. That’s the key difference from Unit 8, where d = MR = P.

Linear demand: MR has the same intercept and double the slope

For a linear demand curve, the slope of the MR curve is exactly double the slope of the demand curve. If d: P = a − bq, then MR = a − 2bq. (Mang: ECON 2Z03 covers MR for curved demand.)

Mang’s restaurant: P = 32 − 0.2q → MR = 32 − (2)0.2q = 32 − 0.4q. Panera: P = 10 − 0.5Q → MR ≈ 10 − Q (the table’s steps of $1.00).

Because P changes every time output changes, total revenue is not a straight line anymore. Mang writes it as TR = P(q) × q: a hill that rises while MR > 0, peaks where MR = 0, and falls once MR < 0. Its slope at any q is MR.

Vocabulary Trap — price-taker vs price-setter A price-taker (Unit 8) can’t affect the price: d is flat and MR = P. A price-setter chooses its own price: d slopes down and MR < P. “Price-setter” doesn’t mean it can charge anything. It picks a point on its demand curve.
Section 3

Marginal Revenue and Price Elasticity

Mang ties MR back to Unit 5. When demand is elastic, a price cut raises total revenue, so MR > 0. When MR = 0, revenue is at its maximum and demand is unit elastic. When demand is inelastic, a price cut lowers total revenue: MR < 0.

Price-setters only operate on the elastic part of demand

Marginal cost is always strictly positive (MC > 0). Profit maximization needs MR = MC, so MR must be positive where the firm operates. That means price-setting firms only operate in the elastic portion of the demand curve, and the profit-maximizing quantity is less than the revenue-maximizing quantity.

Memory Aid — “Elastic: MR is positive; the midpoint is the peak”
  • Direction rule: for linear demand, MR = 0 at half the quantity where demand hits zero (q = 80 for P = 32 − 0.2q, which reaches zero at q = 160). Everything left of it is elastic.
  • Direction rule: profit max (MR = MC > 0) is always left of revenue max (MR = 0).
  • Trap: “maximize revenue” and “maximize profit” are different goals. A firm that kept cutting its price to sell the revenue-maximizing quantity would pay MC on units that bring in less than they cost.
Self-test: for P = 50 − q, what quantity maximizes revenue, and could a profit-maximizer ever sell more than that? (q = 25; no.)
Section 4

Profit Maximization: MR = MC and the Markup

Profit is π = TR − TC. Where TR < TC, profit is negative; at a break-even point TR = TC; between the two break-even points profit is positive. Profit is largest where TR and TC have the same slope, and the slopes are MR and MC, so:

Profit maximization: MR = MC, then read the price off d

When MR > MC, revenues grow faster than costs and profit increases. When MR < MC, costs grow faster and profit decreases. Profit is at its maximum where MR = MC. The price is the height of the demand curve at that quantity.

Because P is on d and MR lies below d, P > MC: the price consumers pay is higher than the marginal cost of making the good. Mang calls the difference the price markup: Markup = P − MC.

Worked Example 2 — Mang’s Restaurant: P = 32 − 0.2q
A restaurant has a daily demand function of P = 32 − 0.2q and a marginal cost function MC = 0.4q. Determine the profit-maximizing quantity and price.
Step 1 — MR (same intercept, double the slope):
  MR = 32 − (2)0.2q = 32 − 0.4q

Step 2 — MR = MC:
  32 − 0.4q = 0.4q
  32 = 0.8q          →  q = 40 meals per day

Step 3 — climb to the demand curve:
  P = 32 − 0.2(40) = $24

Markup = P − MC = 24 − 0.4(40) = 24 − 16 = $8

The textbook’s version (Hubbard 11.2)

Back to Panera (Worked Example 1). With its costs, the 5th sandwich adds $5.50 to revenue and $5.50 to cost (MR = MC), so Panera sells 5 and charges the demand-curve price of $7.50. The 4th sandwich was worth selling: it added $6.50 to revenue but only $5.00 to cost. ATC at 5 sandwiches is $6.00, so profit = ($7.50 − $6.00) × 5 = $7.50 per week.

Don’t Let This Happen to You — the price is not at the MR = MC height In Mang’s example MR and MC cross at a height of $16. That’s the marginal cost, not the price. Go straight up from q = 40 to the demand curve: P = $24. The gap ($8) is the markup.
Memory Aid — “Cross, then Climb” The same recipe works for every downward-sloping demand curve (Units 9, 10 and 11): q where the marginals cross, then climb to d for the price.
  • Direction rule: MR > MC → produce more (and lower P along d). MR < MC → produce less.
  • Trap: P = MC is the Unit 8 rule for price-takers only. For a price-setter, P > MC.
Self-test: if the restaurant’s MC rose to 0.6q, what would q and P be? (32 − 0.4q = 0.6q → q = 32, P = $25.60.)
Section 5

Profits, Losses and the Shutdown Decision

Lesson 9.3 adds ATC. Profit per unit = π ÷ q = P − ATC. When P > ATC at the chosen q, the firm earns a profit; when P < ATC it earns a loss, and qMC (where MR = MC) is then the loss-minimizing quantity.

Worked Example 3 — Profit at Mang’s Restaurant
The restaurant (P = 32 − 0.2q, MC = 0.4q) has average total cost ATC = 300/q + 0.2q. Determine its profit. Then redo it with a fixed cost of $800 (ATC = 800/q + 0.2q).
From Example 2: q = 40, P = $24

π = (P − ATC) × q
ATC(40) = 300/40 + 0.2(40) = 7.50 + 8 = $15.50
π = (24 − 15.50) × 40 = $340
   check: TR = 24 × 40 = 960;  TC = 300 + 0.2(40²) = 620;  960 − 620 = $340 ✓

With FC = $800 (MR and MC unchanged → still q = 40, P = $24):
ATC(40) = 800/40 + 8 = $28  →  π = (24 − 28) × 40 = −$160
VC = 0.2(40²) = $320 < TR = $960 → operate: losing $160 beats losing all $800 of FC
Short-run losses: operate or shut down? (Slides)

If the firm operates, it pays fixed and variable costs but receives revenue. If it shuts down temporarily, it still pays its fixed cost but has no variable costs or revenue. So:

  • Revenue > variable cost (a positive producer surplus) → total loss < fixed cost, because producer surplus covers part of the fixed cost → operate.
  • Revenue < variable cost → total loss > fixed cost → shut down.

Same rule as Unit 8 (P vs AVC), stated in totals. Divide both sides by q: revenue > VC ⇔ P > AVC.

Section 6

The Long Run: Entry, Exit and Zero Economic Profit

Because entry and exit are easy, short-run profits and losses don’t last.

  • Profits → entry. Economic profits encourage new firms to enter. As new competition enters, demand for an existing firm’s output falls: d shifts left (Hubbard adds that it also becomes more elastic, because customers now have more close substitutes). Demand falls until P = ATC.
  • Losses → exit. Economic losses encourage firms to exit. As competitors leave, demand for a remaining firm’s output rises until P = ATC.
Long-run equilibrium: d is tangent to ATC

At the profit-maximizing q (MR = MC), the demand curve just touches the ATC curve: P = ATC, zero economic profit. Mang (slide 31): there is no benefit to either investing in or withdrawing from this industry, because firms earn an accounting profit exactly equal to investors’ risk-adjusted expected rate of return — a normal profit.

Don’t Let This Happen to You — zero economic profit ≠ zero accounting profit (Textbook) You invest $200,000 in a pizza parlour; a similar investment would earn 10%. That $20,000 a year is an opportunity cost. It shows up as accounting profit (you even pay tax on it) but it’s an economic cost. In long-run equilibrium you earn that $20,000, enough to keep you in business, but not a cent more. Zero economic profit is a normal, healthy outcome.

Is zero economic profit inevitable? (Textbook) Only if the firm stands still. The keys to earning an economic profit are to sell a more differentiated product or to produce at a lower cost. Rivals eventually copy both, so successful firms keep innovating: new menu items, better service, new locations.

Memory Aid — “Profit invites, loss evicts, tangency sticks”
  • Direction rule: entry → each firm’s d shifts left (and flatter). Exit → d shifts right.
  • Direction rule: it stops when d is tangent to ATC: P = ATC at MR = MC.
  • Trap: the tangency is not at the bottom of ATC (that’s perfect competition). A downward-sloping d can only touch ATC on ATC’s falling part.
Self-test: three new cafés open on your street. What happens to your café’s demand curve, price and economic profit? (d left and flatter; P falls; profit falls toward zero.)
Section 7

Efficiency and Welfare

Mang’s two long-run conclusions (Lesson 9.3):

1 · P = ATC → capital is allocated efficiently

Zero economic profit means investors earn exactly a normal return. There is no reason to move capital into or out of the industry.

2 · P > MC → production is not efficient

Since P = ATC and P > MC, ATC > MC at the chosen q: the firm is on the falling part of ATC, not at its minimum. Under monopolistic competition, production is not efficient.

Textbook termConditionPerfect competition (LR)Monopolistic competition (LR)
Productive efficiencyProduced at the lowest possible cost: P = min ATC✓✗ (excess capacity)
Allocative efficiencyMarginal benefit = marginal cost: P = MC✓✗ (markup)
Vocabulary Trap — what Mang calls “efficient” Mang says P = ATC means capital is allocated efficiently, and P > MC means production is not efficient. Hubbard says monopolistic competition achieves neither productive nor allocative efficiency. They aren’t contradicting each other. Mang’s “capital” point is about zero economic profit; Hubbard’s two terms are about P vs min ATC and P vs MC. On a Hubbard-style question, answer “neither”.

Welfare (Slides)

With perfect competition, producers would charge P = MC. Facing downward-sloping MR, a monopolistically competitive firm charges more and sells less. So consumer surplus is lower than under perfect competition, and there is a deadweight loss. In the long run, since P = ATC, producer surplus = total fixed cost (PS = TR − VC, and TR = TC). Monopolistically competitive markets are less efficient than perfectly competitive ones.

Worked Example 4 — Welfare at Mang’s Restaurant
Using P = 32 − 0.2q, MC = 0.4q and ATC = 300/q + 0.2q, compare the firm’s outcome (q = 40, P = $24) with the P = MC outcome. Find CS, PS and the deadweight loss.
P = MC benchmark: 32 − 0.2q = 0.4q → q* = 53.33, P* = $21.33

At q = 40, P = $24, MC(40) = $16:
  CS  = ½ × 40 × (32 − 24) = $160
  PS  = TR − VC = 960 − 0.2(40²) = 960 − 320 = $640   (π = PS − FC = 640 − 300 = $340 ✓)
  DWL = ½ × (53.33 − 40) × (24 − 16) = $53.33

At P = MC: CS = ½ × 53.33 × (32 − 21.33) = $284.44  → consumers lose $124.44

This is a short-run picture (the firm still earns $340). In the long run entry would push P down to ATC and PS to $300, the fixed cost, but P > MC, so a deadweight loss remains.

The other side: consumers like variety (Textbook) The gap between monopolistic competition and perfect competition is the price of product differentiation. Hubbard argues consumers are often happy to pay a little more for a restaurant, style or location that suits them better. Economists debate whether the inefficiency is a real loss to society or simply the cost of choice.
Section 8

Marketing and What Makes a Firm Successful

Hubbard 11.5 (textbook only): marketing is all the activities needed for a firm to sell a product to a consumer. Firms use it to differentiate their products:

  • Brand management — keeping a product differentiated over time, as rivals try to copy a successful product.
  • Advertising — raises demand and makes it less elastic (more brand loyalty), but costs money, raising ATC. It pays only if the extra revenue beats the extra cost.
  • Defending a brand name — trademarks give a firm the legal right to its brand name. Firms sue to stop others using their name, or the name becoming generic (like “escalator” or “aspirin”).

What makes a firm successful? Hubbard’s Figure 11.7: factors under the firm’s control — the ability to differentiate its product and to produce it at lower average cost — plus factors outside its control: chance, changes in technology, and changes in consumer tastes. Being the first firm in a market isn’t enough on its own. Many first movers lose to later firms that offer a better product or lower costs.

Memory Aid — “Different or cheaper (and a little lucky)” The only two lasting routes to economic profit in a competitive market: a product people see as different, or a lower cost than rivals. Both get copied, so successful firms keep doing both.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 builds MR from a table, Problems 2–3 are Mang-style equation questions, Problem 4 is the long run, and Problem 5 puts welfare and efficiency together. For more graph practice, the Graph Lab’s Unit 9 drills give you new numbers every time.

Problem 1 — A Bakery’s Cupcakes

A bakery sells 0 cupcakes at $6.00, and one more for each $0.50 price cut (Q = 1 at $5.50 … Q = 8 at $2.00). Its marginal cost is a constant $1.50 and its fixed cost is $5. (a) Find TR and MR for Q = 1 to 8. (b) What quantity and price maximize profit, and what is the profit? (c) What quantity maximizes revenue? Is that quantity on the elastic or inelastic part of demand?

Show answer
(a) TR = 5.50, 10.00, 13.50, 16.00, 17.50, 18.00, 17.50, 16.00. MR = 5.50, 4.50, 3.50, 2.50, 1.50, 0.50, −0.50, −1.50.

(b) MR ≥ MC up to Q = 5, where MR = MC = $1.50 (the 5th cupcake adds nothing to profit but doesn’t lower it); “produce while MR ≥ MC” gives Q = 5, P = $3.50. TR = 17.50, TC = 5 + 1.50 × 5 = 12.50 → π = $5.00 (Q = 4 also earns 16 − 11 = $5.00).

(c) TR peaks at Q = 6 ($18.00): MR(6) = 0.50 > 0, MR(7) < 0. Q = 6 sits at the boundary between elastic (MR > 0) and inelastic (MR < 0). The profit-maximizing Q (5) is on the elastic part, to the left of the revenue maximum.
Problem 2 — A Hair Salon

A salon faces daily demand P = 60 − 0.5q, with MC = 0.5q and ATC = 450/q + 0.25q. (a) Find MR. (b) Find the profit-maximizing q and P, the markup and the profit. (c) What quantity would maximize revenue?

Show answer
(a) MR = 60 − q.

(b) 60 − q = 0.5q → q = 40; P = 60 − 20 = $40. MC(40) = $20 → markup = $20. ATC(40) = 11.25 + 10 = $21.25 → π = (40 − 21.25) × 40 = $750. (TR 1,600 − TC 850.)

(c) MR = 0 → q = 60 (P = $30). The salon stops at 40, on the elastic part of its demand.
Problem 3 — A Bad Year

The salon in Problem 2 signs a pricier lease, so its fixed cost rises to $1,500 (ATC = 1,500/q + 0.25q). (a) Do q and P change? (b) Find the profit or loss. (c) Should it shut down in the short run? (d) What happens in the long run?

Show answer
(a) No. Fixed cost doesn’t affect MR or MC: still q = 40, P = $40.

(b) ATC(40) = 37.50 + 10 = $47.50 → π = (40 − 47.50) × 40 = −$300.

(c) Revenue = $1,600 > VC = 0.25 × 40² = $400 → producer surplus $1,200 covers part of the fixed cost → operate. Losing $300 beats losing the whole $1,500 by shutting down.

(d) If salons in general are losing money, some exit; the survivors’ demand curves shift right until d is tangent to ATC and P = ATC (zero economic profit). (If only this salon has high costs, it may exit itself.)
Problem 4 — The Long Run

(a) In Problem 2 the salon earns $750 a day. What happens to its demand curve, price and profit over time, and when does the process stop? (b) The owner invested $150,000 and could earn 8% a year elsewhere. In long-run equilibrium, what is her yearly economic profit, and what is her accounting profit (counting only this opportunity cost)? (c) True or false: in long-run equilibrium the salon produces at the minimum of its ATC curve.

Show answer
(a) Profit attracts entry. New salons take customers and add substitutes, so d shifts left and becomes more elastic; P and profit fall. It stops when d is tangent to ATC: P = ATC, zero economic profit.

(b) Economic profit = $0; accounting profit = 8% × $150,000 = $12,000 (a normal profit).

(c) False. The tangency is on the falling part of ATC, left of its minimum (excess capacity); ATC > MC there.
Problem 5 — Welfare and Efficiency

Use the salon from Problem 2 (P = 60 − 0.5q, MC = 0.5q, FC = $450).
(a) Find the P = MC (perfectly competitive) quantity and price.
(b) At the salon’s choice (q = 40, P = $40), find consumer surplus, producer surplus and the deadweight loss.
(c) Verify that profit = PS − FC.
(d) Explain, in Mang’s words, why the long run is efficient in one sense but not another, and name the two textbook efficiency terms it fails.
(e) In the long run, what will producer surplus equal?

Show answer
(a) 60 − 0.5q = 0.5q → q* = 60, P* = $30.

(b) CS = ½ × 40 × (60 − 40) = $400. PS = TR − VC = 1,600 − 0.25(40²) = 1,600 − 400 = $1,200. DWL = ½ × (60 − 40) × (40 − 20) = $200.

(c) π = 1,200 − 450 = $750 ✓ (same as Problem 2).

(d) P = ATC → zero economic profit → capital is allocated efficiently (no reason to invest in or leave the industry). But P > MC → production is not efficient (ATC > MC; there is a deadweight loss). Textbook: it achieves neither allocative efficiency (P = MC) nor productive efficiency (P = min ATC).

(e) Since P = ATC, TR = TC, so PS = TR − VC = total fixed cost.
