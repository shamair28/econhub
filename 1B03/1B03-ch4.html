Section 1

Why This Chapter Matters

Chapter 3 told you where a market ends up: at the price and quantity where demand meets supply. Chapter 4 asks a sharper question: how good is that outcome, and what happens when the government overrides it? To answer, we put a dollar value on the gains from trade. Buyers gain consumer surplus, sellers gain producer surplus, and the two together measure how much a market is worth to society.

That one measuring stick lets us grade every policy in this chapter. Minimum wages and milk price supports are price floors; rent control is a price ceiling; dairy and egg production licences are quotas; GST and gasoline taxes are sales taxes. Each one creates winners and losers, and each one shrinks the total — the shrinkage is the deadweight loss. Dr. Mang's Unit 4 is the last unit on Midterm 1 (Saturday, October 3), and it is the most calculation-heavy: expect to compute areas of triangles and rectangles, not just name them.

Section 2

Consumer Surplus

Consumer surplus (CS) is the difference between the highest price a consumer is willing to pay for a good and the price they actually pay. If you would pay up to $18 for a coffee maker and the register rings it up at $12, you walk away with $6 of consumer surplus — value you received but did not pay for.

Consumer Surplus = Perceived Value (willingness to pay) − Price

The demand curve is a list of willingness-to-pay. Read it up from any quantity: the height of the demand curve above unit Q is what the buyer of that unit values it at — its marginal benefit. Because everyone pays the same market price P*, each unit earns CS equal to the gap between the demand curve and the price line. The last unit bought (Q*) earns zero surplus — its buyer values it at exactly P*. Add the gaps up for every unit from 0 to Q* and you get:

Total consumer surplus = the area below the demand curve and above the price line, from 0 to Q*

With a straight-line demand curve that area is a triangle: CS = ½ × base × height = ½ × (Q* − 0) × (demand intercept − P*).

Do not confuse CS with consumer expenditure — the amount consumers actually hand over: Consumer Expenditure = P × Q. On the graph, expenditure is the rectangle under the price line; CS is the triangle above it.

Worked Example — Consumer Surplus One Buyer at a Time (Textbook: Chai Tea)
Four consumers would pay at most: Theresa $6.00, Tom $5.00, Terri $4.00, Tim $3.00 for a cup of chai. The market price is $3.50. Find each person's consumer surplus and the total. What happens if the price falls to $3.00?
At P = $3.50:
  Theresa  $6.00 − $3.50 = $2.50
  Tom      $5.00 − $3.50 = $1.50
  Terri    $4.00 − $3.50 = $0.50
  Tim      will only pay $3.00 < $3.50 → doesn't buy → CS = $0
  Total CS = $2.50 + $1.50 + $0.50 = $4.50

At P = $3.00:
  Theresa, Tom and Terri each gain $0.50 → +$1.50
  Tim now buys, but $3.00 − $3.00 = $0 surplus (he's indifferent)
  Total CS = $4.50 + $1.50 = $6.00

Lesson: a lower price raises CS two ways — existing buyers pay less,
and new buyers enter. The "smooth" demand curve is just this staircase
with millions of buyers.
Worked Example — Consumer Expenditure and CS (Lecture Slides, Markets A and B)
Market A: Demand P = 100 − 2Q, Supply P = 0.5Q. Market B: Demand P = 500 − 5Q, Supply P = 20 + 3Q. Find the consumer expenditure and the consumer surplus in each.
MARKET A
  Equilibrium: 0.5Q = 100 − 2Q  →  2.5Q = 100  →  Q* = 40
               P* = 0.5(40) = $20
  Expenditure = P × Q = $20 × 40 = $800
  CS = ½ × base × height
     = ½ × (40 − 0) × (100 − 20)      ← height = demand intercept − P*
     = ½ × 40 × 80 = $1,600

MARKET B
  Equilibrium: 20 + 3Q = 500 − 5Q  →  8Q = 480  →  Q* = 60
               P* = 500 − 5(60) = $200
  Expenditure = $200 × 60 = $12,000
  CS = ½ × (60 − 0) × (500 − 200) = ½ × 60 × 300 = $9,000
Exam Recipe — Any Surplus Triangle
  1. Find equilibrium: set the two P = … equations equal and solve for Q*, then P*.
  2. Base = the quantity (Q* − 0).
  3. Height = the vertical gap between the price line and the curve's intercept (demand intercept − P* for CS).
  4. Area = ½ × base × height. Write the units ($) — and sanity-check that CS is positive.

Section 3

Producer Surplus

Whatever consumers spend becomes the sellers' income: Consumer Expenditure = P × Q = Producer Revenue (in a market with no sales tax). But revenue is not the producers' gain — they still have to pay for production. Producer surplus (PS) is the difference between the price a producer actually receives and the lowest price it is willing to accept.

Producer Surplus = Price − Marginal Cost of producing the unit

The lowest acceptable price for a unit is its marginal cost, and the supply curve shows that cost unit by unit — the supply curve is a marginal cost curve. So read it like the demand curve, but now the surplus on each unit is the gap from the supply curve up to the price. The last unit sold (Q*) earns zero surplus. Adding every unit's gap:

Total producer surplus = the area above the supply curve and below the price line, from 0 to Q*

With a straight-line supply curve: PS = ½ × (Q* − 0) × (P* − supply intercept). The money left in the rectangle below the supply curve is production cost: Revenue = PS + production cost.

Worked Example — Producer Surplus (Lecture Slides, Markets A and B)
Using the same two markets, find the producer surplus and the total surplus in each.
MARKET A   (supply P = 0.5Q starts at the origin, intercept = 0)
  PS = ½ × (40 − 0) × (20 − 0) = ½ × 40 × 20 = $400
  Total surplus = CS + PS = $1,600 + $400 = $2,000

MARKET B   (supply P = 20 + 3Q has intercept $20)
  PS = ½ × (60 − 0) × (200 − 20) = ½ × 60 × 180 = $5,400
  Total surplus = $9,000 + $5,400 = $14,400

Watch the height in Market B: it is P* minus the SUPPLY INTERCEPT
($200 − $20), not P* minus zero. Forgetting a non-zero intercept is
the most common way to lose marks on PS questions.
Memory Aid — “Consumers Upstairs, Producers Downstairs” Think of the price line as the floor of a two-storey house sitting between the curves. Consumers live upstairs: CS is the space above the price line, up to the demand curve. Producers live downstairs: PS is the space below the price line, down to the supply curve. The house is only as wide as the quantity traded.
  • Demand on top, supply underneath — on the left of Q*, the demand curve is always above the supply curve, so CS is never below the price line.
  • Price ↑ moves the floor up: more room downstairs (PS ↑), less upstairs (CS ↓). Price ↓ does the reverse.
  • A shift changes both rooms. If demand for bananas decreases, P* and Q* both fall, so the downstairs room gets lower and narrower: producer surplus falls. (This exact question was on the F22 midterm.)

Vocabulary Trap — Expenditure ≠ Surplus, Revenue ≠ Surplus Expenditure and revenue are the same dollars seen from two sides (P × Q). Surplus is the net gain: value minus price for buyers, price minus cost for sellers. An MC answer that says “consumer surplus equals the total amount consumers spend” is wrong every time.
Section 4

Total Surplus, Efficiency and Deadweight Loss

Total surplus (the textbook calls it economic surplus) is the sum of the two: Total Surplus = Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus. It is the best single measure we have of what a market is worth to society, so economists judge market efficiency by it.

The key result of Unit 4: the free-market equilibrium (P*, Q*) produces the largest possible total surplus. There are two equivalent ways to see why:

  • Marginal benefit = marginal cost. The demand curve measures marginal benefit (MB) and the supply curve measures marginal cost (MC). Every unit up to Q* has MB ≥ MC, so producing it adds surplus. Every unit beyond Q* has MC > MB, so producing it would destroy surplus. Q* is exactly where MB = MC.
  • Any other price shrinks the quantity traded. Push the price above P* and buyers purchase less (the trade quantity is set by demand). Push it below P* and sellers offer less (the trade quantity is set by supply). Either way, fewer units are traded and their surplus vanishes.

The surplus that disappears when a market produces less than Q* is the deadweight loss (DWL). Nobody receives it — not consumers, not producers, not the government. It is the value of trades that would have made both sides better off but never happen.

One DWL formula for every intervention in this chapter

DWL = ½ × (Q* − Qnew) × (demand price − supply price at Qnew)

Base = the units lost. Height = the vertical gap between the demand curve and the supply curve at the restricted quantity. The triangle's tip always points at the old equilibrium. You can always double-check: DWL = old total surplus − new total surplus (counting any tax revenue as part of the new total).

Efficient ≠ Fair — Why Governments Intervene Anyway “The free market maximizes total surplus” is a positive statement about the size of the pie. It says nothing about how the pie is shared. As Unit 1 warned, free markets can generate inequality, so a government may decide on normative grounds that a less efficient but more equal outcome is better. That is the framing for every slide in Lesson 4.3: the policy causes a deadweight loss, “but for normative reasons, the government might believe it is socially desirable.” On an exam, describe the efficiency cost (positive) separately from whether the policy is worth it (normative).
Section 5

Price Floors

A price floor is a legal minimum price. The market price may be higher than the floor but never lower. Canada's best-known examples are the minimum wage (a floor in the labour market) and the price supports behind supply management of milk, eggs and poultry, in place since the 1960s.

  • Non-binding: if the floor is set at or below P*, nothing happens — the market settles at P* anyway.
  • Binding: if the floor is set above P*, it becomes the new market price. Buyers only want QD at that price, so only QD is sold — less than Q*. Sellers would like to supply QS > QD, so there is a surplus (excess supply) of QS − QD.

Welfare effects (as Lesson 4.3 presents them): the floor benefits producers. Buyers now pay more on every unit they still buy, so part of the old consumer surplus — the rectangle between the old and new price — is transferred to producers. Consumer surplus shrinks, producer surplus grows, and total surplus shrinks by the deadweight loss on the units no longer sold.

Worked Example — A $30 Price Floor in Market A
Market A (P = 100 − 2Q, P = 0.5Q, P* = $20, Q* = 40). The government sets a minimum price of $30. Is it binding? Find the quantity sold, the surplus, CS, PS and the deadweight loss.
Binding?  $30 > P* = $20  → YES

Quantity demanded at $30:  30 = 100 − 2Q  → Q_D = 35   ← amount SOLD
Quantity supplied at $30:  30 = 0.5Q      → Q_S = 60
Surplus (excess supply) = 60 − 35 = 25 units

Supply price of the 35th unit (its marginal cost): 0.5(35) = $17.50

CS  = ½ × 35 × (100 − 30)                      = $1,225   (was $1,600)
PS  = rectangle + triangle under $30, above S, out to Q = 35
    = (30 − 17.5) × 35  +  ½ × 35 × 17.5
    = 437.50 + 306.25                          = $743.75  (was $400)
DWL = ½ × (40 − 35) × (30 − 17.5) = ½ × 5 × 12.5 = $31.25

Check: old TS $2,000 − new TS ($1,225 + $743.75) = $31.25 ✓
PS Under a Floor Is a Trapezoid, Not a Triangle Once the quantity is cut back to QD, the region between the price line and the supply curve stops short of where they meet. Split it into a rectangle (height = floor − MC of the last unit sold) plus the triangle underneath, or just compute PS = old PS + transfer rectangle − producers' share of the DWL. Here: $400 + $350 − $6.25 = $743.75.
Lecture vs. Textbook: “Producers Gain” The slides say a floor makes PS larger and a ceiling makes CS larger. The textbook is more careful: producers gain the transfer rectangle but lose their part of the deadweight-loss triangle, so their net gain is usually positive, not guaranteed. With a very high floor, the lost sales can outweigh the higher price. For Midterm 1, give the slides' answer and say why: the transfer rectangle (CS → PS) is bigger than what producers lose in the DWL.

The minimum wage is the textbook's headline floor. Workers sell labour, firms buy it. A binding minimum wage raises pay for workers who keep their jobs, but firms hire fewer hours (QD falls) while more people want to work (QS rises). The gap is unemployment, and the hours never worked are the deadweight loss. Economists disagree about how large the employment effect is, but not about which direction it goes in the model.

Section 6

Price Ceilings

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price. The market price may be lower than the ceiling but never higher. The textbook's lead example is rent control, used in British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario and Quebec.

  • Non-binding: a ceiling set at or above P* changes nothing.
  • Binding: a ceiling set below P* becomes the market price. Now sellers are the constraint: only QS is offered, so only QS is sold — less than Q*. Buyers want QD > QS, so there is a shortage (excess demand) of QD − QS.

Welfare effects: the ceiling benefits some consumers — the ones lucky enough to buy at the low price. Part of producer surplus is transferred to consumers, PS shrinks, and total surplus shrinks by the deadweight loss. But the slides stress a second point: other consumers are left worse off. The artificially low price creates excess demand, and the buyers who can't find the good at all lose the surplus they used to get.

Worked Example — A $15 Price Ceiling in Market A
Market A again. The government caps the price at $15. Is it binding? Find the quantity sold, the shortage, CS, PS and the deadweight loss.
Binding?  $15 < P* = $20  → YES

Quantity supplied at $15:  15 = 0.5Q      → Q_S = 30   ← amount SOLD
Quantity demanded at $15:  15 = 100 − 2Q  → Q_D = 42.5
Shortage (excess demand) = 42.5 − 30 = 12.5 units

Demand price of the 30th unit (its marginal benefit): 100 − 2(30) = $40

PS  = ½ × 30 × (15 − 0)                          = $225     (was $400)
CS  = rectangle + triangle above $15, below D, out to Q = 30
    = (40 − 15) × 30  +  ½ × 30 × (100 − 40)
    = 750 + 900                                  = $1,650   (was $1,600)
DWL = ½ × (40 − 30) × (40 − 15) = ½ × 10 × 25    = $125

Check: $2,000 − ($1,650 + $225) = $125 ✓
Consumers as a GROUP gain only $50, while the 12.5 units of unmet
demand show many would-be buyers get nothing at all.
Memory Aid — Where They Bind, What They Cause, Who Decides
  • “A floor holds prices UP; a ceiling holds prices DOWN.” So a floor only matters if it sits above P*, and a ceiling only matters if it sits below P*. The names sound backwards on the graph (the floor line is drawn high, the ceiling line low), and that is exactly the MC trap.
  • Floor → Flood; Ceiling → Can't find it. A binding floor floods the market with unsold goods (surplus). A binding ceiling leaves buyers who can't find the good (shortage).
  • The short side rules. Under any price control, the quantity actually traded is the smaller of QD and QS. Floor → buyers are the short side (sold = QD). Ceiling → sellers are the short side (sold = QS).
  • The transfer rectangle follows the policy's purpose. A floor is written for sellers, so CS → PS. A ceiling is written for buyers, so PS → CS.
Self-test: draw a floor and a ceiling on one S&D graph from memory, label which quantity is traded in each, and shade the DWL.
Vocabulary Trap — “Shortage” vs. “Scarcity” Almost everything is scarce (Chapter 1: wants exceed resources). A shortage is narrower: QD > QS at the current price. Most scarce goods have no shortage, because their price has adjusted. “There is a shortage of every scarce good” is false — a textbook favourite.

Black Markets and Other Side Effects

Because a binding ceiling leaves buyers who would gladly pay more, people find ways around it. A black market is buying and selling at prices that break the law. With rent control, it takes the form of illegal side payments, “key money” and waiting-list fees. The textbook works through a case where black-market rents rise above the original equilibrium. Renters as a group can then end up with less surplus than with no rent control at all, while the deadweight loss stays the same. Other side effects: landlords cut maintenance (quality falls), convert units to condos or offices (QS falls further over time), and can more easily discriminate between applicants when there are more renters than apartments.

Section 7

Quotas

A quota is a legal maximum quantity. Output may be below the quota but not above it. A quota set at or above Q* doesn't matter. A quota set below Q* becomes the new market quantity, QQ.

Graphically, the quota makes supply vertical at QQ (labelled Squota on the slides): no matter how high the price goes, no more can be sold. With only QQ units available, buyers bid the price up to the demand price at that quantity, PQ. As with a floor, a quota benefits producers: consumers pay more on every unit, a rectangle of CS is transferred to producers, and the units between QQ and Q* are lost as deadweight loss.

Worked Example — A Quota of 30 Units in Market A
The government limits Market A to 30 units. Find the new price, CS, PS and the deadweight loss.
Binding?  30 < Q* = 40  → YES

Price with the quota (read off DEMAND at Q = 30):  P_Q = 100 − 2(30) = $40
Marginal cost of the 30th unit (read off SUPPLY):  0.5(30) = $15

CS  = ½ × 30 × (100 − 40)                      = $900   (was $1,600)
PS  = (40 − 15) × 30  +  ½ × 30 × 15
    = 750 + 225                                = $975   (was $400)
DWL = ½ × (40 − 30) × (40 − 15)                = $125

Check: $2,000 − ($900 + $975) = $125 ✓
The $25 gap between what buyers pay ($40) and what the last unit costs
($15) is a QUOTA RENT — it is why production licences are valuable.
Real-World Link — Supply Management and Milk Quota The textbook ties floors and quotas together. A milk price floor alone would leave farmers producing far more than consumers buy (a surplus of milk going sour). So Canada pairs the floor with production licences: every commercial dairy farmer must hold milk market quota, and total quota is set so supply equals demand at the government price. The floor sets the price, and the quota removes the surplus. Eggs, chicken, turkey and broiler hatching eggs work the same way, overseen nationally by the Farm Products Council of Canada.

Section 8

Sales Taxes

Mixed economies have governments that provide goods and services, and those must be paid for with tax revenue. A sales tax is added on top of the price consumers pay. On the slides the tax is drawn on the supply side: at every quantity, the price buyers must pay to call forth that quantity is now the old supply price plus the tax. The supply curve shifts up by the tax to S + tax (written Stax).

  • Unit (per-unit) tax: a fixed dollar amount per unit (e.g. 10¢ per litre of gasoline). S + tax is parallel to S, exactly t higher. All the calculations in Unit 4 use unit taxes.
  • Ad valorem (value-added) tax: a percentage of the price (e.g. 13% HST). The dollar amount grows as the price grows, so S + tax pivots upward, getting steeper rather than staying parallel.

The tax raises the price consumers pay, so they buy less. Because producers now sell less, they move down their supply curve to a lower price. The result: a consumer price PC (= PS + tax) above P*, a producer price PS below P*, and a smaller quantity Qt. The gap between them — the tax wedge — equals the tax exactly.

Tax incidence: who really pays

Consumer incidence = PC − P* (how much the consumer's price rose). Producer incidence = P* − PS (how much the producer's price fell). They always add up to the tax: Tax Amount = Consumer Incidence + Producer Incidence. Consumers' price rises, but by less than the full tax (unless demand is perfectly vertical).

Welfare: consumers pay more and buy less, so CS shrinks. Producers receive less and sell less, so PS shrinks. The government collects Tax Revenue = Tax × Quantity Sold — the rectangle between PC and PS, out to Qt. What's left over is the deadweight loss: surplus on the units no longer traded, which nobody collects. The textbook calls this DWL the excess burden of the tax.

Worked Example — A $5 Tax in Market A (Lecture Slides)
Market A: P = 100 − 2Q, P = 0.5Q, with CS = $1,600 and PS = $400 before the tax. The government adds a tax of $5 per unit. Find the new quantity, producer price, consumer price, tax revenue, CS, PS and DWL.
Step 1 — SHIFT supply up by the tax
  S + tax:  P = 0.5Q + 5

Step 2 — SOLVE S + tax = D for the new quantity
  0.5Q + 5 = 100 − 2Q
  2.5Q = 95  →  Q_t = 38

Step 3 — SPLIT into the two prices
  Producer price (original S):  P_S = 0.5(38)           = $19
  Consumer price (D or S+tax):  P_C = 100 − 2(38)        = $24
                                check 0.5(38) + 5        = $24 ✓
  Incidence: consumers 24 − 20 = $4,  producers 20 − 19 = $1  (4 + 1 = 5 ✓)

Step 4 — AREAS
  CS  = ½ × 38 × (100 − 24)          = $1,444
  PS  = ½ × 38 × (19 − 0)            = $361
  Rev = $5 × 38                      = $190
  DWL = ½ × (40 − 38) × (24 − 19)    = $5

Check: (1,600 + 400) − (1,444 + 361 + 190) = $5 ✓
Worked Example — A $24 Tax in Market B (Lecture Slides)
Market B: P = 500 − 5Q, P = 20 + 3Q, with CS = $9,000 and PS = $5,400. The government adds a $24 per-unit tax. Find everything.
SHIFT:  S + tax:  P = 20 + 3Q + 24 = 44 + 3Q
SOLVE:  44 + 3Q = 500 − 5Q  →  8Q = 456  →  Q_t = 57
SPLIT:  P_S = 20 + 3(57) = $191        P_C = 500 − 5(57) = $215
        Incidence: consumers 215 − 200 = $15, producers 200 − 191 = $9

CS  = ½ × 57 × (500 − 215)        = $8,122.50
PS  = ½ × 57 × (191 − 20)         = $4,873.50   ← height uses the $20 intercept!
Rev = $24 × 57                    = $1,368
DWL = ½ × (60 − 57) × (215 − 191) = $36

Check: (9,000 + 5,400) − (8,122.5 + 4,873.5 + 1,368) = $36 ✓
Memory Aid — “Shift, Solve, Split” and the Four-Box Check
  1. Shift — add the tax to the supply equation (P = old supply + t).
  2. Solve — set S + tax equal to demand to get Qt.
  3. Split — PC comes from demand at Qt; PS comes from the original supply at Qt. Check PC − PS = t.
Then the four-box check: every dollar of the old total surplus ends up in exactly one box — CS + PS + Revenue + DWL = old CS + old PS. If your four boxes don't add back to the original total, one area is wrong. Find it before moving on.
Who Pays Depends on Steepness, Not on Who Sends the Cheque The textbook shows that taxing buyers instead (shifting demand down by t) gives the same PC, PS and Qt. Legal responsibility doesn't decide the burden — the slopes do. The steeper (less price-responsive) side pays more. In Market A demand is steeper (slope 2) than supply (0.5), and consumers bore $4 of the $5 tax. That is 80%, the same split as the textbook's 10¢ gasoline tax (8¢ consumers, 2¢ sellers). Quick check for linear curves: consumer share = demand slope ÷ (demand slope + supply slope). Market A: 2 ÷ 2.5 = 80%; Market B: 5 ÷ 8 = 62.5% of $24 = $15 ✓. (Slopes as magnitudes; this is a derived shortcut, not on the slides — use it to check, but show the full working on the test.) Chapter 6 renames “steepness” as elasticity.
Vocabulary Trap — Three Different “Prices” After a tax there is no single market price. PC (consumer price, tax included) is read off the demand curve. PS (producer price, what the seller keeps) is read off the original supply curve. P* is the old, no-tax equilibrium, used only to measure incidence. Mixing up PC and PS flips CS and PS, and it is the most common error on tax questions.

Section 9

Tax Cuts and Subsidies

Cutting a tax runs the process in reverse (lecture slide “Effect of Cutting Taxes”). S + tax shifts back down, the quantity sold increases, producers' price rises, and consumers pay a lower price. But the consumer price falls by less than the tax cut — the same incidence logic works in both directions.

A subsidy is a payment from the government to firms that covers part of the cost of production, paid as cash or as tax rebates. It lowers each unit's cost, so supply shifts down/right by the subsidy amount to S − subsidy (Ssub). The results are the mirror image of a tax: production rises, consumers pay a lower price, and producers receive a higher price (the consumer price plus the subsidy). The catch, from the slides: the money for the subsidy must be raised by taxing other goods. Removing a subsidy reverses everything, and price and quantity return to the free-market equilibrium.

Worked Example — A $10 Subsidy in Market A
The government pays producers $10 for every unit sold in Market A. Find the new quantity, the price consumers pay, the price producers receive and the cost of the program.
SHIFT:  S − subsidy:  P = 0.5Q − 10
SOLVE:  0.5Q − 10 = 100 − 2Q  →  2.5Q = 110  →  Q = 44
SPLIT:  Consumer price (demand):  100 − 2(44) = $12   (down $8)
        Producer receives (S):    0.5(44)     = $22   (up $2)
        Check: 22 − 12 = $10 ✓
Cost to government = $10 × 44 = $440

Beyond the slides (good to know): CS rises by $336 and PS by $84, a
$420 gain that costs taxpayers $440. The missing $20 = ½ × 4 × 10 is a
deadweight loss from OVERproduction — units 41–44 cost more to make
than buyers value them.
The Big Takeaway (Last Slide of Unit 4) Taxes distort any market they touch: production falls, a deadweight loss appears, and the market becomes less efficient. Society can still come out ahead if the government spends the revenue productively, especially on goods free markets don't provide well (Unit 12: public goods and externalities). There's no guarantee it will, though. Be ready to give both halves of this argument in your own words.
Section 10

Comparing Interventions: Who Gets the Rectangle?

Every intervention in this chapter cuts the quantity traded below Q*. When different policies cut Market A to the same 30 units, something striking happens. The deadweight loss is identical ($125) every time, because it depends only on which units are lost. The only difference between the policies is who ends up with the $750 rectangle between the demand price ($40) and the supply price ($15) on the 30 units that are still traded.

Market A, 30 units tradedCSPSGovernmentDWLTotal
Free market (40 units)$1,600$400—$0$2,000
Price ceiling $15$1,650$225—$125$2,000
Price floor $40*$900$975—$125$2,000
Quota of 30$900$975—$125$2,000
$25 unit tax$900$225$750$125$2,000

* Assumes producers make only the 30 units buyers want. If they produce the 80 units they'd like to supply at $40, the 50 unsold units are wasted and the true loss is larger — the reason supply management pairs its price floor with a quota.

Memory Aid — “The Rectangle Goes to Whoever the Policy Was Written For”
  • Ceiling → written for buyers → rectangle to consumers.
  • Floor and quota → written for sellers → rectangle to producers.
  • Tax → written to raise money → rectangle to the government (as revenue).
  • The triangle goes to nobody. Whatever the policy, the DWL triangle points at the old equilibrium and is simply lost.
Use it backwards on MC questions. If an answer says a price ceiling “transfers surplus from consumers to producers,” it has the rectangle going the wrong way.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problems 1–4 use one market for bike helmets: Demand P = 120 − 3Q, Supply P = 20 + 2Q (Q in hundreds of helmets).

Problem 1 — Surplus at Equilibrium

(a) Find the equilibrium price and quantity. (b) Find consumer expenditure. (c) Find consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus.

Show answer
(a) 20 + 2Q = 120 − 3Q → 5Q = 100 → Q* = 20; P* = 20 + 2(20) = $60. Check: 120 − 60 = 60 ✓

(b) Expenditure = $60 × 20 = $1,200 (also producer revenue).

(c) CS = ½ × 20 × (120 − 60) = $600. PS = ½ × 20 × (60 − 20) = $400 (height uses the $20 supply intercept). TS = $1,000.
Problem 2 — Binding or Not?

For the helmet market (P* = $60, Q* = 20), say whether each policy is binding: (a) a price ceiling of $70; (b) a price floor of $50; (c) a quota of 25; (d) a price floor of $72. For the binding one, find the quantity sold, the surplus or shortage, CS, PS and DWL.

Show answer
(a) Ceiling $70 > P* → not binding. (b) Floor $50 < P* → not binding. (c) Quota 25 > Q* → not binding. (d) Floor $72 > P* → binding.

At $72: QD: 72 = 120 − 3Q → Q = 16 (sold — the short side). QS: 72 = 20 + 2Q → Q = 26. Surplus = 10.
MC of the 16th unit = 20 + 2(16) = $52.
CS = ½ × 16 × (120 − 72) = $384
PS = (72 − 52) × 16 + ½ × 16 × (52 − 20) = 320 + 256 = $576 (up from $400 — producers gain)
DWL = ½ × (20 − 16) × (72 − 52) = $40. Check: 1,000 − 384 − 576 = 40 ✓
Problem 3 — A Binding Price Ceiling

The government caps helmet prices at $48. Find (a) the quantity sold and the shortage, (b) CS and PS, (c) the deadweight loss. (d) Do consumers as a group gain?

Show answer
(a) QS: 48 = 20 + 2Q → Q = 14 sold. QD: 48 = 120 − 3Q → Q = 24. Shortage = 10.

(b) Demand price at 14 = 120 − 42 = $78.
CS = (78 − 48) × 14 + ½ × 14 × (120 − 78) = 420 + 294 = $714. PS = ½ × 14 × (48 − 20) = $196.

(c) DWL = ½ × (20 − 14) × (78 − 48) = $90. Check: 1,000 − 714 − 196 = 90 ✓

(d) Yes, as a group: CS rises from $600 to $714. But buyers who want another 1,000 helmets at $48 can't get them, and producers lose $204.
Problem 4 — A $10 Unit Tax

A $10 per-unit tax is placed on helmet producers. Find Qt, PC, PS, the consumer and producer incidence, tax revenue, CS, PS and DWL. Who bears more of the tax, and why?

Show answer
Shift: P = 30 + 2Q. Solve: 30 + 2Q = 120 − 3Q → 5Q = 90 → Qt = 18.
Split: PC = 120 − 54 = $66; PS = 20 + 36 = $56. (66 − 56 = 10 ✓)
Incidence: consumers 66 − 60 = $6; producers 60 − 56 = $4.

Revenue = 10 × 18 = $180. CS = ½ × 18 × 54 = $486. PS = ½ × 18 × 36 = $324. DWL = ½ × 2 × 10 = $10.
Four-box check: 486 + 324 + 180 + 10 = 1,000 ✓

Consumers bear more because demand is steeper (slope 3) than supply (slope 2): 3 ÷ 5 = 60% of $10 = $6.
Problem 5 — The In-Class Exercise from the Unit 4 Lecture

This is the exercise from the Unit 4 lecture deck, whose answer slides were left blank to fill in during class. Demand: P = 260 − 3Q. Supply: P = 20 + 2Q.
(a) Find the equilibrium price and quantity, consumer surplus and producer surplus.
(b) The government adds a tax of $20 per unit. Find the new quantity, producer price, consumer price with tax, tax revenue, consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss.
(c) How is the $20 burden split between consumers and producers?
(d) What fraction of the revenue raised does the DWL represent, and what would happen if the government later cut the tax back to zero?
(e) Instead of the tax, the government imposes a quota at the same quantity. Who now gets the revenue rectangle, and how big is PS?

Show answer
(a) Free market
20 + 2Q = 260 − 3Q → 5Q = 240 → Q* = 48; P* = 20 + 96 = $116 (check 260 − 144 = 116 ✓)
CS = ½ × 48 × (260 − 116) = ½ × 48 × 144 = $3,456
PS = ½ × 48 × (116 − 20) = ½ × 48 × 96 = $2,304 → TS = $5,760

(b) With the $20 tax
Shift: P = 40 + 2Q. Solve: 40 + 2Q = 260 − 3Q → 5Q = 220 → Qt = 44
Split: PS = 20 + 2(44) = $108; PC = 260 − 3(44) = $128 (128 − 108 = 20 ✓)
Revenue = $20 × 44 = $880
CS = ½ × 44 × (260 − 128) = ½ × 44 × 132 = $2,904
PS = ½ × 44 × (108 − 20) = ½ × 44 × 88 = $1,936
DWL = ½ × (48 − 44) × (128 − 108) = ½ × 4 × 20 = $40
Four-box check: 2,904 + 1,936 + 880 + 40 = 5,760 ✓

(c) Incidence
Consumers: 128 − 116 = $12 (60%). Producers: 116 − 108 = $8 (40%). Demand is steeper (3 vs 2), so consumers carry more: 3 ÷ 5 = 60% ✓

(d) Efficiency cost and a tax cut
DWL ÷ revenue = 40 ÷ 880 ≈ 4.5%: for every $1 raised, about 4.5¢ of surplus simply disappears. Cutting the tax to zero reverses everything: Q rises back to 48, producers' price rises from $108 to $116, and consumers' price falls from $128 to $116 — a $12 drop, less than the $20 cut. The DWL disappears, along with the $880 of revenue.

(e) Quota of 44 instead
Price with the quota is read off demand: $128. Producers keep the whole rectangle: PS = (128 − 108) × 44 + ½ × 44 × 88 = 880 + 1,936 = $2,816. CS is still $2,904, DWL still $40, government gets $0. Same quantity, same DWL; only the $880 rectangle changes hands (Section 10).
Summary

Chapter Summary

Slides = how Dr. Mang's Unit 4 slides and lecture define the term; Textbook = the Hubbard glossary definition (the wording MyLab and test-bank-style questions tend to use).

ConceptFormula / DefinitionExam Tip
Consumer surplusWillingness to pay − price; area below D, above P: ½ × Q × (D-intercept − P)

Slides: the difference between the price consumers are willing to pay for a good (their perceived value of it) and the price they actually pay: CS = perceived value − price. The demand curve shows that value unit by unit, so total CS is the area between the demand curve and the price line. The last unit bought (Q*) earns no CS.
Textbook: the highest price a consumer is willing to pay minus the price actually paid.		“Consumers upstairs” — the triangle above the price line
Producer surplusPrice − marginal cost; area above S, below P: ½ × Q × (P − S-intercept)

Slides: the difference between the price producers are willing to accept and the price they actually receive. The lowest acceptable price is that unit's marginal cost, which the supply curve shows: PS = price − marginal cost of producing the unit. Total PS is the area between the price line and the supply curve. The last unit sold (Q*) earns no PS.
Textbook: the price a firm actually receives minus the lowest price it would be willing to accept.		Use the supply intercept, not zero, when S doesn't start at the origin
Expenditure / revenueP × Q (the same dollars from two sides)

Slides: consumer expenditure is the total amount consumers pay for Q units, P × Q (in a competitive market everyone pays the same P*). In a market without sales taxes, all of it becomes producer revenue. Firms pay their production costs (the area under the supply curve) out of that revenue, and what is left over is producer surplus.		Not the same as surplus — the rectangle, not the triangle
Marginal benefit & marginal costTextbook: marginal benefit (MB) is the extra benefit a consumer gets from one more unit; marginal cost (MC) is the extra cost a firm pays to produce one more unit.
Slides: the demand curve tells us how much consumers value each unit (MB); the supply curve tells us how much it costs a producer to make each unit (MC).		Read up from a quantity: the height of D is MB, the height of S is MC. Output is efficient where they are equal, at Q*
Total surplus & efficiencyTS = CS + PS; maximized at the free-market (P*, Q*) where MB = MC

Slides: market efficiency is measured by the total gains to both consumers (CS) and producers (PS). The free-market equilibrium (P*, Q*) produces the largest possible total surplus. A price that is too high or too low lowers the quantity sold and therefore the surplus, which is why any government intervention reduces market efficiency.
Textbook: economic surplus = CS + PS. Economic efficiency is the outcome where the marginal benefit of the last unit produced equals its marginal cost and economic surplus is at its maximum.		Any price other than P* reduces the quantity traded and TS
Deadweight loss½ × (Q* − Qnew) × (demand price − supply price at Qnew)

Slides: the reduction in total market surplus caused by a floor, ceiling, quota or tax: DWL = old surplus − new surplus.
Textbook: the reduction in economic surplus that results from a market not being in competitive equilibrium. The DWL of a tax is also called its excess burden.		Check with old TS − new TS (counting tax revenue)
Why governments interveneSlides: free markets are the most efficient, but they can also generate inequality. For normative reasons, a government may decide to change the market outcome, accepting a deadweight loss because it believes the result is socially desirable. Governments also need tax revenue to pay for the goods and services they provide in a mixed economy.Keep efficiency (a positive question) separate from desirability (a normative one). An answer saying an intervention raises total surplus is wrong
Binding vs. non-bindingA control is binding when it actually changes the market outcome.
Slides: a floor set too low, a ceiling set too high, or a quota set too high “does not matter”: the market still trends to P* and Q*.		Floor above P*, ceiling below P*, quota below Q* — otherwise nothing changes. A control set exactly at P* (or Q*) is not binding
Price floorLegal minimum; binding only above P*; sold = QD; surplus QS − QD

Slides: a minimum price set by the government. The price may be higher than the floor but not lower. Set above P*, the floor becomes the new market price and fewer units are sold than in the free market. It benefits producers: PS gets larger, CS gets smaller, and total surplus shrinks by a deadweight loss.
Textbook: a legally determined minimum price that sellers may receive (e.g. the minimum wage, milk prices under supply management).		Floor → Flood. Rectangle CS → PS; PS is a trapezoid
Price ceilingLegal maximum; binding only below P*; sold = QS; shortage QD − QS

Slides: a maximum price set by the government. The price may be lower than the ceiling but not higher. Set below P*, the ceiling becomes the new market price and fewer units are sold. It benefits some consumers (CS gets larger, PS smaller), but the artificially low price creates excess demand, so other consumers are left worse off. Total surplus shrinks by a deadweight loss.
Textbook: a legally determined maximum price that sellers may charge (e.g. rent control).		Ceiling → Can't find it. Rectangle PS → CS; some buyers get nothing; black markets
Excess demand / excess supplyExcess demand (shortage): QD > QS at the current price — what a binding ceiling (or any price below P*) creates.
Excess supply (surplus): QS > QD — what a binding floor (or any price above P*) creates.		“Surplus” has two meanings: excess supply (a quantity) vs. consumer/producer surplus (a dollar area). A shortage is not the same as scarcity
Black marketTextbook: buying and selling at prices that violate government price regulations, e.g. tenants paying landlords more than a rent-control ceiling allows. If black-market prices rise high enough, renters as a group can end up with less CS than with no ceiling at all.Usually caused by a binding ceiling (unmet demand). The DWL stays the same; only who gets the transfer changes
QuotaLegal maximum quantity; binding only below Q*; price = demand price at the quota

Slides: a maximum quantity set by the government. Output may be below the quota but not above it. Set below Q*, the quota becomes the new market quantity. It benefits producers: PS gets larger, CS gets smaller, and total surplus shrinks by a deadweight loss.		Vertical supply at the quota; rectangle CS → PS (quota rent)
Unit vs. ad valorem taxFixed $ per unit (parallel shift) vs. % of price (pivot)

Slides: a sales tax is added on top of the price consumers pay, and the vertical gap between the before-tax and after-tax supply curves is the tax amount. A unit tax is a fixed amount that doesn't change with the price producers charge. A value-added (ad valorem) tax is a percentage of the price, so the tax grows as the price rises.		All Unit 4 calculations are unit taxes
Tax effectsS shifts up by t; PC − PS = t; Revenue = t × Qt

Slides: the tax raises the price, so consumers buy less. Selling less, producers move down their supply curve to a lower price. The consumer price rises, but not by the full amount of the tax. The producer price falls and the quantity falls. CS and PS both shrink, the government collects tax revenue = tax × quantity sold, and the units no longer traded are a deadweight loss. Society can still come out ahead if the revenue is spent productively (e.g. on goods free markets under-provide), but there is no guarantee that it will be.		Shift → Solve → Split; then CS + PS + Rev + DWL = old TS
Tax incidenceConsumer: PC − P*; Producer: P* − PS; they sum to t

Slides: the change in the consumer price is the consumer incidence of the tax; the change in the producer price is the producer incidence. Tax amount = consumer incidence + producer incidence.
Textbook: tax incidence is the actual division of a tax's burden between buyers and sellers. It doesn't depend on which side is legally required to pay the tax.		The steeper side pays more; who legally pays doesn't matter
Tax cut / subsidyCut: reverse of a tax. Subsidy: S shifts down by the subsidy; Q ↑, PC ↓, producers receive more

Slides: cutting a tax works in reverse: quantity sold rises, producers raise their price, and consumers pay less — but their price falls by less than the tax cut. A subsidy is a government payment to firms that covers part of the cost of production (cash or tax rebates). It lowers the per-unit cost and increases supply, so production rises and the price consumers pay falls. Removing a subsidy reverses this: the price rises and quantity falls back to the free-market equilibrium.		Subsidies must be funded by taxing other goods
Looking Ahead — Elasticity (Unit 5, Chapter 6) This chapter kept saying “the steeper side pays more” and “the DWL depends on how many units are lost.” Chapter 6 turns steepness into a number — price elasticity — and uses it to predict exactly how much quantity responds to a price change. Once you have elasticity, you can say in advance which taxes raise the most revenue for the least deadweight loss, and why gasoline taxes fall mostly on drivers.
