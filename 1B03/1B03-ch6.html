Section 1

Why Elasticity Matters

Chapters 3 and 4 gave us the Law of Demand: when price rises, quantity demanded falls. That tells you the direction of the response, but not its size. Size is what decisions turn on. A firm wondering whether a price cut will bring in more revenue needs to know whether customers will respond a lot or a little. A government taxing gasoline (people grumble, but keep driving) versus restaurant meals (people eat at home instead) needs the same information. Elasticity is the measure of how much one economic variable responds to a change in another.

The one question elasticity answers: how much does quantity respond? The Law of Demand says quantity demanded goes down when price goes up. Elasticity says by how much, as a ratio of percentage changes: elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. It is a pure number, so it works for pizza, tuition or oil and for straight or curved demand curves alike.

Why not just use the slope of the demand curve? Because the slope depends on the units you choose. Hubbard's illustration: a $1 rise in the price of a 2-litre pop bottle cuts quantity demanded from 10.2 to 10.1 million bottles. The slope is −0.1 ÷ 1 = −0.1. Measure the price in cents and it becomes −0.001; measure quantity in thousands and it becomes −100; change both and it is −1. Same market, four different slopes. Percentage changes have no units, so they give the same answer every time. Dr. Mang puts it as: elasticity is “not the slope, but related to the slope.”

Opening case — do soft drink taxes work? (Hubbard, chapter opener) Cities such as Philadelphia (2016, 1.5 cents per ounce) and San Francisco (2 cents per ounce) taxed sweetened drinks. Two goals pull in opposite directions. For health, you want quantity to fall a lot. For revenue, you want it to fall a little, because tax revenue = tax per ounce × ounces sold (1.5 cents × 6 billion ounces = $90 million). The bigger the drop in quantity, the better the health effect and the smaller the revenue. PepsiCo said Philadelphia sales fell 40%, and a Chicago grocer said customers drove to Indiana, where there was no tax. Which goal wins depends on elasticity. We return to this in Section 8.

Memory Aid — “Response over Cause” Every elasticity in this chapter has the same shape: the % change in the thing that responds (a quantity) on top, the % change in the thing that caused it (a price or an income) on the bottom.
  • Direction rule: quantity always goes on top. Only the bottom changes: own price (PED and PES), income (income elasticity), or another good's price (cross-price).
  • Trap: flipping the fraction (price on top). That gives the reciprocal, which is not elasticity.
Self-test: which % change goes on the bottom of a cross-price elasticity? (The other good's price.)

Section 2

Price Elasticity of Demand and the Midpoint Formula

The price elasticity of demand (PED) is the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to a change in price, measured by dividing the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage change in price:

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions (the Law of Demand), PED is always negative. Dr. Mang keeps the sign (E = −3.54) and notes that some economists quote only the absolute value. Hubbard also says PED is always negative, but then “drops the minus sign and compares absolute values” (the book uses no special symbol for it). Either way, the size of the number is what classifies the demand.

The midpoint formula

With initial point (Q1, P1) and final point (Q2, P2):

E = [ (Q2 − Q1) ÷ ((Q1 + Q2)/2) ] ÷ [ (P2 − P1) ÷ ((P1 + P2)/2) ]

In words (Mang): change in quantity over average quantity, divided by change in price over average price. Hubbard gives the same formula (§6.1).

  1. Find both points. If you are given a demand equation, plug each price in to get its quantity.
  2. Numerator: (Q2 − Q1) divided by the average of Q1 and Q2. This is %ΔQ.
  3. Denominator: (P2 − P1) divided by the average of P1 and P2. This is %ΔP.
  4. Divide, keep the sign, then classify by size (Section 3).
Worked Example 1 — Same Slope, Very Different Elasticities (Dr. Mang's demand curve P = 100 − 2Q)
Demand is P = 100 − 2Q. Find the price elasticity of demand (a) between $80 and $76, and (b) between $30 and $26.
Solve for Q from P:  Q = (100 − P) ÷ 2
  P = 80 → Q = 10      P = 76 → Q = 12
  P = 30 → Q = 35      P = 26 → Q = 37

(a) $80 → $76 (Q: 10 → 12)
    %ΔQ = (12 − 10) ÷ [(10 + 12)/2] = 2 ÷ 11  = 0.1818
    %ΔP = (76 − 80) ÷ [(80 + 76)/2] = −4 ÷ 78 = −0.0513
    E = 0.1818 ÷ (−0.0513) = −3.54   (exactly 156 ÷ 44 = 3.545...;
                                      the slides quote −3.54)
    |E| = 3.54 > 1  →  ELASTIC

(b) $30 → $26 (Q: 35 → 37)
    %ΔQ = 2 ÷ [(35 + 37)/2] = 2 ÷ 36  = 0.0556
    %ΔP = −4 ÷ [(30 + 26)/2] = −4 ÷ 28 = −0.1429
    E = 0.0556 ÷ (−0.1429) = −0.39
    |E| = 0.39 < 1  →  INELASTIC

Slope is −2 (ΔP/ΔQ) in both cases. Elasticity went from −3.54 to −0.39.
Worked Example 2 — Why the Midpoint? (Same Curve, Two Directions)
Using the same curve, compute the elasticity from $80 to $76 and then from $76 back to $80 with the plain % change formula (initial value as the base). Compare with the midpoint answer.
Going down, $80 → $76 (Q: 10 → 12), base = starting point
  %ΔQ = 2 ÷ 10   = +20%
  %ΔP = −4 ÷ 80  = −5%
  E = 20 ÷ (−5)  = −4.00

Going back up, $76 → $80 (Q: 12 → 10), base = new starting point
  %ΔQ = −2 ÷ 12  = −16.7%
  %ΔP = +4 ÷ 76  = +5.26%
  E = −16.7 ÷ 5.26 = −3.17

Two answers for the same stretch of curve!  Midpoint: −3.54, both
directions (it uses the average as the base, so the starting point
no longer matters).  In size, 3.17 < 3.54 < 4.00: the midpoint answer
lies between the two one-direction answers.

Hubbard makes the identical point with a Coca-Cola demand curve through $1.50/1,000 bottles and $1.35/1,200 bottles: the plain formula gives −2.0 going down and −1.5 going up, while the midpoint gives −1.7 both ways. Its three takeaways: the curve is elastic between the points, the midpoint value lies between the two one-direction values, and it is the same in either direction.

Hubbard Solved Problem 6.1 — Coca-Cola’s 2-litre bottle Price falls from $1.50 to $1.30 (average price $1.40, so %ΔP = −0.20 ÷ 1.40 = −14.3%). On demand curve D1, quantity rises from 2,000 to 2,500 a day: %ΔQ = 500 ÷ 2,250 = 22.2%, so E = 22.2 ÷ (−14.3) = −1.6, elastic. On a steeper curve D2, quantity rises only to 2,100: %ΔQ = 100 ÷ 2,050 = 4.9%, so E = −0.3, inelastic. Same price cut, different demand curves, different elasticities.
Vocabulary Trap — elasticity ≠ slope Slope = ΔP ÷ ΔQ is constant along a straight line and changes with units. Elasticity = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP changes all along a straight line (Example 1) and has no units. Hubbard’s rule for two demand curves that cross: the flatter one is more elastic at the point where they cross (for the same price change). It does not mean you can read a curve’s elasticity from its steepness alone.

Elastic, Inelastic, Unit Elastic — and the Extremes

Dr. Mang classifies demand by where E falls on the number line; Hubbard states the same thing in absolute value. Both agree on the cut-offs (Hubbard Table 6.1).

(The bagel and wheat examples are Hubbard’s “Apply the Concept” in §6.1.) Dr. Mang adds that perfectly inelastic demand is realistic only over a price range, and that every ordinary demand curve has an elastic portion and an inelastic portion, separated by a point of unit elasticity. Some curves are mostly elastic and some mostly inelastic, but the straight-line demand curve shows the pattern most clearly.

The unit-elastic point on a straight-line demand curve

On P = 100 − 2Q the unit-elastic point is at the midpoint of the line: Q = 25, P = $50, where E = −1. Above $50 (small Q, large P) demand is elastic; below $50 (large Q, small P) it is inelastic. The intuition: near the top, a $1 price cut is a tiny % of a big price, but each extra unit is a big % of a small quantity, so %ΔQ beats %ΔP. Near the bottom it is the other way around.

Hubbard’s version (§6.3, Figure 6.3): a demand curve where every $1 price cut sells 2 more litres, running from $8 at Q = 0 to $0 at Q = 16. It is elastic from $8 down to $4 (Q = 0 to 8), inelastic from $4 to $0, and unit elastic at $4 and 8 litres.

Memory Aid — “I” is for Inelastic (and it’s vertical) Perfectly Inelastic demand looks like the letter I: vertical, E = 0. Perfectly elastic demand is flat like a stretched elastic band: horizontal, E = ∞.
  • Elastic = Extra-responsive (|E| > 1). Inelastic = Insensitive (|E| < 1).
  • Trap: the sign. −3.54 is more elastic than −0.39; compare absolute values, not the signed numbers.
Self-test: classify −0.8, −1, −2.5, 0 and ∞. (Inelastic, unit elastic, elastic, perfectly inelastic, perfectly elastic.)
Vocabulary Trap — “inelastic” ≠ “perfectly inelastic” (Hubbard’s “Don’t Let This Happen to You”) If a question says “demand for gasoline is inelastic,” draw an ordinary steep downward-sloping curve, not a vertical line. Gasoline buyers still cut back a little when the price rises (|E| is small but not zero). Truly vertical or horizontal demand curves are rare. Likewise “elastic” does not mean “perfectly elastic.”
Section 4

What Makes Demand More or Less Elastic?

Dr. Mang lists five determinants of the price elasticity of demand. Hubbard (§6.2) lists the same five, in the same order, and calls the availability of substitutes the most important one. Learn them as a set; exam questions ask you to pick the more elastic good in a pair and name the reason.

1. Availability of close substitutes

The more (and closer) the substitutes, the more elastic the demand: a price rise sends buyers elsewhere. Gasoline has few alternatives (small response); pizza has many. Hubbard: “the availability of substitutes is the most important determinant.”

2. Passage of time

The more time that passes, the more elastic demand becomes. Consumers need time to change habits: switch to transit, buy a more fuel-efficient car, find another supplier.

3. Luxuries vs. necessities

Demand for a luxury is more elastic than demand for a necessity. Bread is inelastic; concert tickets are elastic. (Hubbard: “whether the good is a luxury or a necessity.”)

4. Definition of the market

The narrower the market, the more elastic the demand. “Tim Hortons coffee” is more elastic than “coffee” because other cafés are close substitutes for one brand, but few are for the whole category.

5. Share of the consumer’s budget

The bigger the share of the budget, the more elastic the demand. Table salt is a trivial expense and a price rise goes unnoticed; a car, a house or tuition takes a large share, so buyers respond.

Memory Aid — Price hikes make shoppers “STuMBLe” Substitutes · Time · Market definition · Budget share · Luxury vs. necessity (five determinants, and Hubbard’s count is also five).
  • Direction rule: more substitutes, more time, narrower market, bigger budget share, luxury → more elastic. The opposite of each → less elastic.
  • Trap: “necessity” here means a must-have good with low price sensitivity. It is not the same “necessity” as in income elasticity (0 < EI < 1); see Section 6.
Self-test: cover the list and name all five with their direction.

Philadelphia’s bottler saw sales fall 40%, because Pepsi bought just outside the city is a close substitute. That is the market-definition and substitutes determinants at work, and why a local Pepsi bottler would prefer a national tax.
Section 5

Elasticity and Total Revenue

Total revenue (TR) is the total amount a seller receives: TR = P × Q. When a firm changes its price, TR can go up or down, because price and quantity move in opposite directions. Which way it goes depends on elasticity.

Think of the revenue box. A price cut has two effects:

  • Price effect (loss): every unit you were already selling now brings in less. Lost revenue = (price cut) × (old quantity).
  • Quantity effect (gain): you sell extra units. Gained revenue = (new price) × (extra quantity).

Elasticity tells you which is bigger. If demand is elastic, the % rise in quantity beats the % fall in price and the gain wins. If it is inelastic, the loss wins.

Worked Example 4 — Total Revenue on P = 100 − 2Q
Using the same curve, compare total revenue (a) when the price is cut from $80 to $76, and (b) when it is cut from $30 to $26. Where is revenue largest?
(a) $80 → $76 (elastic, E = −3.54)
    TR before = 80 × 10 = $800      TR after = 76 × 12 = $912  → RISES
    Box check:  lost   4 × 10 = $40    (price cut on the 10 units already sold)
                gained 76 × 2 = $152   (2 extra units at the new price)
                net = 152 − 40 = +$112  ✓ (912 − 800)

(b) $30 → $26 (inelastic, E = −0.39)
    TR before = 30 × 35 = $1,050    TR after = 26 × 37 = $962  → FALLS
    Box check:  lost   4 × 35 = $140
                gained 26 × 2 = $52
                net = 52 − 140 = −$88  ✓ (962 − 1,050)

(c) Maximum TR: TR = P × Q = (100 − 2Q) × Q = 100Q − 2Q²
    peak at Q = 25, P = $50, TR = 50 × 25 = $1,250
    That is the unit-elastic point (E = −1).

Dr. Mang’s slides say it as: “At the point of unit elasticity, revenues are at a maximum!” Hubbard’s shorthand (Table 6.3) is that if demand is inelastic, price and total revenue move in the same direction; if elastic, they move in opposite directions.

Memory Aid — “The bigger % wins” TR moves with whichever % change is larger.
  • Elastic → quantity’s % is bigger → TR moves with quantity (opposite to price).
  • Inelastic → price’s % is bigger → TR moves with price. Shortcut: “Inelastic: TR In step with price.”
  • Trap: a firm on the inelastic part of demand should raise price to raise revenue (it sells slightly less and earns more per unit). A profit-seeking firm never chooses to sit on the inelastic portion.
Self-test: demand elasticity is −0.4 and price falls 10%. What happens to TR? (Quantity rises only about 4%, so TR falls, by roughly 6%.)

Real-world revenue tests (Hubbard)

  • Gas station (Fig. 6.2): at $1.25 and 1,000 litres, TR = $1,250. Cut to $1.15: if demand is inelastic and sales rise to 1,050 L, TR = $1,207.50 (down, a $100 loss against a $57.50 gain). If demand is elastic and sales rise to 1,200 L, TR = $1,380 (up, a $230 gain against the $100 loss).
  • Solved Problem 6.2, the rec-centre fee: New York raised the annual fee from $75 to $150 (+100%), expecting only 5% fewer members (so managers assumed E = −5% ÷ 100% = −0.05, very inelastic) and about $4 million more revenue. Revenue actually fell by about $200,000. Since a price increase cut revenue, demand must have been elastic.
  • Amazon and e-books: Amazon estimated the elasticity of e-book demand at −1.74. At $14.99 and 100,000 copies, revenue is about $1.5 million; at $9.99 and 174,000 copies it is about $1.74 million, so “the total pie is bigger” (publisher Hachette argued the elasticity differs across titles).

Section 6

Income and Cross-Price Elasticities

Demand also responds to things other than a good’s own price. Two more elasticities (Lesson 5.3, Hubbard §6.4) measure the response to income and to another good’s price. Dr. Mang computes both with the midpoint formula (average values as the base).

Income elasticity of demand

EI = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔIncome (midpoint: change ÷ average, for both Q and income).

EI > 0: normal good. EI > 1: luxury. 0 < EI < 1: necessity. EI < 0: inferior. It varies with the type of good and with the consumer’s income level.

Cross-price elasticity of demand

Exy = %ΔQ of good A ÷ %ΔP of good B

Exy > 0: substitutes. Exy < 0: complements. Exy = 0: unrelated.

Worked Example 5 — Income Elasticity (income rises from $40,000 to $50,000)
Lucy’s income rises from $40,000 to $50,000. She goes from 8 to 12 restaurant meals a month and from 20 to 16 packs of instant noodles a month. Find the income elasticity for each good and classify it.
%ΔIncome = 10,000 ÷ [(40,000 + 50,000)/2] = 10,000 ÷ 45,000 = 22.2%

Restaurant meals (8 → 12):
  %ΔQ = 4 ÷ [(8 + 12)/2] = 4 ÷ 10 = 40%
  E_I = 40% ÷ 22.2% = +1.80   → positive and above 1: NORMAL, a LUXURY

Instant noodles (20 → 16):
  %ΔQ = −4 ÷ [(20 + 16)/2] = −4 ÷ 18 = −22.2%
  E_I = −22.2% ÷ 22.2% = −1.00   → negative: INFERIOR
Worked Example 6 — Cross-Price Elasticity (coffee price rises from $2.00 to $2.50)
The price of coffee rises from $2.00 to $2.50. Tea purchases go from 30 to 36 cups, and cream purchases from 50 to 40 portions. Find each cross-price elasticity and say how the good is related to coffee.
%ΔP (coffee) = 0.50 ÷ [(2.00 + 2.50)/2] = 0.50 ÷ 2.25 = 22.2%

Tea (30 → 36):
  %ΔQ = 6 ÷ 33 = 18.2%
  E_xy = 18.2% ÷ 22.2% = +0.82   → positive: SUBSTITUTES

Cream (50 → 40):
  %ΔQ = −10 ÷ 45 = −22.2%
  E_xy = −22.2% ÷ 22.2% = −1.00  → negative: COMPLEMENTS
Memory Aid — the sign tells the story
  • Income: “+ = Normal (big + above 1 = Luxury, small + between 0 and 1 = Necessity), − = Inferior.”
  • Cross-price: “Substitutes Same sign (+), Complements Contrary (−)” — the same rule as the Chapter 3 shifters.
  • Trap: an income elasticity of 0.4 is a normal good (a necessity), not inferior. Only a negative number is inferior.
Self-test: classify EI = 2.3, 0.6, −0.5 (luxury, necessity, inferior) and Exy = +1.2, −0.7, 0 (substitutes, complements, unrelated).
Vocabulary Trap — “necessity” and “luxury” appear twice As a price-elasticity determinant (Section 4), necessities have inelastic price demand and luxuries have elastic price demand. As an income-elasticity class, a necessity has 0 < EI < 1 and a luxury has EI > 1. Check which elasticity the question is asking about. Hubbard adds that normal, inferior, necessity and luxury are just labels, not value judgments.
Hubbard Table 6.5 and the business cycle Examples: bread (normal necessity), caviar (normal luxury), high-fat meat (inferior). In expansions, sales rise for most firms, most of all for luxury sellers; in recessions, demand for inferior goods rises (bus trips versus air travel, hamburger versus steak). Hubbard also reports a beer study (alcohol “Apply the Concept”): price elasticity −0.30, income elasticity +0.09 (a necessity), and cross-price elasticity with wine −0.83 and with spirits −0.50. The surprise is that beer and wine come out as complements, not substitutes.

Section 7

Price Elasticity of Supply

The price elasticity of supply (PES) is the responsiveness of the quantity supplied to a change in price: the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. Supply slopes up, so PES is always positive. The classes use the same cut-offs as demand: between 0 and 1 is inelastic, exactly 1 is unit elastic, above 1 is elastic (and 0 and ∞ are the vertical and horizontal extremes).

ES = [ (Q2 − Q1) ÷ avg Q ] ÷ [ (P2 − P1) ÷ avg P ]

Hubbard (Table 6.6 and §6.6) gives some real numbers: gasoline over a year about 0.20 (a 10% price rise gives a 2% increase in quantity: inelastic), wheat 1.5 (10% price rise, 15% quantity rise: elastic), bottled water 1 (unit elastic), and a parking lot with a fixed number of spaces in the short run is perfectly inelastic.

A helpful fact about straight-line supply Where a straight supply line hits the axes tells you its elasticity everywhere: a line that hits the price axis above 0 is elastic at every point; one that hits the quantity axis is inelastic at every point; one through the origin is unit elastic at every point. (Dr. Mang’s slides draw curved supply curves, which can have both an elastic and an inelastic portion.)
Worked Example 7 — Two Straight-Line Supply Curves
(a) Supply is Q = 2P − 20. Find ES when the price rises from $30 to $40. (b) Supply is Q = 20 + P. Find ES over the same price rise.
(a) Q = 2P − 20:  P = 30 → Q = 40;  P = 40 → Q = 60
    %ΔQ = 20 ÷ [(40 + 60)/2] = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40
    %ΔP = 10 ÷ [(30 + 40)/2] = 10 ÷ 35 = 0.286
    E_S = 0.40 ÷ 0.286 = 1.40   → ELASTIC
    (the line hits the price axis at P = $10, above 0)

(b) Q = 20 + P:  P = 30 → Q = 50;  P = 40 → Q = 60
    %ΔQ = 10 ÷ [(50 + 60)/2] = 10 ÷ 55 = 0.182
    %ΔP = 10 ÷ 35 = 0.286
    E_S = 0.182 ÷ 0.286 = 0.64  → INELASTIC
    (the line hits the quantity axis at Q = 20)

Determinants of supply elasticity

Dr. Mang gives two: the availability of inputs, and the passage of time. In the short run it is hard to hire and fire workers or source materials and capital, so supply is less elastic. In the long run firms can hire and train, open or close facilities, and substitute materials or processes, so supply is more elastic. Hubbard names one main determinant: the ability and willingness of firms to alter the quantity they produce as price increases, which comes down to time (a pizza restaurant can buy ingredients in a day or two, but needs months to add cooks and ovens). Its one exception: a product that uses a resource in fixed supply (a winery whose land is all planted with one grape) stays inelastic even in the long run. Note that time pushes both elasticities the same way: longer period, more elastic demand and more elastic supply.

Worked Example 8 — Why Supply Elasticity Matters for Price (Dr. Mang, slide 57)
Demand rises from P = 100 − Q to P = 140 − Q. Find the new equilibrium (a) if supply is elastic, P = 40 + 0.25Q, and (b) if supply is inelastic, P = 2Q − 44. Both supply curves pass through the starting equilibrium. Compare the price rises.
Start (both cases): 100 − Q = 40 + 0.25Q  → Q = 48, P = $52
                    (check, inelastic S: 100 − Q = 2Q − 44 → Q = 48 ✓)

(a) ELASTIC supply:   140 − Q = 40 + 0.25Q  → 100 = 1.25Q
      Q = 80, P = 140 − 80 = $60       price rises $8,  quantity +32

Same demand shift: the price jump is more than three times as big

Dr. Mang’s rule: for a given change in consumer demand, the market price will change more when producers are less price responsive. Hubbard shows it with Canada Day parking at a beach resort (Figure 6.5): the normal price is $2.00 an hour for 1,200 spaces. When demand jumps and supply is inelastic, the price rises to $4.00 and quantity only to 1,400. When vacant land can be turned into parking (elastic supply), the price rises only to $2.50 and quantity to 2,100.

Hubbard “Apply the Concept” — why are oil prices so unstable? Both short-run supply (years from exploration to production) and short-run demand (no close substitutes) for oil are inelastic. When demand grew after the recession, quantity rose less than 5% (85 to 89 million barrels a day) while price rose 175%, from $40 to $110 a barrel. When US fracking added supply, the price fell from $110 (2011) to $47 (2017). Low elasticities on both sides mean small shifts cause large swings in price.
Memory Aid — supply “IT” depends on Inputs and Time Supply elasticity is driven by Inputs (can the firm get more of them?) and Time (the longer the period, the more elastic).
  • Direction rule: inputs easy to get, or a longer period → more elastic supply. Short run, scarce inputs → less elastic.
  • Trap: Hubbard names only time as the main determinant; Dr. Mang lists inputs and time. Learn both and expect either wording.
Self-test: is the supply of beachfront parking on Canada Day elastic or inelastic, and which of IT explains it? (Inelastic; short time and fixed land as the input.)
Section 8

Application: Why Good Harvests Can Hurt Farmers

Textbook §6.5 and Solved Problem 6.3 — not part of Dr. Mang’s Lessons 5.1–5.4, but they are in the reading, MyLab and the end-of-chapter problems.

Hubbard §6.5 asks why family farms are disappearing. In 1931 Canada had 728,623 farms (average about 91 hectares) and almost 3.3 million people, close to 31.7% of the population, lived on farms. By the 2016 Census of Agriculture only about 193,492 farms remained and about 2% of Canadians lived on them. Over the same stretch wheat output rose from about 320 million bushels (1931) to about 1,102 million bushels (2017), even though wheat acreage fell, because of better strains and bigger, more productive machinery. Two elasticity facts explain what happened to prices and farm income:

  • The price elasticity of demand for wheat is inelastic.
  • The income elasticity of demand for wheat is low: as incomes grow, people do not buy much more bread, so the demand curve shifts right only a little.

Hubbard’s Figure 6.4 shows Canadian farmers producing about 14.1 million tonnes of wheat at $498.80 a tonne in 1960 and about 30 million tonnes at $275.06 a tonne in 2017 (2013 dollars). Supply shifted far to the right, demand shifted only a little, and inelastic demand turned a big supply increase into a big price fall. Only the most efficient farms stay profitable. Hubbard calls this “the paradox of North American farming”: ever more abundant, cheaper food from fewer and fewer farms.

Worked Example 9 — A Bumper Harvest (illustrative numbers)
These numbers are hypothetical, chosen to match the pattern of the story. Wheat demand is P = 24 − 0.2Q (Q in millions of bushels, P in dollars). Productivity gains shift supply from P = −6 + 0.1Q to P = −9 + 0.1Q. Find the equilibria, total revenue before and after, and the elasticity of demand between them.
Before: 24 − 0.2Q = −6 + 0.1Q  → 30 = 0.3Q  → Q = 100, P = $4
        TR = 4 × 100 = $400 million

After:  24 − 0.2Q = −9 + 0.1Q  → 33 = 0.3Q  → Q = 110, P = $2
        TR = 2 × 110 = $220 million

Midpoint elasticity of demand between the two points:
  %ΔQ = 10 ÷ [(100 + 110)/2] = 10 ÷ 105 = 0.0952
  %ΔP = −2 ÷ [(4 + 2)/2]     = −2 ÷ 3   = −0.6667
  E = 0.0952 ÷ (−0.6667) = −0.14   → very INELASTIC

Output rises 10%, but price falls two-thirds, so farm revenue drops
from $400 million to $220 million, a loss of $180 million.
Hubbard Solved Problem 6.3 — back to the soft drink tax A tax of $0.75 a bottle, starting at 90,000 bottles a day at $1.75. If demand is inelastic, quantity falls to 80,000 and the price rises to $2.25, so tax revenue = $0.75 × 80,000 = $60,000 a day. If demand is elastic, quantity falls to 70,000 and the price rises only to $2.00, so revenue = $0.75 × 70,000 = $52,500 a day. A soft drink tax is more effective at reducing consumption when demand is elastic, but more effective at raising revenue when demand is inelastic. This is the tension in the Section 1 opener.
Vocabulary Trap — you need both elasticities for the farm story Inelastic price elasticity alone is not the whole explanation: it makes any price fall large. The income elasticity being low explains why demand grew so little while supply grew so much. If demand had been highly income elastic, rising incomes would have pushed the demand curve out and propped up the price.
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 drills the midpoint formula and the revenue test, Problem 2 the determinants, Problem 3 income and cross-price elasticities, and Problem 4 supply. Problem 5 is Dr. Mang’s in-class exercise from the Unit 5 lecture, which puts it all together.

Problem 1 — Concert Tickets

A concert venue raises the price of tickets from $40 to $60, and the quantity sold falls from 1,000 to 800. (a) Find the price elasticity of demand and classify it. (b) What happens to total revenue? Is that what the total-revenue test predicts?

Show answer
(a) %ΔQ = −200 ÷ 900 = −0.222; %ΔP = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40. E = −0.222 ÷ 0.40 = −0.56 → inelastic.

(b) TR rises from 40 × 1,000 = $40,000 to 60 × 800 = $48,000. Yes: for inelastic demand, a price rise raises revenue.
Problem 2 — Which Is More Elastic?

For each pair, pick the more elastic demand and name the STuMBLe determinant. (a) Petro-Canada gas at one station vs. gasoline in general. (b) Electricity next week vs. over the next 10 years. (c) A pack of gum vs. university tuition. (d) Cruise vacations vs. prescription medicine.

Show answer
(a) Petro-Canada at one station — market definition (narrow; other stations are substitutes).

(b) Over 10 years — time.

(c) University tuition — budget share.

(d) Cruise vacations — luxury vs. necessity (medicine is a necessity, often without substitutes).
Problem 3 — Income and Cross-Price

(a) Income rises from $50,000 to $70,000 and bus rides fall from 40 to 30 a month. Find the income elasticity and classify the good. (b) The price of an Xbox rises from $400 to $500 and PlayStation sales go from 100 to 130. Find the cross-price elasticity and say how the goods are related.

Show answer
(a) %ΔQ = −10 ÷ 35 = −28.6%; %ΔI = 20,000 ÷ 60,000 = 33.3%. EI = −28.6 ÷ 33.3 = −0.86 → inferior (negative).

(b) %ΔQ = 30 ÷ 115 = 26.1%; %ΔP = 100 ÷ 450 = 22.2%. Exy = 26.1 ÷ 22.2 = +1.17 → substitutes (positive).
Problem 4 — Supply

The price rises from $10 to $12 and the quantity supplied goes from 200 to 260. Find the price elasticity of supply and classify it. Would it be more or less elastic in the long run? Why?

Show answer
%ΔQs = 60 ÷ 230 = 0.261; %ΔP = 2 ÷ 11 = 0.182. ES = 0.261 ÷ 0.182 = 1.43 → elastic.

It would be more elastic in the long run: with more time, firms can hire, train, expand capacity and switch inputs (time, the “T” in IT).
Problem 5 — Dr. Mang’s In-Class Exercise: P = 260 − 3Q

Demand is P = 260 − 3Q. (a) Find E between $170 and $155. (b) Find E between $50 and $35. (c) Find total revenue at each of the four prices and check it against (a) and (b). (d) Find the unit-elastic point and the maximum total revenue. (e) A firm is charging $50. Should it raise or cut its price to get more revenue?

Show answer
(a) Q = (260 − P) ÷ 3: P = 170 → Q = 30; P = 155 → Q = 35. %ΔQ = 5 ÷ 32.5 = 0.1538; %ΔP = −15 ÷ 162.5 = −0.0923. E = 0.1538 ÷ (−0.0923) = −1.67 → elastic.

(b) P = 50 → Q = 70; P = 35 → Q = 75. %ΔQ = 5 ÷ 72.5 = 0.0690; %ΔP = −15 ÷ 42.5 = −0.3529. E = 0.0690 ÷ (−0.3529) = −0.20 → inelastic.

(c) $170 × 30 = $5,100 and $155 × 35 = $5,425, so TR rises $325 on the price cut (elastic, consistent with (a)). $50 × 70 = $3,500 and $35 × 75 = $2,625, so TR falls $875 (inelastic, consistent with (b)).

(d) The unit-elastic point is the midpoint of the line: the P-intercept is 260 and the Q-intercept is 260 ÷ 3 = 86.67, so the midpoint is Q = 260 ÷ 6 = 43.33 and P = $130. Maximum TR = 130 × 43.33 = $5,633.33.

(e) Raise it. $50 is well below $130, in the inelastic region (E = −0.20 near there), so a higher price raises revenue.
