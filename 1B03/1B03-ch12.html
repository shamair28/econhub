Section 1

What Is an Oligopoly? Measuring Concentration

Oligopoly (Slides, Lesson 10.1)

An oligopoly is a market with only a few major firms. At the most extreme, there are only two firms: a duopoly. With only a few firms, each has a large market share and market power (a downward-sloping product demand). Barriers to entry prevent new firms from joining.

  • Homogeneous oligopoly: the firms produce undifferentiated goods and compete only on price (cement, steel, gasoline).
  • Differentiated oligopoly: the firms produce differentiated goods and compete on both price and quality (cars, phones, airlines).

Measuring concentration

How “few” is few? Two measures (Dr. Mang’s lecture deck):

Concentration ratio Cj

The revenue of the largest j firms as a share of total industry revenue: C_j = (R₁ + R₂ + … + R_j) ÷ (R₁ + … + R_n). Usually C₃, C₄ or C₁₀. It ranges from 0% to 100%; the larger it is, the more concentrated the industry. Cj = 100% means there are no more than j firms.

Textbook: Statistics Canada publishes four-firm ratios; most economists treat C₄ > 40% as a sign of oligopoly. Flaws: they ignore imports, they’re national when competition may be local (restaurants), and they miss competition between industries (Walmart competes with supermarkets and toy stores).

Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)

HHI = 10,000 × Σ (each firm’s share)², which is the same as adding up the squared percentage shares. It ranges from 0 to 10,000 (one firm with 100%).

  • HHI < 100: highly / perfectly competitive
  • 100 – 1,500: unconcentrated
  • 1,500 – 2,500: moderately concentrated
  • HHI > 2,500: highly concentrated
Worked Example 1 — C₄ and HHI
Five firms share an industry’s revenue: 35%, 30%, 20%, 10% and 5%. Find C₄ and the HHI, and classify the industry.
C₄ = 35 + 30 + 20 + 10 = 95%          (> 40% → an oligopoly, by Hubbard’s rule)

HHI = 35² + 30² + 20² + 10² + 5²
    = 1,225 + 900 + 400 + 100 + 25 = 2,650   (> 2,500 → highly concentrated)

Why square? Big firms count for more. Ten firms with 10% each also
have C₄ = 40%, but HHI = 10 × 10² = 1,000 (unconcentrated).
Memory Aid — HHI bands: “1-15-25” Under 100: competitive · 1,500 and 2,500 are the two cut-offs: unconcentrated → moderately → highly concentrated.
  • Direction rule: square the percentages (35² = 1,225), not the decimals, and don’t multiply again by 10,000.
  • Trap: the HHI uses all firms; C₄ uses only the top four.
Self-test: a duopoly with 50% each — HHI? (5,000: highly concentrated.)

Section 2

Barriers to Entry

In perfect and monopolistic competition, economic profits attract new firms that compete them away. That does not happen in an oligopoly. A barrier to entry is anything that prevents new firms from entering. Mang lists four:

1 · Economies of scale

If the minimum efficient scale (MES) is small relative to market demand, there’s room for many firms to operate efficiently, so the market is competitive. If MES is large relative to market demand, only a few firms can be efficient. A small firm producing a small quantity has a higher average cost and can’t survive against a large one. (Hubbard calls this the most important barrier.)

2 · Government regulations

Licences: some industries need one to operate, and the government can limit how many it issues. Patents: the inventor’s exclusive right to make a product for 20 years. Copyrights: the creator’s exclusive right to copy, distribute and profit from a work for life + 70 years. Patents and copyrights encourage creators by protecting their investment.

3 · Limited ownership of a resource

When production needs an input that isn’t widely available, new firms struggle to enter. The resource can be natural (Hubbard: PotashCorp controls most of the world’s potash), human (scarce specialists) or knowledge (know-how, trade secrets). Brand value can be a resource too.

4 · Operating history and brand recognition

Customers trust names they know. A brand-new airline or bank has to overcome years of reputation its rivals already have.

Mang’s “Why oligopoly?” examples: car manufacturers (high capital and R&D costs), telecommunications (high capital costs; the government restricts wireless and broadcast spectrum), airlines (high capital and knowledge costs; airport landing fees and restrictions).

Memory Aid — oligopoly barriers are “SLOB” Scale economies · Law (licences, patents, copyrights) · Ownership of a resource · Brand (operating history and recognition).
  • Direction rule: the bigger MES is relative to market demand, the fewer firms the market can hold. A huge MES in a tiny market → natural monopoly (Unit 11).
  • Trap: Hubbard lists three barriers (economies of scale, ownership of a key input, government-imposed barriers). Mang adds brand recognition as a fourth.
Self-test: which barrier keeps new carriers out of Canada’s wireless market? (Scale — huge network costs — and Law — spectrum licences.)
Section 3

Profit Maximization in an Oligopoly

Each oligopolist faces a downward-sloping demand, so it uses the same recipe as Unit 9: produce where MR = MC (call it qOL), charge the price on its demand curve, and P > MC gives a price markup (P − MC). What’s different: a profit can last into the long run, because barriers stop new firms from entering to compete it away.

Worked Example 2 — A Wireless Carrier
A carrier faces demand P = 150 − q (thousands of plans), with MC = 30 + q and ATC = 1,200/q + 30 + 0.5q. Find q, P, the markup and the profit. Then suppose a higher fixed cost of $3,000 makes ATC = 3,000/q + 30 + 0.5q. Finally, a rival goes out of business and this carrier’s demand rises to P = 180 − q.
MR = 150 − 2q = 30 + q → q = 40;  P = 150 − 40 = $110
MC(40) = $70 → markup = $40
ATC(40) = 30 + 30 + 20 = $80 → π = (110 − 80) × 40 = $1,200

FC = $3,000: same q and P, ATC(40) = 75 + 30 + 20 = $125
  → loss = (125 − 110) × 40 = $600 (loss-minimizing q)

Rival exits: d' = 180 − q → MR = 180 − 2q = 30 + q → q = 50, P = $130
  ATC(50) = 60 + 30 + 25 = $115 → π = (130 − 115) × 50 = $750
Losses in an oligopoly (Slides) When P < ATC, qOL is the loss-minimizing quantity. Two ways out: a firm might go out of business, which increases demand for the remaining firms’ goods as its customers move to them, or the firm can try to reduce its average total cost over the long run. With so few firms, one firm’s exit is a big deal for the others. That’s the first hint of the strategic thinking in Section 4.
Section 4

Strategic Behaviour and Game Theory

Mang’s story (Lesson 10.2): two firms, A and B. Firm B spends more on advertising. Its ATC rises, but so does demand for its product: more customers, more profit. Where did those customers come from? Some of them from Firm A, whose demand falls, so it faces pressure to cut its price, and its profit falls. Will A accept that? Maybe… maybe not.

Mutual interdependence

In an oligopoly a firm’s profit depends not just on its own decisions but also on the decisions of its rivals. When making any business decision, an oligopolist must consider how its rivals will react. Oligopolists behave more strategically than monopolistically competitive firms. Game theory is the tool economists use to predict the actions of agents in these situations.

Game-theory vocabulary (Slides)

TermMeaning
ConjectureAn assumption one player makes about the behaviour of a competitor.
StrategyA choice made from a selection of possible actions.
Best responseThe choice that maximizes profit given an assumption about what the competitor will do.
Dominant strategyA choice that maximizes profit regardless of what competitors do (the best response to every scenario).
Nash equilibriumBoth players choose a best response: each is satisfied with its own choice after seeing the opponent’s.
Dominant strategy equilibriumBoth players play a dominant strategy. Dominant strategies don’t always exist, so this outcome doesn’t occur in every game.
Payoff matrix (Textbook)A table showing each firm’s payoff from every combination of strategies.
Vocabulary Trap — every dominant strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, not the other way round A dominant strategy is a best response to everything, so if both play one, both are best-responding: Nash. But a Nash equilibrium only needs each player to best-respond to what the other actually does. The Front Page game (Section 7) has two Nash equilibria and no dominant strategies at all.
Memory Aid — the circle method Mang marks best responses with a circle.
  1. Fix the rival’s choice (one column). Circle your bigger payoff in that column. Repeat for the other column.
  2. Do the same for the rival, one row at a time.
  3. Same row circled in both columns → dominant strategy. A cell with both payoffs circled → Nash equilibrium.
Self-test: in a 2 × 2 game, how many circles do you draw in total? (Four: two for each player, one per rival choice, unless there’s a tie.)

The Advertising Game: a Prisoner’s Dilemma

Two duopolists each choose a Low or High advertising budget. If they choose the same budget, they split the market evenly. If one goes High and the other Low, the High spender gets most of the customers. Profits in $ millions (A’s first, B’s second):

Firm B
Low budgetHigh budget
Firm ALow budgetA: $10m · B: $10mA: $5m · B: $12m
High budgetA: $12m · B: $5mA: $7m · B: $7m
Dr. Mang’s Advertising Game (Lesson 10.2). Circles = best responses; green = Nash equilibrium.
Worked Example 3 — Solving the Advertising Game
Find each firm’s best responses, any dominant strategies, and the equilibrium.
Firm B’s best responses (compare B’s payoffs across a row):
  if A goes Low:  High (12) beats Low (10) → High
  if A goes High: High (7)  beats Low (5)  → High
  → High is B’s DOMINANT strategy

Firm A (compare A’s payoffs down a column), by symmetry:
  if B goes Low:  High (12) beats Low (10)
  if B goes High: High (7)  beats Low (5)  → High is dominant

Outcome: (High, High) → $7m each
  Both play best responses     → Nash equilibrium
  Both play dominant strategies → dominant strategy equilibrium
But (Low, Low) would give $10m each: both are worse off.

Before the change, both firms had lower costs and higher profits. Now both spend more on ads, have higher costs and lower profits, and neither took customers from the other. Mang’s lesson: individual agents working in their own self-interest can naturally end up in a situation that is not socially optimal (more of this in Unit 12).

Prisoner’s dilemma (Textbook)

A game where pursuing dominant strategies results in noncooperation that leaves everyone worse off. The outcome is a noncooperative equilibrium; the better outcome where the players cooperate to increase their mutual payoff is a cooperative equilibrium.

Hubbard’s version (Figure 12.3): Apple and Spotify each charge $14.99 or $9.99 for streaming. Both at $14.99 → $10m each; one undercuts → $15m for the undercutter, $5m for the other; both at $9.99 → $7.5m each. Charging $9.99 is dominant for both, so they end up at $7.5m instead of $10m.

Memory Aid — spotting a prisoner’s dilemma: “Both dominant, both sorry” Both players have a dominant strategy, and the cell they land in is worse for both than another cell.
  • Trap: a game with a dominant strategy isn’t automatically a prisoner’s dilemma. The equilibrium must leave both players worse off than some other outcome.
Self-test: why can’t the two firms simply agree to stay Low? (That’s collusion: illegal, and each still gains by cheating. See Section 6.)
Section 6

Collusion and Cartels

The firms would clearly prefer not to compete: keep prices high and don’t undercut to steal customers, keep advertising low, pay low wages and don’t bid for each other’s workers.

Collusion and cartels (Slides, Lesson 10.3)

When firms coordinate their actions to raise prices above the competitive level or cut costs below the competitive level, it’s collusion. Firms participating are a trust or a cartel. Collusion is illegal in developed countries because it harms consumers, workers or both. Antitrust laws have been enforced since the late 19th century (Canada’s first, 1889, is the oldest in the Western world — see Ch. 13).

Why collusion doesn’t work
  • Collusion can work for a short time, but firms have an incentive to cheat: in the payoff matrix, undercutting the agreed price is still each firm’s best response.
  • If firms believe the agreement won’t last forever, they have an incentive to be the first one to abandon it.
  • Confession brings lesser penalties, so individual managers or employees have an incentive to report the cartel to competition authorities. Whistleblower legislation protects employees who report illegal activity.

How firms escape the dilemma legally (Textbook)

  • Repeated games. Most business “games” are played over and over. Cheating today invites retaliation tomorrow, so the losses from not cooperating are bigger than in a one-shot game. Hubbard’s pizza example: Domino’s and Pizza Hut choosing $12 or $10 every day. A simple retaliation strategy is tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then copy whatever the rival did last round. Mang’s version: in a repeated game, players can learn from their opponent’s actions and send signals through their own.
  • Price leadership (implicit collusion): one firm announces a price change and the others match it (GM, Ford and Chrysler in the 1970s). No explicit agreement, so it’s harder to prosecute.
  • Price-matching guarantees (Unit 11, Lesson 11.3): if every rival promises to match any lower price, undercutting wins no customers, so high prices become stable.
OPEC: a cartel that keeps cracking (Textbook) OPEC members agree on production quotas to keep oil prices high, but each member gains by quietly producing more than its quota. When too many cheat, prices fall. Members with very different sizes and reserves (Saudi Arabia vs smaller producers) find it especially hard to agree. That’s why oil prices swing so much.
Memory Aid — cartels crack because of “CFW” Cheat (each firm’s best response) · First to leave wins · Whistleblowers get lighter penalties. Self-test: in the Advertising Game, if both firms agree to go Low, what is A’s payoff from secretly going High? ($12m instead of $10m: the incentive to cheat.)

Games Without Dominant Strategies

Two local newspapers decide each day whether their front-page story is about Politics or Business. More readers prefer politics. Readers in thousands (A’s first, B’s second):

Newspaper B
PoliticsBusiness
Newspaper APoliticsA: 50 · B: 50A: 100 · B: 70
BusinessA: 70 · B: 100A: 35 · B: 35
Dr. Mang’s Front Page Game (Lesson 10.3). Two Nash equilibria (green).
Worked Example 4 — The Front Page Game
Find each paper’s best responses. Does either have a dominant strategy? Find every Nash equilibrium.
Newspaper A:
  if B runs Politics: Business (70) beats Politics (50) → Business
  if B runs Business: Politics (100) beats Business (35) → Politics
  → best response changes with B’s choice: NO dominant strategy
Newspaper B (symmetric): also no dominant strategy

Cells with both payoffs circled:
  (A Politics, B Business) → 100, 70
  (A Business, B Politics) → 70, 100
→ two Nash equilibria; no dominant strategy equilibrium

Each paper wants to be the one running politics, but both prefer splitting the topics to clashing. Outcomes like (Politics, Politics) can happen too, but they’re out of equilibrium: at least one paper would want to switch.

Signalling and updating (Slides) Played only once, a game without dominant strategies gives no chance to learn what your opponent will do. Repeated, players can learn from each other’s actions and signal through their own (a paper that runs politics every day signals “that’s our beat”). Rational players use all the information available.
Section 8

Sequential Games and the Five Competitive Forces

Textbook only (Hubbard 12.3–12.4). So far both players moved at the same time. In a sequential game one firm moves first and the other responds. We draw it as a decision tree and solve it backward: work out the last mover’s best choice at each node first, then the first mover chooses knowing those responses.

Worked Example 5 — Apple Deters Dell (Hubbard Figure 12.7)
Apple is launching a thin, light laptop and picks a price ($1,000 or $800). Dell then decides whether to enter. Each firm needs a 15% return to break even. Rates of return: $1,000 + Dell enters → 16% each; $1,000 + no entry → Apple 30%; $800 + Dell enters → 5% each; $800 + no entry → Apple 20%.
Step 1 — Dell (the last mover):
  after $1,000: enter → 16% > 15% needed → ENTER
  after $800:   enter → 5%  < 15% needed → STAY OUT

Step 2 — Apple, knowing Dell’s replies:
  $1,000 → Dell enters → Apple earns 16%
  $800   → Dell stays out → Apple earns 20%
→ Apple charges $800 and deters entry (20% > 16%)

Is deterring entry always a good idea? No: if Apple’s return from the low price were below 16%, it would do better charging $1,000 and sharing the market. Hubbard applies the same tree logic to bargaining, e.g., a small software firm negotiating with Dell over a royalty.

The five competitive forces model (Textbook)

The number of competitors isn’t the only thing that matters. Michael Porter’s model says five forces determine the level of competition and profit in an industry:

  1. Competition from existing firms
  2. The threat from potential entrants
  3. Competition from substitute goods or services
  4. The bargaining power of buyers
  5. The bargaining power of suppliers
Memory Aid — the five forces are “RESBS”: Rivals, Entrants, Substitutes, Buyers, Suppliers Picture the firm in the middle: rivals beside it, entrants at the door, substitutes across the street, buyers on one side and suppliers on the other. Each can push its profit down. Self-test: a dominant ride-share app raises fees until drivers switch to a rival app. Which force pushed back? (Bargaining power of suppliers: the drivers supply the rides.)

Section 9

Welfare Effects of Oligopoly

Lesson 10.4. From Unit 5: the availability of substitutes is a main determinant of the price elasticity of demand. Fewer competitors → fewer substitutes → less elastic demand → a bigger price markup → a larger deadweight loss. Consumers are worse off. The deadweight loss in an oligopoly is larger than in monopolistic competition. Oligopolies can also earn excess economic profits in the long run, because barriers to entry keep competitors out.

Oligopoly and inequality (Slides)

Unit 1: income and consumption inequality are a major disadvantage of market economies. Oligopoly firms can earn excess profits and grow into very large enterprises, so their owners can accumulate a disproportionately large share of wealth and income.

Oligopolies and innovation — Schumpeter

Joseph Schumpeter argued that oligopoly is the most beneficial market structure, so long as oligopolists compete rather than collude. Innovation needs R&D spending, and perfect and monopolistic competition generate no excess profit to fund it. Only oligopoly generates (a) the excess profits needed to fund R&D in the first place and (b) the excess profits that provide a return on that investment (remember patents!). Because oligopolists fight for market share, they have an incentive to keep innovating. A firm that fails to innovate is eventually overtaken and goes out of business: creative destruction.

  • Product innovation creates new goods and new markets, from which society draws economic surplus (Unit 4), raising social welfare.
  • Process innovation creates new production methods that improve productive efficiency (Unit 1): more output from fewer inputs.

Both kinds can be protected by patents.

Are oligopolies that bad? And Canada’s competition problem (Slides) Oligopolies have disadvantages (markups, DWL, inequality) and advantages (innovation). The benefits only appear when oligopolies compete, so governments must actively encourage competition and prevent collusion. Mang’s lecture deck notes that Canadian industries have become less competitive over time: markups and profits have been rising, which hurts consumers, and Canada invests comparatively less in R&D than other major developed countries.
Memory Aid — oligopoly’s report card: “3 minuses, 1 big plus” − bigger markup and DWL than monopolistic competition · − lasting excess profits · − inequality · + innovation (only if they compete). Self-test: according to Schumpeter, why can’t perfect competition fund much R&D? (Long-run economic profit is zero: no excess profit to invest or to reward the investment.)
Practice

Practice Problems

Problem 1 is an oligopoly graph from equations, Problem 2 concentration measures (with a Unit 11 link), Problems 3–4 payoff matrices, and Problem 5 a sequential game. More matrices with new numbers every time: Graph Lab Unit 10 drills.

Problem 1 — An Airline Route

One of three airlines on a route faces P = 200 − 2q, with MC = 20 + 2q and ATC = 2,000/q + 20 + q. (a) Find the profit-maximizing q and P. (b) Find the markup and the profit. (c) Why doesn’t this profit attract new airlines the way it would in Unit 9?

Show answer
(a) MR = 200 − 4q = 20 + 2q → q = 30; P = 200 − 60 = $140.

(b) MC(30) = $80 → markup = $60. ATC(30) = 66.67 + 20 + 30 = $116.67 → π = (140 − 116.67) × 30 = $700 (TR 4,200 − TC 3,500).

(c) Barriers to entry: huge capital costs (scale), landing slots and fees (law), and brand/operating history. Entry can’t compete the profit away, so it can last.
Problem 2 — Concentration and a Merger

Six firms have revenue shares of 30%, 25%, 20%, 15%, 5% and 5%. (a) Find C₄ and the HHI and classify the industry. (b) The 15% and one 5% firm merge. Find the new HHI. (c) Using Unit 11’s Competition Bureau rule of thumb, would the Bureau look closely?

Show answer
(a) C₄ = 30 + 25 + 20 + 15 = 90% (oligopoly). HHI = 900 + 625 + 400 + 225 + 25 + 25 = 2,200 → moderately concentrated.

(b) Shares 30, 25, 20, 20, 5 → HHI = 900 + 625 + 400 + 400 + 25 = 2,350 (still moderate, but up 150).

(c) The new firm has 20% (< 35%), but the top four would hold 30 + 25 + 20 + 20 = 95% > 65% → yes, the Bureau would examine it more closely.
Problem 3 — A Coffee Price War

Two coffee chains each charge a High or Low price. Profits ($m, A’s first): High/High (8, 8); A High, B Low (3, 10); A Low, B High (10, 3); Low/Low (5, 5). (a) Find each chain’s dominant strategy. (b) Find the Nash equilibrium. Is it a dominant strategy equilibrium? (c) Is this a prisoner’s dilemma? (d) Why would an agreement to charge High break down?

Show answer
(a) A: if B High, Low (10) > High (8); if B Low, Low (5) > High (3) → Low is dominant. B is symmetric: Low.

(b) (Low, Low), $5m each. Both play dominant strategies → also a dominant strategy equilibrium.

(c) Yes: both have dominant strategies and both would earn more at (High, High), $8m vs $5m.

(d) Each chain earns $10m instead of $8m by secretly cutting its price (an incentive to cheat). Collusion is also illegal, and anyone who reports it gets a lighter penalty.
Problem 4 — Radio Formats

Two radio stations each pick a Country or Rock format. Listeners (000s, A’s first): Country/Country (20, 20); A Country, B Rock (60, 50); A Rock, B Country (50, 60); Rock/Rock (25, 25). (a) Does either station have a dominant strategy? (b) Find all Nash equilibria. (c) If the game is repeated every year, how might a station steer toward the equilibrium it prefers?

Show answer
(a) A: if B Country → Rock (50 > 20); if B Rock → Country (60 > 25). The best response changes, so no dominant strategy. Same for B.

(b) (Country, Rock) → (60, 50) and (Rock, Country) → (50, 60). Two Nash equilibria, no dominant strategy equilibrium.

(c) By signalling: committing to its preferred format (announcing it, investing in country hosts) so the rival learns that switching to the other format is its best response.
Problem 5 — Entry Deterrence at an Airport

An incumbent airline first chooses to Expand capacity or keep it Normal. A rival then decides to Enter or Stay out. Payoffs ($m; incumbent, rival): Expand → Enter (2, −1); Expand → Stay out (6, 0); Normal → Enter (4, 3); Normal → Stay out (9, 0).
(a) Solve by backward induction. What does the rival do after each choice, and what does the incumbent choose?
(b) Why isn’t (Normal, Stay out), which pays the incumbent $9m, the outcome?
(c) Suppose expanding costs more, so Expand → Stay out pays the incumbent only $3m. What changes?
(d) Which of the five competitive forces is the incumbent managing here?

Show answer
(a) After Expand, the rival compares −1 (enter) with 0 (stay out) → Stay out. After Normal, 3 vs 0 → Enter. The incumbent compares Expand → $6m with Normal → $4m → Expand. Equilibrium: (Expand, Stay out) with payoffs (6, 0).

(b) Because after Normal the rival would enter (3 > 0). The incumbent can’t get $9m; the rival’s reply decides which outcomes are reachable.

(c) Now Expand → $3m vs Normal → $4m, so the incumbent keeps capacity Normal and accepts entry: (Normal, Enter), payoffs (4, 3). Deterring entry isn’t always worth it.

(d) The threat from potential entrants.
