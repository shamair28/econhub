Section 1

What Is a Monopoly?

Unit 8 built the most competitive market possible. Units 9 and 10 let firms differentiate their products and act strategically. Unit 11 goes to the other extreme. Dr. Mang’s Lesson 11.1 and Hubbard (13.1) use the same definition:

Monopoly

A monopoly is the only seller of a good or service that does not have a close substitute. Barriers to entry prevent other firms from entering.

Because there is only one firm, the firm’s demand curve is the market demand curve: d = D, and the firm’s quantity is the market quantity: q = Q.

The tricky word is close. Something substitutes for almost everything. Candles substitute for electric light, but so weakly that your electric utility can ignore candle prices. Hubbard gives two tests:

  • Narrow definition: a firm is a monopoly if it can ignore the actions of all other firms — no one else sells a close enough substitute to matter.
  • Broader definition (the one most economists use): a firm is a monopoly if no other firm can compete away its economic profit in the long run. The only pizza parlour in a small town competes with burgers and fried chicken, but as long as no second pizza place can open, its profit survives — so it is treated as a monopoly.
Don’t Let This Happen to You — “the only one” ≠ “a monopoly” Hubbard’s Netflix example: for years Netflix was Canada’s only legal streaming service, but it was never a monopoly. TV could always be watched on cable or satellite (close substitutes), and other firms could enter easily — Amazon Prime, Crave and others did. A monopoly needs both no close substitutes and barriers that keep rivals out.

Section 2

Where Monopolies Come From: Barriers to Entry

Every firm would like to be a monopoly. To stay one, the barriers keeping rivals out must be high. Dr. Mang’s Unit 11 slides list three sources, “just like in Unit 10” (his Unit 10 list also had a fourth, operating history and brand recognition — see Ch 12). Hubbard (13.2) lists four, adding network externalities. (Mang’s lecture deck folds network platforms and brands into “ownership of a resource”.)

1 · Economies of scale → natural monopoly

If the minimum efficient scale (MES) is larger than the quantity consumers demand, one firm supplying the whole market produces at a lower average cost than several smaller firms. That is a natural monopoly. The graph test: ATC is still falling where it crosses the demand curve.

Hubbard’s electricity example (Figure 1): one firm producing 30 billion kWh has ATC = $0.04. Split the market between two firms producing 15 billion each and ATC rises to $0.06. Whichever firm expands moves down its ATC, undercuts the other and drives it out — the monopoly arises “naturally”. Natural monopolies appear where fixed costs are huge and marginal cost is small: power lines, water mains, pipelines, rail track.

2 · Government action blocks entry

Patents (exclusive right to a new product, 20 years from filing), copyrights (creator’s lifetime + 50 years in Hubbard’s Canadian figures; Canada extended it to 70 years in 2022) and licences, all from Unit 10. Here Mang adds the public franchise: the government gives one firm the sole right to provide a good or service. Sometimes it grants the franchise to itself and creates a state-owned enterprise (SOE), which Canadians call a Crown corporation (e.g., provincial electricity utilities, public auto insurance in BC, Saskatchewan and Manitoba).

Why grant patents? Without them, rivals could copy a new drug the day it launched, and no one would spend ~12 years and huge sums developing it. When Prozac’s patent expired and generics entered, its sales fell by more than 80%.

3 · Ownership of a key resource

From Unit 10: natural, human and knowledge resources. Mang adds that owning a physical premises is a common source of monopoly power: amusement-park and movie-theatre concessions, stadium merchandise and food. (His lecture deck also lists owning a brand or a platform with network externalities.)

Textbook examples: De Beers bought up most of the world’s diamond supply to keep prices high; Alcoa controlled nearly all bauxite; International Nickel controlled 90%+ of nickel supplies; NHL teams own or lease the big arenas, which blocks a rival league.

4 · Network externalities (Textbook)

A product has network externalities if it becomes more useful the more people use it — operating systems, eBay, social networks. Once a firm is ahead, its lead feeds itself: more users → more valuable → more users. Hubbard notes economists debate how strong a barrier this really is (Windows’ and eBay’s shares have slowly fallen).

Memory Aid — barriers are “SLOG”: why rivals can’t get in Scale (economies of scale → natural monopoly) · Law (government: patents, copyrights, licences, public franchises, Crown corporations) · Ownership of a key resource (incl. premises) · Growth of the network (network externalities — the textbook’s 4th).
  • Direction rule: natural monopoly = ATC still falling where it crosses D. If ATC has already turned up before D, there’s room for more than one firm.
  • Direction rule: huge fixed cost + small MC → strong economies of scale → natural monopoly.
  • Trap: Mang’s Unit 11 slides list three barriers (four in Unit 10, adding brand); Hubbard lists four. Learn Mang’s three for his test and recognize network externalities as Hubbard’s fourth.
Self-test: name the barrier behind Hydro One’s power lines, Pfizer’s new drug, popcorn at Cineplex, and Facebook. (Scale · Law · Ownership of premises · Growth of the network — also scale, per Hubbard’s Solved Problem 13.1.)
Vocabulary Trap — public franchise vs patent vs licence A patent protects a product or process (20 years). A copyright protects a creative work. A licence is government permission to operate (Unit 10; e.g., taxis). A public franchise makes one firm the only legal provider of a service. If the government keeps the franchise for itself, the firm is a state-owned enterprise / Crown corporation.

Section 3

Marginal Revenue for a Monopoly

A price taker can sell all it wants at the market price, so MR = P. A monopoly faces the whole downward-sloping market demand curve: to sell one more unit it must cut its price — and (selling at one price) the cut applies to every unit, not just the extra one. Hubbard’s “good thing / bad thing”:

  • The good thing: it sells one more unit and gains that unit’s price.
  • The bad thing: it gets less for every unit it could have sold at the higher price.

MR = the good thing − the bad thing, so MR < P for every unit after the first. As you learned in Units 9 and 10, this is true for every firm with market power.

The shortcut for straight-line demand

If demand is P = a − bq, then MR = a − 2bq. Same intercept, double the slope. MR hits zero at half the quantity where demand hits zero.

Mang’s slide example: P = 700 − 2q → MR = 700 − (2)2q = 700 − 4q. Demand reaches $0 at q = 350; MR reaches $0 at q = 175.

Memory Aid — “Same start, twice as steep” For linear demand, MR starts at the same price intercept and falls twice as fast.
  • Direction rule: where MR > 0, demand is elastic; where MR < 0, it’s inelastic (Unit 5). A monopolist never produces where MR < 0 — that unit lowers revenue and raises cost.
  • Trap: double the slope (the number on q), not the intercept. P = 120 − 3q → MR = 120 − 6q, not 240 − 6q.
Self-test: P = 90 − 0.5q. What is MR, and at what q is MR = 0? (MR = 90 − q; q = 90.)
Section 4

Profit Maximization: MR = MC, Then Read the Price

The rule hasn’t changed since Unit 8. While MR > MC, each unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so profit grows. Once MR < MC, each unit subtracts from profit. Profit is largest where MR = MC. What’s new is the price. For a price taker the price was given. A monopoly chooses its price, and it chooses the highest price the demand curve allows for the quantity it picked.

The monopoly recipe: Cross → Climb → Compare → Box
  1. Cross: find the quantity where MR crosses MC → qM.
  2. Climb: go straight up from qM to the demand curve and read across → the price PM. (Not to the MR curve, not to MC.)
  3. Compare: at qM, compare P with ATC. P > ATC → profit; P < ATC → loss.
  4. Box: profit = (P − ATC) × q, the rectangle between P and ATC, from 0 to qM.

Because P is read off demand and MR lies below demand, P > MC at the monopoly’s chosen output. Mang calls the gap the price markup: Markup = P − MC. Consumers pay more than it costs to make the last unit.

Worked Example 2 — Mang’s Slide Example: P = 700 − 2q
A monopoly has daily demand P = 700 − 2q, marginal cost MC = 6q and average total cost ATC = 8,000/q + 3q. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price, and the profit.
Step 1 — MR (same intercept, double the slope):
  MR = 700 − (2)2q = 700 − 4q

Step 2 — Cross: MR = MC
  700 − 4q = 6q
  700 = 10q            →  q = 70

Step 3 — Climb to demand:
  P = 700 − 2(70) = $560

Step 4 — Compare and box:
  ATC(70) = 8,000/70 + 3(70) = 114.29 + 210 = $324.29
  π = (P − ATC) × q = (560 − 324.29) × 70 = $16,500

Check with totals: TC = ATC × q = 8,000 + 3q² = 8,000 + 14,700 = $22,700
                   TR = 560 × 70 = $39,200 → π = 39,200 − 22,700 = $16,500 ✓

Markup = P − MC = 560 − 6(70) = 560 − 420 = $140

Notice that the TC behind this ATC is 8,000 + 3q²: fixed cost $8,000, and MC = 6q is the slope of TC. You can always rebuild TC as ATC × q.

A monopoly can lose money In the right panel fixed cost rises to $30,000. MR, MC, q and P don’t change (fixed cost never moves q). But ATC(70) = 30,000/70 + 210 = $638.57 > $560, so π = (560 − 638.57) × 70 = −$5,500. Mang: qM is then the loss-minimizing quantity, and the firm must try to reduce its ATC over the long run. Unit 8’s shutdown rule still applies: AVC(70) = 3(70) = $210 < $560, so it covers its variable costs and keeps operating in the short run.

The textbook’s version (Hubbard 13.3)

Back to Rogers (Worked Example 1). With U-shaped cost curves, the 6th subscription has MR = MC = $27, so Rogers sells 6 and charges the demand-curve price of $42. ATC at 6 subscriptions is $30, so profit = ($42 − $30) × 6 = $72 (TR $252 − TC $180). Because no rival can enter, that profit can last into the long run.

Don’t Let This Happen to You — a monopoly can’t charge “whatever it wants” Hubbard’s Solved Problem 13.2: Shaw’s profit-maximizing price is $24 for 6 subscriptions (π = $34). The city then adds a flat $25-per-month tax. It’s tempting to say Shaw just raises its price to pass the tax on. It doesn’t. The flat tax is a fixed cost: it doesn’t change MR or MC, so q = 6 and P = $24 are still best; profit simply falls to $9. A monopolist is limited by demand. A higher price loses customers, and $24 was already the profit-maximizing price.
Memory Aid — “Cross, then Climb” Find q where the marginals cross; climb to the demand curve for P.
  • Direction rule: MR > MC → expand output (and lower P along demand). MR < MC → cut output.
  • Direction rule: a change in fixed cost moves ATC only → q and P stay put, profit changes. A change in marginal cost moves MC → q and P change.
  • Trap: the price is NOT where MR = MC (that height is MC, $420 in Mang’s example). The price is on the demand curve above it ($560).
Self-test: in Mang’s example, what is the markup, and what would happen to q if MC rose to 8q? (Markup $140; MR = MC → 700 = 12q → q ≈ 58.3, P ≈ $583.33.)

Section 5

Monopoly vs Competition: Consumer Surplus and Deadweight Loss

Hubbard (13.4) asks: what if a perfectly competitive industry became a monopoly? The competitive industry’s supply curve is the sum of its firms’ MC curves, so it becomes the monopoly’s MC curve. Then:

  • Competition: output where demand meets supply (P = MC) → PC, QC.
  • Monopoly: output where MR = MC → less output and a higher price: QM < QC, PM > PC.
Worked Example 3 — Surplus in Mang’s Example (Hubbard’s A, B, C)
Use P = 700 − 2q and MC = 6q. Compare consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss under competition (P = MC) and monopoly (MR = MC).
Competition: 700 − 2q = 6q → q = 87.5, P = $525
  CS = ½ × 87.5 × (700 − 525) = $7,656.25
  PS = ½ × 87.5 × 525         = $22,968.75     total = $30,625

Monopoly: q = 70, P = $560, MC(70) = $420
  CS = ½ × 70 × (700 − 560)   = $4,900
  PS = area between $560 and MC from 0 to 70
     = 560(70) − 3(70²)       = $24,500        total = $29,400

The three pieces (Hubbard Figure 13.5):
  A = (560 − 525) × 70            = $2,450   CS that becomes PS
  B = ½ × (87.5 − 70) × (560 − 525) = $306.25  CS lost by everyone
  C = ½ × (87.5 − 70) × (525 − 420) = $918.75  PS lost by everyone

Link to Unit 8: PS here is TR − VC = 39,200 − 14,700 = $24,500, and profit = PS − FC = 24,500 − 8,000 = $16,500, the same as Worked Example 2.

Why the deadweight loss exists

Between q = 70 and q = 87.5, consumers value each unit (the demand curve) more than it costs to make (the MC curve). Those trades would make both sides better off, but the monopoly doesn’t make them, because selling more means cutting the price on all units. In Mang’s words: P > MC means “the value to consumers of the last unit of output is greater than the resource cost of producing that unit.” That is allocative inefficiency.

The bigger the markup, the bigger the loss (Slides)

Mang’s Lesson 11.2 lines up the three structures with market power. Fewer close substitutes → steeper demand → bigger markup over MC → bigger deadweight loss. A monopoly has the largest markup, so its deadweight loss is larger than in any market with competition.

How big is the loss in the real economy? (Textbook) Arnold Harberger’s estimates for the U.S. put the deadweight loss from market power below 1% of output; Canadian estimates are similar. True monopolies are rare, and in most industries competition keeps P fairly close to MC. Hubbard also defines market power as “the ability of a firm to charge a price greater than marginal cost”. Every firm that isn’t perfectly competitive has some, so some efficiency is lost in almost every market.
Memory Aid — “A moves, B and C vanish” A = the rectangle of CS the monopoly takes; B + C = the triangles no one gets.
  • Direction rule: monopoly vs competition → P ↑, Q ↓, CS ↓, PS ↑, total surplus ↓.
  • Direction rule: the DWL triangle always points at the efficient point where demand meets MC, and its left edge stands at qM, running from MC up to PM.
  • Trap: rectangle A is not deadweight loss — it’s a transfer from consumers to the firm. Only B + C is lost to society.
Self-test: in Figure 4, which areas did consumers lose, which did the monopoly gain, and which are gone? (A + B · A (minus C) · B + C.)
Section 6

Monopoly Rent and Production Inefficiency

Mang’s Lesson 11.2 lists what goes wrong with a monopoly beyond the deadweight loss:

Monopoly rent

A monopoly typically earns an economic profit, which Mang also calls a monopoly rent. Unlike in perfect or monopolistic competition, it can last in the long run, because barriers stop new firms from entering to compete it away.

Monopoly inefficiency — Mang’s two points

1. The markup: P > MC. The value of the last unit to consumers exceeds its resource cost (allocative inefficiency; the deadweight loss of Section 5).

2. Not at minimum efficient scale: MC ≠ ATC. The profit-maximizing quantity is usually not the minimum of ATC, so the firm doesn’t produce at the lowest possible average cost (productive inefficiency). It is possible for qM to land exactly at the MES (MC1 = ATC1), “but it is not likely.”

Plus: because profits persist and P ≠ ATC, investment capital is not allocated efficiently. Capital would flow into the industry if it could, but the barriers keep it out.

Vocabulary Trap — “MES” on Mang’s slides vs in the textbook As in Unit 7, Mang labels the minimum of the (short-run) ATC curve the minimum efficient scale. Hubbard defines MES on the long-run average cost curve: the lowest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum. For Mang’s questions, “at MES” means “at the bottom of the ATC curve drawn” → MC = ATC.

The other side of the argument — Schumpeter (Textbook) Joseph Schumpeter argued that market power can help the economy. Big profits fund the research behind new products (cars replacing carriages, computers replacing calculators), and that “gale of creative destruction” matters more than slightly higher prices. Critics answer that many breakthroughs came from small firms (Apple in a garage, Google from grad students). Patents are the policy version of this trade-off: a temporary monopoly in exchange for innovation.
Section 7

Competition Policy and Mergers

Because monopolies raise prices and make consumers worse off, governments try to stop them forming. In Canada the main law is the Competition Act, enforced by the Competition Bureau (headed by the Commissioner of Competition). Hubbard notes Canada’s first antitrust law (1889) is the oldest of its kind in the Western world.

  • Collusion = an agreement among firms to charge the same price or otherwise not compete (Unit 10). Price fixing, bid rigging and predatory pricing are offences: up to 14 years in prison, fines up to $25 million, or both.
  • Horizontal merger (Mang: horizontal integration): two competing firms in the same industry merge. This is what competition policy watches most, because it can create market power.
  • Vertical merger (Textbook): firms at different stages of producing a good merge (e.g., a PC maker and a hard-drive maker). Less likely to increase market power.
The Bureau’s rule of thumb: 35% and 65%

The Bureau typically prevents (Mang) / examines more closely (Hubbard) a merger when the new firm would have more than 35% market share, or when the four largest firms would have more than 65% combined market share. Below both thresholds, a merger usually doesn’t concern the Bureau.

Example: the four largest firms hold 30%, 25%, 20% and 10%. If the 25% and 20% firms merge, the new firm holds 45% (> 35%), and the top four already held 85% (> 65%). Expect the Bureau to take a close look, and likely block it.

Two complications (Textbook)

1. What is “the market”? Hershey + Mars would own over 70% of chocolate bars, but a much smaller share of “snacks” or “food”. The Bureau starts with the narrowest market (say, cola) and asks whether a monopolist there could profitably sustain a small but permanent price increase, usually 5%. If yes, that’s the market. If customers would switch away and make the rise unprofitable, the definition is too narrow, so widen it (soft drinks). Markets also have a geographic dimension: if consumers can’t buy from a supplier, it isn’t in their market.

2. Mergers can cut costs. If the merged firm is much more efficient (shared warehouses, a better distribution system), its MC curve shifts down. If MC falls enough, the merged firm’s MR = MC point can deliver a lower price and higher output than the old competitive industry (Hubbard Figure 13.6). That’s why every merging firm claims big cost savings.

Memory Aid — “Horizontal = Head-to-head; Vertical = Value chain” Horizontal mergers join rivals; vertical mergers join supplier and customer.
  • Direction rule: above 35% for one firm or 65% for the top four → the Bureau gets involved (“35 alone, 65 for four”).
  • Trap: the merging firms’ claim of lower costs can be true. A merger that lowers MC enough can lower price, but the Bureau has to weigh that against the market power it creates.
Self-test: Rogers buying Shaw (cable vs cable) — horizontal or vertical? A coffee chain buying a coffee-bean roaster? (Horizontal · Vertical.)

Section 8

Regulating Monopolies

When a monopoly or oligopoly already exists, governments can intervene directly. Mang’s Lesson 11.2 covers three tools. Each has a distinct graph, and the key is knowing which curve each tool moves.

Tool 1 — A licence fee (a fixed cost)

A licensing fee strips away part of the economic profit. It’s paid once, whatever the output, so it’s a fixed cost: it shifts ATC up but not MC. MR and MC still cross at the same q, so q and P don’t change, and profit falls by the fee. Consumers are no worse off, and the government can use the money to reduce taxes in other markets (Unit 4’s point: taxes elsewhere carry deadweight losses).

Worked Example 4 — A $6,000 Licence Fee on Mang’s Monopoly
In Worked Example 2 the government charges a licence fee of $6,000 per day. What happens to q, P, profit and consumer surplus?
New fixed cost: 8,000 + 6,000 = 14,000 → ATC' = 14,000/q + 3q
MC = 6q is unchanged → MR = MC still at q = 70, P = $560

ATC'(70) = 14,000/70 + 210 = $410
π = (560 − 410) × 70 = $10,500   (was $16,500: down exactly $6,000)
CS = $4,900 (unchanged — same P and q)
Government revenue = $6,000

Tool 2 — A price ceiling

Mang: “A price ceiling actually makes MR = P because the firm cannot raise its price (just like in perfect competition!).” Up to the quantity where demand meets the ceiling, the firm can sell every extra unit at Pmax without cutting any other price. So the MR curve becomes flat at Pmax, then drops down to the original MR. With a lower price and higher output, consumer surplus increases and the market is more efficient.

Worked Example 5 — Capping Mang’s Monopoly
Same monopoly (P = 700 − 2q, MC = 6q, ATC = 8,000/q + 3q). The government sets a price ceiling. Find q, profit, CS and DWL for Pmax = $540, $525 and $480.
P_max = $540 (between P_C = 525 and P_M = 560):
  demand at $540: 540 = 700 − 2q → q = 80; MC(80) = $480 < $540
  → MR = $540 > MC all the way to q = 80; past 80 MR drops to 700 − 4(80) = 380 < MC
  → produce q = 80 at P = $540
  π  = 540(80) − (8,000 + 3·80²) = 43,200 − 27,200 = $16,000
  CS = ½ × 80 × (700 − 540) = $6,400   (up from $4,900)
  DWL = ½ × (87.5 − 80) × (540 − 480) = $225   (down from $1,225)

P_max = $525 (= the competitive price, where demand meets MC):
  q = 87.5, P = $525 → DWL = 0. The ceiling replicates perfect competition.

P_max = $480 (BELOW the competitive price):
  firm produces only where MC = 480 → 6q = 480 → q = 80
  quantity demanded at $480: 480 = 700 − 2q → q = 110
  → shortage of 30, and DWL = ½ × (87.5 − 80) × (540 − 480) = $225 again
Don’t Let This Happen to You — a ceiling that’s too low backfires Mang: if the government gets the ceiling “just right” (where demand meets MC), it can replicate the perfectly competitive outcome, but “in practice … this outcome may be difficult to achieve.” Set it below that point and you’re back in Unit 4: the firm produces only where MC = Pmax, quantity demanded is higher, and there’s a shortage and a deadweight loss again. A monopoly ceiling helps only between P = MC (the competitive price) and PM.

Tool 3 — Natural monopoly: a non-profit SOE that sets P = ATC

For a natural monopoly, ATC is still falling where it meets demand, so MC lies below ATC there. That creates a dilemma (Hubbard Figure 13.7):

  • P = MC is efficient, but P < ATC → the firm makes a loss and won’t stay in business in the long run without a subsidy.
  • P = ATC lets the firm break even (zero economic profit). Output is below the efficient level, but much closer to it than the monopoly’s. This is what regulators (public utility boards) usually choose. In Mang’s version the government creates a non-profit-maximizing state-owned enterprise and sets price equal to average total cost.
Worked Example 6 — Pricing a Natural Monopoly
A water utility faces demand P = 100 − q (q in thousands of households). MC = $20 (constant) and fixed cost = $1,200 (thousands), so ATC = 20 + 1,200/q. Compare the three prices.
Unregulated monopoly:  MR = 100 − 2q = 20 → q = 40, P = $60
  ATC(40) = 20 + 30 = $50 → π = (60 − 50) × 40 = $400
  DWL = ½ × (80 − 40) × (60 − 20) = $800

Efficient (P = MC):    100 − q = 20 → q = 80, P = $20
  ATC(80) = 20 + 15 = $35 → π = (20 − 35) × 80 = −$1,200  (a loss equal to FC)

Regulated P = ATC:     100 − q = 20 + 1,200/q
  q² − 80q + 1,200 = 0 → (q − 20)(q − 60) = 0 → q = 60 (the larger root)
  P = 100 − 60 = $40, π = 0
  DWL = ½ × (80 − 60) × (40 − 20) = $200

Take the larger root: the regulator wants the most output that still breaks even. (At q = 20 the price would be $80, worse than the monopoly’s own price.)

Memory Aid — “Fee Fixes nothing, Ceiling Flattens MR, SOE Sets P = ATC”
  • Licence fee = fixed cost → ATC ↑ only → same q, same P, profit ↓ by the fee, CS unchanged.
  • Price ceiling between P = MC and PM → MR flat at Pmax → q ↑, P ↓, CS ↑, DWL ↓. At P = MC: DWL = 0. Below it: shortage.
  • Natural monopoly → P = MC means a loss, so the SOE/regulator sets P = ATC (break even).
  • Trap: a per-unit tax is different from a licence fee. It raises MC, so q falls and P rises.

Section 9

Pricing Strategies and Price Discrimination

Lesson 11.3 (Slides) asks how firms with market power can capture more of the consumer surplus that a single price leaves on the table. (The older printing of Hubbard used for these notes ends Chapter 13 at 13.5. The course schedule assigns 6.x questions, so the current edition adds a section; Mang’s slides are the guide here.)

Price discrimination

Price discrimination is charging different prices to different customers for the same good or service. It is a legal business practice. It only works if all three conditions hold:

  1. The firm has market power (a price taker can’t charge anyone more than the market price).
  2. The firm can identify customers who are willing to pay more than others.
  3. The firm can prevent resale (or low-price buyers would resell to high-price buyers).
Memory Aid — the three conditions are “PIR”: Power, Identify, Resale blocked
  • Direction rule: a student discount checked by student ID satisfies Identify and Resale blocked at once (the ID travels with the ticket).
  • Trap: charging different prices because costs differ (delivery to a farther town) is not price discrimination. Same good, same cost, different prices is.
Self-test: why can’t a wheat farmer price discriminate? (No market power: it’s a price taker.)

Other pricing strategies (Slides)

Two-part tariffs and “junk” fees

A two-part tariff charges a membership or entrance fee plus a fee for each use (Costco membership + prices; amusement-park entry + rides; a gym joining fee + monthly fee). A “junk fee” is a processing fee or administrative charge added on top of the stated price (ticket “service fees”). Both capture a portion of the consumer surplus. The best fees depend on how much consumers’ preferences vary and on their willingness and ability to pay.

Price matching (low-price guarantees)

An LPG is good for reputation and profits. It can enforce collusion in markets with strong price competition: if your rival cuts its price, you match it, so neither gains by cutting (the Unit 10 prisoner’s dilemma loses its bite). You don’t have to monitor rivals, because customers report lower prices to you. And it price discriminates: customers who don’t know about cheaper prices pay more.

Challenges: (1) you need a way to verify the rival’s price, not just take the customer’s word; (2) a lower-cost rival may set a price you can’t match profitably. So firms often match only “regular” prices, not sale prices.

Loss leaders

A loss leader is an item sold below cost and advertised as on sale. The seller takes a guaranteed loss on it, and rivals may be unwilling to match a price that guarantees them a loss. The goal: get customers into your store, where they buy other things whose profits more than cover the loss.

Vocabulary Trap — loss leader vs predatory pricing A loss leader (legal) prices one item below cost to bring shoppers in. Predatory pricing (an offence under the Competition Act — see Section 7) prices below cost to drive a rival out of the market, then raises prices once it’s gone. The difference is the target: customers’ shopping baskets vs a competitor’s survival.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Monopoly from Equations

A monopoly has demand P = 360 − 2q and total cost TC = 3,000 + q² (so MC = 2q and ATC = 3,000/q + q). (a) Find MR. (b) Find the profit-maximizing q and P, and the profit. (c) Find the markup. (d) Find the competitive (P = MC) outcome and the deadweight loss. (e) Is the monopoly at minimum efficient scale?

Show answer
(a) MR = 360 − 4q (double the slope).

(b) 360 − 4q = 2q → q = 60; P = 360 − 120 = $240. ATC(60) = 50 + 60 = $110 → π = (240 − 110) × 60 = $7,800. (Check: TR 14,400 − TC 6,600 = 7,800.)

(c) MC(60) = $120 → markup = 240 − 120 = $120.

(d) 360 − 2q = 2q → q = 90, P = $180. DWL = ½ × (90 − 60) × (240 − 120) = $1,800.

(e) ATC is lowest where ATC = MC: 3,000/q + q = 2q → q = √3,000 ≈ 54.8 (ATC ≈ $109.54). The monopoly produces 60, so MC ($120) ≠ ATC ($110): not at MES. It isn’t productively efficient (and P > MC means it isn’t allocatively efficient either).
Problem 2 — From a Table

A monopoly’s demand and total cost:
Q: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7
P ($): 50, 46, 42, 38, 34, 30, 26, 22
TC ($): 30, 40, 48, 58, 70, 86, 106, 130
(a) Compute TR, MR and MC. (b) Find the profit-maximizing Q, P and profit. (c) The government adds a $20 licence fee. What happens? Should the firm raise its price to cover it?

Show answer
(a) TR = 0, 46, 84, 114, 136, 150, 156, 154. MR = 46, 38, 30, 22, 14, 6, −2. MC = 10, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24.

(b) MR ≥ MC through Q = 4 (22 > 12); at Q = 5, MR 14 < MC 16 → stop. Q = 4, P = $34, π = 136 − 70 = $66. (Q = 3: $56; Q = 5: $64.)

(c) The fee is fixed: TC rises by $20 at every Q, so MC and MR don’t change. Still Q = 4, P = $34; profit falls to $46. No price rise: $34 was already the profit-maximizing price. Charging more would only lower profit (at Q = 3, P = $38: 114 − 78 = $36).
Problem 3 — Price Ceilings on a Monopoly

Use the monopoly from Problem 1 (P = 360 − 2q, MC = 2q, FC = 3,000; unregulated q = 60, P = $240). Find output, profit and deadweight loss if the government sets a price ceiling of (a) $200, (b) $180, (c) $150.

Show answer
(a) $200: demand at $200 → q = 80, and MC(80) = $160 < $200, so MR = $200 > MC up to 80 → q = 80. π = 200(80) − (3,000 + 6,400) = $6,600. DWL = ½ × (90 − 80) × (200 − 160) = $200 (down from $1,800). CS = ½ × 80 × 160 = $6,400 (up from $3,600).

(b) $180 = the competitive price → q = 90, DWL = 0; π = 16,200 − 11,100 = $5,100.

(c) $150 is below the competitive price: the firm produces where MC = 150 → q = 75, but 105 are demanded → shortage of 30. π = 11,250 − 8,625 = $2,625. DWL = ½ × (90 − 75) × (210 − 150) = $450. Too low a ceiling brings the deadweight loss back.
Problem 4 — Regulating a Natural Monopoly

A gas distributor faces P = 120 − q, has constant MC = $30 and fixed cost $1,800 (so ATC = 30 + 1,800/q). (a) Why is it a natural monopoly? (b) Find the unregulated q, P and profit. (c) What happens at P = MC? (d) Find the regulated price if the regulator sets P = ATC. (e) Compare the deadweight loss in (b) and (d).

Show answer
(a) ATC = 30 + 1,800/q falls at every q (MC is below ATC everywhere), so ATC is still falling where it meets demand: one firm can serve the market most cheaply.

(b) MR = 120 − 2q = 30 → q = 45, P = $75; TC = 1,800 + 1,350 = 3,150 → π = 3,375 − 3,150 = $225.

(c) 120 − q = 30 → q = 90, P = $30 = MC < ATC ($50) → loss = $1,800 (= FC). It can’t survive without a subsidy.

(d) 120 − q = 30 + 1,800/q → q² − 90q + 1,800 = 0 → (q − 30)(q − 60) = 0 → q = 60, P = $60, π = 0.

(e) Unregulated DWL = ½ × (90 − 45) × (75 − 30) = $1,012.50. At P = ATC: ½ × (90 − 60) × (60 − 30) = $450.
Problem 5 — Cinema Pricing

A town’s only cinema has MC = $6 per ticket. Adult demand: P = 30 − QA. Student demand: P = 14 − QS (hundreds of tickets per week).
(a) Which three conditions must hold for the cinema to charge students less? How does it meet them?
(b) With separate prices, find each group’s quantity, price and profit.
(c) With one price for everyone, what price maximizes profit? Show that serving both groups at one price is not better.
(d) Compare output and profit in (b) and (c). Which degree of price discrimination is (b)?
(e) If the cinema could perfectly price discriminate among adults, what would adult output, profit and consumer surplus be?

Show answer
(a) Market power (the only cinema in town), identify high- and low-WTP buyers (student ID), prevent resale (ID checked at the door, so a student ticket can’t be handed to an adult).

(b) Adults: 30 − 2Q = 6 → QA = 12, PA = $18, profit (18 − 6) × 12 = $144. Students: 14 − 2Q = 6 → QS = 4, PS = $10, profit (10 − 6) × 4 = $16. Total 16 (hundred) tickets, $160.

(c) Adults-only price: $18, Q = 12, profit $144 (students won’t pay $18 > $14). To include students the price must be ≤ $14: combined demand Q = 44 − 2P → P = 22 − Q/2 → MR = 22 − Q = 6 → Q = 16, P = $14 (the edge of the range): profit = (14 − 6) × 16 = $128 < $144. Best single price: $18.

(d) Discrimination raises output from 12 to 16 and profit from $144 to $160, as Mang’s slide says. Student pricing is 3rd-degree (multi-market) discrimination.

(e) Sell until demand = MC → 30 − Q = 6 → QA = 24; profit = ½ × 24 × (30 − 6) = $288; adult CS = 0 (and no deadweight loss).
